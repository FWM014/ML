Decision Trees

A decision tree is a flowchart the computer writes for itself: a stack of yes/no questions that carves the data into boxes. It is the most readable model you will ever ship, the building block of the strongest tabular models in Chapter 10, and the fastest way to understand what "overfitting" physically looks like.

Intermediate ⏱ 65 min read · Prereqs: Ch 3, 6, 7

By the end of this chapter you will be able to

  • Read a tree diagram and translate it into the rectangles it draws in feature space.
  • Compute Gini impurity, entropy, and information gain by hand for a candidate split — and say which split a tree would pick.

  • Fit classification and regression trees in scikit-learn, export their rules as text, and read feature importances with the right suspicion.
  • Know exactly where a single tree fails (instability, extrapolation) and why that motivates forests and boosting.
  • Decide when a single readable tree is the right deliverable for a business.

1. Twenty questions

You have played the game. "Is it alive?" — "Is it bigger than a cat?" — "Does it live in water?" Each question splits the set of possible answers in two, and a good player asks the question that cuts the remaining possibilities the most. Twenty well-chosen questions distinguish about a million things.

A decision tree is that game, played by an algorithm against a table of data. Each question is a test on one feature: is support_calls > 1.5? is plan == basic? is monthly_spend ≤ 42.7? The rows that answer "yes" go one way, "no" go the other, and the process repeats on each side until the rows in a group mostly share the same label. To classify a new customer, you start at the top and follow the answers down to a leaf, which holds the prediction.

Explain it like I'm 9

Imagine sorting a big pile of mixed Lego bricks into "goes in the castle box" and "goes in the spaceship box." You don't look at every brick individually; you ask one question at a time about the whole pile: "Is it grey? Grey bricks are almost all spaceship." Then, in the not-grey pile: "Is it a flat tile? Flat tiles are almost all castle." Each question makes the sub-piles purer. When a sub-pile is nearly all one kind, you stop and label the box. That stack of questions is a decision tree.

Three things make trees special. They are non-linear without any feature engineering: a tree can represent "risk is high for the very young and the very old" with two splits, which a linear model cannot do without a hand-crafted feature. They are indifferent to scale: only the ordering of values matters, so log transforms and StandardScaler change nothing (Chapter 8). And they are readable: a depth-3 tree is a paragraph of rules that a compliance officer can audit line by line.

The example threaded through this chapter is the churn table from Chapter 8: tenure, monthly spend, support calls, plan, region. A tree built on it asks questions a customer-success manager would recognize — and, as we will see, will happily ask a hundred more that nobody would.

2. Anatomy: nodes, leaves, and rectangles

The vocabulary is small. The root is the first question, applied to all training rows. Internal nodes are subsequent questions. Leaves (terminal nodes) hold no question, only a prediction: the majority class (classification) or the mean target (regression) of the training rows that landed there. Depth is the number of questions on the longest path from root to leaf. A node's samples is how many training rows reach it, and its value is their class counts.

Every question in a tree tests one feature against one threshold, so each split draws a line parallel to an axis. Two features, three splits: the plane is chopped into four rectangles, each labeled with a class. This geometric view is the key to everything that follows — depth is the number of times you are allowed to slice, and overfitting is slicing until every rectangle contains one point.

Key idea

A tree is a piecewise-constant function: inside each rectangle the prediction is one constant. That single fact explains its strengths (any shape can be approximated by enough boxes; no scaling needed) and its weaknesses (diagonal boundaries need staircases of boxes; outside the training range the prediction is flat).

3. How a split is chosen: impurity and information gain

The whole algorithm rests on one question: given a node full of rows, which feature and which threshold should we split on? The answer is "the split that makes the children as pure as possible," so we need a number that says how mixed a group is.

Explain it like I'm 9

You have a bag of marbles. If they are all red, the bag is pure — you know exactly what you'll pull out. If it is half red and half blue, the bag is as mixed as it can be. Impurity is a score for how mixed the bag is: 0 for all-one-color, biggest for an even mix. A good question splits one mixed bag into two purer bags.

The math (optional): Gini impurity
Gini(node) = 1 − ∑k pk2

pk is the fraction of rows in the node that belong to class k, and the sum runs over the classes. Interpretation: pick two rows at random from the node (with replacement); Gini is the probability they have different labels. Pure node → 0. Two classes 50/50 → 1 − (0.25 + 0.25) = 0.5, the maximum for two classes. This is scikit-learn's default criterion.

The math (optional): entropy and information gain
H(node) = − ∑k pk log2 pk      IG(split) = H(parent) − [ (nL/n)·H(left) + (nR/n)·H(right) ]

H is Shannon entropy in bits; with two classes it runs from 0 (pure) to 1 (50/50). nL and nR are the number of rows sent left and right, n = nL + nR. Information gain is how much entropy the split removes, weighting each child by its share of rows. The same weighted-average construction with Gini in place of H gives the Gini decrease. The tree picks the split with the largest gain (or decrease). In practice Gini and entropy choose the same split about 98% of the time; Gini is marginally faster because it has no logarithm.

Both curves are 0 at p = 0 and p = 1 (pure) and peak at p = 0.5 (maximally mixed). Gini tops out at 0.5, entropy at 1 bit; the shapes are nearly the same, which is why the two criteria almost always pick the same split.

A fully worked split

Ten customers, one feature (support calls), and the label. Five stayed, five churned, so the parent node has Gini = 1 − (0.5² + 0.5²) = 0.5 and entropy = 1 bit. A tree considers every threshold between consecutive distinct values of the feature — here 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, 4.5, 5.5 — and evaluates each one.

# The worked split by hand: 10 customers, which support-call threshold separates churners best?
import numpy as np

def gini(counts):
    p = np.asarray(counts) / np.sum(counts)
    return 1 - np.sum(p ** 2)

def entropy(counts):
    p = np.asarray(counts) / np.sum(counts)
    p = p[p > 0]
    return -np.sum(p * np.log2(p))

calls   = np.array([0, 0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6])
churned = np.array([0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0, 1])   # 5 stayed, 5 churned

parent = [5, 5]
print(f"parent     : Gini={gini(parent):.3f}  entropy={entropy(parent):.3f}")
for thr in [0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, 4.5, 5.5]:
    left, right = churned[calls <= thr], churned[calls > thr]
    cl = [int((left == 0).sum()), int((left == 1).sum())]
    cr = [int((right == 0).sum()), int((right == 1).sum())]
    wl, wr = len(left) / 10, len(right) / 10
    g  = wl * gini(cl) + wr * gini(cr)
    ig = entropy(parent) - (wl * entropy(cl) + wr * entropy(cr))
    print(f"calls<={thr}: left(stay,churn)={cl} right={cr}  weighted Gini={g:.3f}  info gain={ig:.3f}")
# parent     : Gini=0.500  entropy=1.000
# calls<=0.5: left(stay,churn)=[2, 0] right=[4, 4]  weighted Gini=0.400  info gain=0.200
# calls<=1.5: left(stay,churn)=[4, 0] right=[2, 4]  weighted Gini=0.267  info gain=0.449
# calls<=2.5: left(stay,churn)=[5, 1] right=[1, 3]  weighted Gini=0.317  info gain=0.285
# calls<=3.5: left(stay,churn)=[5, 2] right=[1, 2]  weighted Gini=0.419  info gain=0.120
# calls<=4.5: left(stay,churn)=[5, 3] right=[1, 1]  weighted Gini=0.475  info gain=0.036
# calls<=5.5: left(stay,churn)=[6, 3] right=[0, 1]  weighted Gini=0.400  info gain=0.174

Notice that the split is scored by weighted impurity. A split that peels off one pure row (≤ 5.5 above) looks tempting — one child is perfectly pure — but it barely changes the other child, so its weighted score is poor. The weighting is what stops trees from wasting splits on single outliers early on.

Common mistake

Believing a tree "finds the best tree." It does not. At each node it picks the split that is best right now, without looking ahead. A greedy choice at the root can lock the tree into a worse overall structure — the XOR pattern is the classic case, where no single split helps but two together separate perfectly. Random forests and boosting (Chapter 10) soften this by building many trees; a single tree simply lives with it.

4. Regression trees

Swap the label for a number and almost nothing changes. A leaf predicts the mean of the training targets that reached it, and impurity becomes the variance (equivalently, the mean squared error around the leaf mean). A split is chosen to make the weighted variance of the children as small as possible: rows with similar target values are grouped together.

The math (optional)
MSE(node) = (1/n) ∑i∈node (yi − ȳnode)2     Δ = MSE(parent) − [ (nL/n)·MSE(left) + (nR/n)·MSE(right) ]

ȳnode is the mean target of rows in the node — also the leaf's prediction, because the mean is the constant that minimizes squared error. Δ is the variance reduction; the split with the largest Δ wins. Use criterion="absolute_error" to predict the median instead (robust to outliers in y, but slower), or "poisson" for count targets.

# Regression tree on a sine wave: a step function that cannot extrapolate
import numpy as np
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 6, 120)).reshape(-1, 1)
y = np.sin(X.ravel()) + rng.normal(0, 0.2, 120)

for leaf in [1, 5, 15]:
    t = DecisionTreeRegressor(min_samples_leaf=leaf, random_state=0).fit(X, y)
    X_new = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 6, 400)).reshape(-1, 1)
    rmse = mean_squared_error(np.sin(X_new.ravel()), t.predict(X_new)) ** 0.5
    print(f"min_samples_leaf={leaf:2d}: leaves={t.get_n_leaves():3d}  RMSE vs true curve={rmse:.3f}")

t = DecisionTreeRegressor(min_samples_leaf=5, random_state=0).fit(X, y)
print("prediction at x=5.9:", t.predict([[5.9]]).round(2), " at x=9.0:", t.predict([[9.0]]).round(2), " at x=50:", t.predict([[50.0]]).round(2))
print("true sin(9.0) =", np.sin(9.0).round(2))
# min_samples_leaf= 1: leaves=120  RMSE vs true curve=0.216
# min_samples_leaf= 5: leaves= 18  RMSE vs true curve=0.123
# min_samples_leaf=15: leaves=  6  RMSE vs true curve=0.153
# prediction at x=5.9: [-0.46]  at x=9.0: [-0.46]  at x=50: [-0.46]
# true sin(9.0) = 0.41
Explain it like I'm 9

Guess how tall a kid is without seeing them. If I tell you nothing, your best guess is the average of the whole school. If I tell you "they're in the third grade," you guess the third-grade average and you're much closer. A regression tree keeps asking questions that sort kids into groups whose heights are as similar as possible, and then guesses each group's average. The "steps" in Figure 9.4 are those group averages.

Two lessons in five lines of output. With min_samples_leaf=1 the tree grows 120 leaves — one per training point — and its error against the true curve is the worst of the three, because every leaf reproduces one point's noise. With 5 points per leaf the noise averages out. And the last two lines show the flat-extrapolation problem we return to in Section 8: past the largest training x, the prediction is frozen at the last leaf's mean, forever.

5. The greedy recursive algorithm

You now have every piece. The algorithm that builds a tree (known as CART, Classification And Regression Trees, which is what scikit-learn implements) is:

  1. Start with all training rows at the root. Compute the node's impurity (Gini/entropy for classes, MSE for numbers).
  2. Scan every feature and every threshold. For a numeric feature, sort its values and consider the midpoint between each pair of consecutive distinct values. For each candidate, split the rows, compute the weighted child impurity, and remember the best (feature, threshold).
  3. Apply the best split — unless a stopping rule fires: the node is pure, it has fewer than min_samples_split rows, a child would have fewer than min_samples_leaf rows, the depth reached max_depth, or the best split's impurity decrease is below min_impurity_decrease. Then it becomes a leaf.
  4. Recurse into the left child and the right child, each with its own subset of rows, and repeat from step 2.
  5. Label the leaves with the majority class (and class fractions) or the mean target.

Cost: for n rows and d features, one node costs roughly O(d · n log n) for the sort-and-scan; a whole tree is typically O(d · n log² n). Trees train fast, and prediction is a walk of at most max_depth comparisons — microseconds per row, which is why a tree-based model can sit in a latency-critical scoring path.

from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier, export_text

iris = load_iris()
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(iris.data, iris.target, test_size=0.3, random_state=0, stratify=iris.target)
tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=2, random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print("train acc = %.3f   test acc = %.3f" % (tree.score(X_tr, y_tr), tree.score(X_te, y_te)))
print("depth =", tree.get_depth(), " leaves =", tree.get_n_leaves())
print(export_text(tree, feature_names=list(iris.feature_names), class_names=list(iris.target_names)))
# train acc = 0.962   test acc = 0.956
# depth = 2  leaves = 3
# |--- petal width (cm) <= 0.80
# |   |--- class: setosa
# |--- petal width (cm) >  0.80
# |   |--- petal width (cm) <= 1.75
# |   |   |--- class: versicolor
# |   |--- petal width (cm) >  1.75
# |   |   |--- class: virginica

Two questions, 96% accuracy, and a rule set you can print on a card: petal width under 0.8 cm → setosa; under 1.75 → versicolor; otherwise virginica. export_text is the tool for handing rules to humans; plot_tree draws the same thing as a diagram, and tree_.feature, tree_.threshold, tree_.value expose the raw arrays if you need to translate a tree into SQL or a rules engine.

At work

Trees are one of the few models where the shape of the output matters as much as its accuracy. Before you tune anything, print export_text for a depth-3 tree and read it with the domain owner. If the top split is a feature that "shouldn't" matter, you have just found a leakage or a data-quality bug in five minutes — far cheaper than discovering it after deployment.

6. Why trees overfit, and the controls

Explain it like I'm 9

Two ways to pass a spelling test: learn the rules ("i before e except after c") or memorize every single word on the practice sheet. Memorizing gets you 100% on the practice sheet and falls apart on new words. A tree that keeps splitting until every training row has its own box has memorized the practice sheet. Limiting depth, or demanding that each box hold many rows, forces it to learn rules instead.

Left alone, the recursion only stops when every leaf is pure or contains identical rows. On real data with noise that means growing until each leaf holds one or two rows — a tree with hundreds of leaves, 100% training accuracy, and rectangles so thin they exist only to fence in individual outliers. Such a tree has memorized, not learned; Chapter 7 called this high variance. The picture makes it obvious:

Watch training accuracy climb to 100% as depth increases, and watch what the rectangles have to do to get there. Ask yourself at each depth: would I trust this boundary on tomorrow's data?

The controls

scikit-learn gives you several knobs. They overlap, and you rarely need more than two of them.

# Depth sweep: where does a single tree stop improving?
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_wine
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, StratifiedKFold
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

X, y = load_wine(return_X_y=True)
cv = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
for d in [1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, None]:
    t = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=d, random_state=0)
    s = cross_val_score(t, X, y, cv=cv)
    t.fit(X, y)
    print(f"max_depth={str(d):4s}  train={t.score(X, y):.3f}  cv={s.mean():.3f} ± {s.std():.3f}  leaves={t.get_n_leaves()}")
# max_depth=1     train=0.697  cv=0.618 ± 0.038  leaves=2
# max_depth=2     train=0.921  cv=0.843 ± 0.036  leaves=4
# max_depth=3     train=0.978  cv=0.938 ± 0.041  leaves=8
# max_depth=4     train=0.989  cv=0.927 ± 0.051  leaves=11
# max_depth=6     train=1.000  cv=0.927 ± 0.051  leaves=12
# max_depth=8     train=1.000  cv=0.927 ± 0.051  leaves=12
# max_depth=None  train=1.000  cv=0.927 ± 0.051  leaves=12

The classic picture from Chapter 7: training accuracy climbs monotonically to 100%; cross-validated accuracy peaks (here at depth 3) and then drifts down as the extra leaves fit noise. Note that on this small, clean dataset the full tree only needs 12 leaves and the damage is mild — on a noisy business table with 100,000 rows the unlimited tree grows thousands of leaves and the gap between train and CV becomes a canyon.

Cost-complexity pruning

Pre-setting max_depth is blunt: it stops every branch at the same depth even though some regions of the data deserve more detail than others. Cost-complexity pruning (also called weakest-link pruning) grows the full tree and then removes branches whose contribution is not worth their complexity:

The math (optional)
Rα(T) = R(T) + α · |leaves(T)|

R(T) is the tree's total training impurity (misclassification or squared error, summed over leaves and weighted by samples); |leaves(T)| is the number of leaves; α ≥ 0 is the price per leaf. For each α there is a unique smallest subtree minimizing Rα. As α increases from 0, branches are collapsed one at a time in order of the least impurity gain per leaf they provide — that ordered sequence is the pruning path. You choose α by cross-validation.

# Cost-complexity pruning: grow fully, then find the alpha that generalizes best
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, cross_val_score
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=800, n_features=20, n_informative=6, n_redundant=4,
                           flip_y=0.08, random_state=3)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=1, stratify=y)
full = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
path = full.cost_complexity_pruning_path(X_tr, y_tr)
alphas = path.ccp_alphas[:-1]           # the last alpha collapses the tree to its root
print(f"{len(alphas)} candidate alphas; full tree has {full.get_n_leaves()} leaves, train acc {full.score(X_tr, y_tr):.2f}")

best = None
for a in alphas:
    cv = cross_val_score(DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0, ccp_alpha=a), X_tr, y_tr, cv=5).mean()
    if best is None or cv > best[1]:
        best = (a, cv)
pruned = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0, ccp_alpha=best[0]).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(f"best alpha = {best[0]:.4f}  (cv acc {best[1]:.3f})")
print(f"full  : leaves={full.get_n_leaves():3d}  test acc={full.score(X_te, y_te):.3f}")
print(f"pruned: leaves={pruned.get_n_leaves():3d}  test acc={pruned.score(X_te, y_te):.3f}")
# 36 candidate alphas; full tree has 62 leaves, train acc 1.00
# best alpha = 0.0128  (cv acc 0.814)
# full  : leaves= 62  test acc=0.779
# pruned: leaves= 10  test acc=0.846

From 62 leaves to 10, and test accuracy goes up by seven points. That is the typical size of the win on noisy data, and 10 leaves is a tree a human can still read. In practice I tune ccp_alpha (or min_samples_leaf) with GridSearchCV exactly as Chapter 8 tuned a pipeline; the loop above just makes the mechanism visible.

Key idea

Every control on a tree is a way of saying "don't believe a pattern unless enough rows support it." min_samples_leaf says it directly; max_depth says it by limiting how specific a rule can get; ccp_alpha says it by charging a price per leaf. Tune one of them by cross-validation and leave the rest at their defaults.

7. Feature importance and its biases

After fitting, tree.feature_importances_ gives one number per feature that sums to 1. It is the total impurity decrease attributable to that feature — every split on the feature contributes its (sample-weighted) Gini or MSE reduction — normalized across features. Features used near the root, on many rows, score high; features never used score 0. It is free, it is intuitive, and it is biased in two specific ways you must know about.

  1. High-cardinality bias. A feature with many distinct values offers many candidate thresholds, so purely by chance it can find a split that reduces impurity a little. A continuous noise column will beat a genuinely informative binary flag in the impurity ranking, because the flag offers exactly one threshold.
  2. Correlated-feature masking. If two features carry the same signal, the tree uses whichever wins the first tie-break, and the other gets near-zero importance. The ranking then says "feature B is irrelevant" when it is really "feature B is redundant given A" — a very different statement for the person deciding which data to keep collecting.
# Impurity-based importance is biased toward high-cardinality features
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance

data = load_breast_cancer()
weak = ["mean texture", "mean smoothness", "mean symmetry", "mean fractal dimension"]
X = pd.DataFrame(data.data, columns=data.feature_names)[weak]
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X["random_id"] = rng.random(len(X))            # pure noise, 569 distinct values
X["random_coin"] = rng.integers(0, 2, len(X))  # pure noise, only 2 distinct values
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, data.target, random_state=0, stratify=data.target)
tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
perm = permutation_importance(tree, X_te, y_te, n_repeats=30, random_state=0)
out = pd.DataFrame({"impurity": tree.feature_importances_.round(3),
                    "permutation": perm.importances_mean.round(3)}, index=X.columns)
print(out.sort_values("impurity", ascending=False))
#                         impurity  permutation
# mean texture               0.347        0.127
# mean smoothness            0.239        0.113
# mean fractal dimension     0.202        0.035
# mean symmetry              0.124        0.002
# random_id                  0.088        0.014
# random_coin                0.000        0.000
Key idea

Importance answers "which columns did this model lean on," never "what causes the outcome." Impurity importance is computed on training data during fitting and inherits the tree's biases; permutation importance is computed on held-out data after fitting and measures what the model actually needs to predict well. Report the second, and always alongside the caveat about correlated features.

Permutation importance is the fix: shuffle one feature's column in the test set, re-score the model, and record how much the score drops. A feature the model truly relies on hurts when scrambled; a noise feature does not. It is model-agnostic, measured on unseen data, and honest about high-cardinality columns. Its own weakness — correlated features can substitute for each other, so both look unimportant — is handled by SHAP values and grouped permutation in Chapter 20.

Common mistake

Reporting feature_importances_ to a business audience as "what drives churn." It is a description of how this tree used the columns, not of causation, and it is biased toward continuous columns with many values (customer id, timestamps, amounts). Use permutation importance on a held-out set for the ranking, and never let a raw id column into X in the first place.

8. Strengths, weaknesses, and practicalities

✅ What trees do well

  • No scaling, no transforms. Splits depend only on order; log, z-score and min-max leave the tree unchanged.
  • Mixed types. Numbers, ordered codes, and (with native support or one-hot) categories, in one model.
  • Non-linear and interacting effects for free. "High risk if young AND basic plan" is two splits.
  • Interpretable. A shallow tree is a readable rule set; probabilities come from leaf counts.
  • Fast to train and near-instant to predict; robust to outliers in the features (an outlier just lands in the end box).
  • Missing values are handled natively (sklearn ≥ 1.3): each split learns a default direction for NaN.

❌ Where a single tree fails

  • Unstable. Remove 5% of the rows and the root split can change, taking the whole structure with it (Figure 9.9).
  • Overfits by default. Must be constrained or pruned; the defaults are not safe.
  • Cannot extrapolate. Predictions are flat outside the training range (Figure 9.8) — dangerous for trends, prices, growth.
  • Axis-aligned. A diagonal boundary needs a staircase of many splits; a linear model gets it with one line.
  • Coarse probabilities. A 10-leaf tree emits at most 10 distinct probability values; calibrate (Chapter 13) before using them as risk scores.
  • Greedy. Locally-best splits are not globally best (the XOR problem).

Missing values and categorical features

Since scikit-learn 1.3, DecisionTreeClassifier and DecisionTreeRegressor accept NaN in numeric features (with the default splitter="best"). At each split the algorithm tries sending the missing rows left and right and keeps whichever is better — so "missingness" becomes something the tree can use, exactly the MNAR argument from Chapter 8. No imputer needed:

# Since scikit-learn 1.3, trees accept NaN: each split learns which side missing values go to
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, StratifiedKFold
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X_miss = np.where(rng.random(X.shape) < 0.2, np.nan, X)     # 20% of cells blanked
cv = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=4, random_state=0)
print("complete data :", cross_val_score(tree, X, y, cv=cv).mean().round(3))
print("20% NaN, no imputation:", cross_val_score(tree, X_miss, y, cv=cv).mean().round(3))
tree.fit(X_miss, y)
print("NaN rows predicted fine:", tree.predict(X_miss[:3]), " (true:", y[:3], ")")
# complete data : 0.926
# 20% NaN, no imputation: 0.903
# NaN rows predicted fine: [0 0 0]  (true: [0 0 0] )

Categorical features are the one place sklearn's trees are still behind. They require numbers, so you either one-hot encode (fine for a handful of levels; each level becomes its own yes/no split, which is exactly what a tree wants) or ordinal-encode (compact, but the tree can only cut the levels in the encoded order — with 20 regions coded 0–19 it needs several splits to isolate regions {3, 11, 17}). HistGradientBoostingClassifier(categorical_features=…), LightGBM and CatBoost implement true categorical splits that can send any subset of levels left; when categories matter, those are the tools (Chapter 10).

Instability: why one tree is never the final model

This instability is not a bug to fix with a bigger dataset; it is a structural property of greedy, hierarchical splitting. A near-tie at the root — and with many correlated features there is always a near-tie — is resolved by whichever rows happen to be present. The lower levels then adapt to a different top split, and the two trees can look unrelated while scoring the same. Chapter 10 turns the weakness into a strength: fit hundreds of trees on bootstrap samples and average them (random forests), or fit trees sequentially to each other's errors (gradient boosting). Those ensembles are the strongest general-purpose models for tabular data, and everything you learned here about splits, depth, leaves and importance carries over unchanged.

9. At work: when one tree is the right answer

Given that a forest almost always scores better, why would you ever deploy a single tree? Because accuracy is not the only deliverable. A single shallow tree is the right answer when:

The rules must be read by people

Triage rules for a support queue, eligibility rules for a promotion, a fraud-review checklist, a clinical screening protocol. A depth-3 tree is eight rules a manager can approve, print, and apply without software. Nobody can approve a 500-tree forest.

The model must live outside Python

A tree exports to SQL CASE WHEN, to a rules engine, to a spreadsheet, to a paper form. If the scoring environment is a legacy system or a regulated decision document, a tree ships where a forest cannot.

You are exploring, not deploying

A quick tree on a new dataset shows you the strongest interactions, the natural thresholds ("spend ≤ 42.7" often reveals a pricing tier), and suspicious features, in seconds. Use it as a flashlight before building the real model.

Segments, not scores, are the goal

Marketing wants "five customer segments with different churn rates and a description of each." A tree with max_leaf_nodes=5 is precisely that: five boxes, each defined by two or three plain conditions, each with a measured churn rate.

At work

When you do deploy a single tree as a rule set, set min_samples_leaf to at least 1–2% of the rows so that every rule is backed by real volume, cross-validate the depth, and — this is the step people skip — re-fit on two or three bootstrap samples and check that the top two splits stay the same. If they do not, the rules are an artefact of this sample, and you should either enlarge the leaves or hand the business a forest with SHAP explanations (Chapter 20) instead of a flowchart that will change next quarter.

At work: the tree as a debugging tool

Fit DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=3) with the model's errors as the target (1 if your production model got the row wrong) and the original features as inputs. The resulting tree tells you, in three rules, where your model fails — "customers with tenure ≤ 2 and region = west" — which is exactly the segment to investigate, collect more data on, or hand-rule around. This is the cheapest error analysis in existence.

Quiz

Check your understanding

1. A node holds 8 rows of class A and 2 of class B. Its Gini impurity is…

Gini = 1 − (0.8² + 0.2²) = 1 − (0.64 + 0.04) = 0.32. It is the chance that two random draws from the node disagree.

2. You apply np.log1p to the monthly_spend column and refit the same DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0). What happens to the tree?

Splits are chosen by ordering; a monotonic transform preserves order, so every candidate split partitions the rows identically and the same one wins. The threshold is now printed on the log scale.

3. A colleague's tree has 100% training accuracy and 71% cross-validated accuracy. Which single change is most likely to help?

The gap is variance: the tree memorized. Requiring more rows per leaf, or pruning, removes the slivers that fit noise. Criterion choice and scaling do not address it; more depth makes it worse.

4. A regression tree trained on sales for months 1–24 is asked to predict month 36. The prediction will be…

Beyond the largest training value every row falls into the same final rectangle, whose prediction is constant. Trees cannot extrapolate; model trends with a linear component or differences (Chapter 14).

5. In a fitted tree, customer_id (a unique integer per row) has the highest feature_importances_. The right conclusion is…

A unique id offers a threshold between every pair of rows, so the tree can isolate individual rows with it — pure memorization, rewarded by impurity importance. Drop it; use permutation importance for rankings.

6. Legal requires that a loan-screening rule be written on one page and applied by staff without software. The best choice is…

The deliverable is a readable, hand-applicable rule set; only a shallow single tree satisfies it. Large leaves keep the rules stable and supported by volume; you accept some accuracy loss for auditability.

Exercise 9.1 — Score the second split by hand

After the best split in Figure 9.3, the right child holds six customers with calls ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5, 6} and labels [stay, churn, churn, churn, stay, churn] (the two customers with 2 calls are one stay, one churn). Compute the weighted Gini for the candidate thresholds 2.5, 3.5, 4.5 and 5.5 within this child. Which does the tree pick, and what does the resulting leaf structure look like?

Show answer

Child counts: [2 stay, 4 churn], Gini 0.444. Threshold 2.5: left {2,2} = [1,1] Gini 0.5; right {3,4,5,6} = [1,3] Gini 0.375 → weighted (2/6)·0.5 + (4/6)·0.375 = 0.417. Threshold 3.5: left [1,2] Gini 0.444; right [1,2] Gini 0.444 → 0.444 (no gain). Threshold 4.5: left [1,3] Gini 0.375; right [1,1] Gini 0.5 → 0.417. Threshold 5.5: left [2,3] Gini 0.48; right [0,1] Gini 0 → (5/6)·0.48 = 0.400. The tree picks 5.5 (weighted 0.400, the smallest), peeling off the 6-call churner into a pure leaf. With min_samples_leaf=2 that split would be forbidden and it would fall back to 2.5 or 4.5 (tied at 0.417; sklearn takes the first feature/threshold found). This is the kind of low-support split pruning removes.

How many distinct root features appear? Repeat with min_samples_leaf=30. Show answer

Typically 3–5 distinct root features appear across the 20 trees (e.g. "worst radius", "worst perimeter", "mean concave points", "worst area" — all highly correlated, so they trade places). The forest's per-row probability standard deviation across bootstraps is several times smaller than the single tree's: averaging cancels the structural noise, which is the whole argument of Chapter 10.

Chapter summary

  • A decision tree is a stack of one-feature threshold questions; each leaf predicts the majority class or the mean target of its training rows.
  • Geometrically a tree is an axis-aligned partition: leaves are rectangles, the prediction is constant inside each.
  • Splits are chosen greedily to minimize weighted child impurity: Gini 1 − ∑p², entropy −∑p log₂p, or MSE for regression; information gain is the parent's impurity minus the weighted children's.
  • Growing to purity memorizes noise. Control with max_depth, min_samples_leaf, max_leaf_nodes, or prune with ccp_alpha chosen by cross-validation.
  • Trees need no scaling, handle mixed types and (since sklearn 1.3) NaN natively, train fast, and export as readable rules.
  • Impurity importance is biased toward high-cardinality and hides correlated features; use permutation importance on held-out data.
  • Single trees are unstable and cannot extrapolate; ensembles (Chapter 10) fix the first, a linear component fixes the second.
  • Deploy a single shallow tree when rules must be read, audited, or applied by hand; otherwise use it as a flashlight and ship an ensemble.
