Clustering & Dimensionality Reduction

Most data at work has no labels. This chapter teaches you to find structure anyway: group customers, products or machines into clusters you can defend in a meeting, squeeze hundreds of columns into a few, and draw a 2-D map of a high-dimensional table without lying to yourself about what it shows.

Intermediate ⏱ 75 min read · Prereqs: Ch 3, 8

By the end of this chapter you will be able to

  • Run k-means correctly (scaled, k-means++, several restarts) and pick k with the elbow and silhouette, knowing what each can and cannot tell you.
  • Choose between k-means, hierarchical clustering, DBSCAN and Gaussian mixtures for a given data shape, and explain the choice.
  • Evaluate a clustering with no labels (silhouette, Davies–Bouldin, stability) and with labels (ARI, NMI).
  • Turn cluster ids into a profile table a stakeholder can act on.
  • Use PCA for compression, noise removal and preprocessing, read a scree plot, and avoid its two classic pitfalls.
  • Make and correctly read a t-SNE/UMAP map, and say out loud what it does not show.

1. What "no labels" changes

Every chapter so far had an answer key: a y column, a loss to minimise, a test set to score. Take that away and two things happen. First, the goal becomes fuzzier: "find groups" or "find a smaller representation" instead of "predict this column". Second, and this is the part that trips people up, there is no accuracy. You cannot ask "was the clustering right?" because nobody wrote down the right answer. You can only ask whether it is useful, stable and internally tidy.

Here is the running example for this chapter. You work at an online retailer with 40,000 customers and a table of behaviour per customer: days since last order (recency), orders in the past year (frequency), total spend (monetary), average basket size, share of orders with a discount code, share of returns, and a few dozen more. Marketing wants "segments" so they can send different campaigns. Nobody has labels; segments are the thing we are supposed to invent. By the end of the chapter you will have the whole recipe: scale, cluster, choose k, check stability, profile, name, ship.

Explain it like I'm 9

Supervised learning is a spelling test: you write the answer, the teacher marks it. Unsupervised learning is being handed a box of 500 LEGO pieces and told "sort these into piles that make sense." There is no answer sheet. Sorting by colour is one good answer; sorting by size is another. The only way to know whether your piles are good is whether they help you build something afterwards.

So how do we know it worked? Three lenses, used together:

  1. Internal quality. Are points close to their own group and far from others? Metrics like the silhouette (§2, §6) measure this with no labels at all.
  2. Stability. Re-run on a bootstrap sample or a different random seed. If the groups reshuffle completely, they were never real.
  3. Downstream usefulness. Does a campaign targeted by segment lift conversion? Does the compressed representation keep a supervised model's accuracy? This is the only judge that matters at work, and it is the one people forget to set up.
Key idea

Unsupervised results are hypotheses, not findings. A clustering is a proposal ("maybe customers come in five kinds") that you then test with stability checks, human inspection and a downstream experiment. Treat the cluster ids as a feature you invented, and hold it to the same standard as any other feature.

2. k-means, properly

The algorithm

k-means is the first clustering method everyone learns and the one you will use most. You pick k, the number of groups. The algorithm places k centroids (centre points) and then alternates two steps until nothing changes:

  1. Assign. Give every point to its nearest centroid (Euclidean distance).
  2. Update. Move every centroid to the mean of the points now assigned to it.

That is the whole thing. Each step can only lower the total squared distance, so it always converges, usually in a few dozen iterations. Figure 12.1 shows four snapshots from a deliberately bad start.

Explain it like I'm 9

Three ice-cream trucks park somewhere in a town. Every kid walks to the nearest truck (assign). Each truck then looks at where its kids came from and drives to the middle of that crowd (update). The kids re-pick the nearest truck, the trucks move again… until the trucks stop moving. Where they end up are the cluster centres. Where they started matters, which is the k-means++ story below.

Interactive: step through k-means
Phase: – Iteration: 0 Inertia: –
Press Init several times: different random starts give different final answers, and sometimes two centroids share one blob while another blob is split. Inertia (total squared distance to centroids) only ever goes down within a run, but different runs finish at different values. That is why sklearn runs several inits and keeps the best.

The objective: inertia

k-means is not just a procedure; it is minimising a specific number, the inertia (within-cluster sum of squares):

J = ∑j=1k ∑xi ∈ Cj ‖xi − μj‖²
The math (optional)

J is the inertia we minimise. k is the number of clusters. Cj is the set of points assigned to cluster j and μj is its centroid. ‖xi − μj‖² is the squared Euclidean distance from a point to its centroid. The assign step minimises J over assignments with centroids fixed; the update step minimises J over centroids with assignments fixed (the mean is the point that minimises total squared distance, Chapter 3). Alternating the two is coordinate descent, so J never increases, but it can stop at a local minimum. Note the squared distance: it is why k-means loves round blobs and hates outliers.

Initialization and k-means++

Because k-means only finds a local minimum, where the centroids start decides where they end. Pure random starts are bad: two centroids often land in the same blob. k-means++ fixes this by picking the first centroid at random, then picking each next centroid with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest already-chosen centroid, so starts are spread out. It is sklearn's default (init="k-means++"), and sklearn additionally restarts n_init times and keeps the lowest inertia. Set n_init=10 explicitly for anything you will show someone.

Choosing k: the elbow and the silhouette

Inertia always falls as k grows (k = n gives inertia 0), so you cannot pick k by minimising it. The elbow method plots inertia against k and looks for the bend where extra clusters stop paying off. It is a judgement call, and on real data the elbow is often a gentle curve with no obvious corner. The silhouette score gives you a number instead:

s(i) = (b(i) − a(i)) / max(a(i), b(i)),   silhouette score = mean of s(i) over all points
The math (optional)

For one point i, a(i) is its mean distance to the other points in its own cluster (how tight the cluster is) and b(i) is its mean distance to the points of the nearest other cluster (how far the closest rival is). s(i) lies in [−1, 1]: near 1 means "much closer to my own cluster than to any other", near 0 means "on the border between two clusters", negative means "probably in the wrong cluster". The score is the average over points. Higher is better, and it needs no labels. Rules of thumb: above 0.5 is a genuinely separated structure, 0.25–0.5 is weak, below 0.25 you are mostly carving a continuum. It costs O(n²) distances, so compute it on a sample of a few thousand points when n is large.

# k-means on blobs: elbow + silhouette
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

X, y_true = make_blobs(n_samples=600, centers=4, cluster_std=[1.0, 1.5, 0.8, 1.2],
                       random_state=42)
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)      # ALWAYS scale before k-means

for k in range(2, 9):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X)
    print(f"k={k}  inertia={km.inertia_:8.1f}  silhouette={silhouette_score(X, km.labels_):.3f}")
# k=2  inertia=   635.8  silhouette=0.555
# k=3  inertia=   153.2  silhouette=0.728
# k=4  inertia=    47.2  silhouette=0.766   <- elbow AND silhouette peak agree: k=4
# k=5  inertia=    39.5  silhouette=0.668
# k=6  inertia=    34.9  silhouette=0.567
# k=7  inertia=    31.6  silhouette=0.571
# k=8  inertia=    28.9  silhouette=0.479
At work: k is a business decision too

The silhouette says k = 4; marketing can run at most three campaigns and wants three segments. Use k = 3 and say why. The statistic is an input to the decision, not the decision. What you must not do is quietly pick the k that makes the story look best after peeking at the profiles. Decide your selection rule (silhouette, stability, business constraint) before you look, and write it in the notebook.

Failure modes

k-means assumes clusters are round, similar in size and spread, and free of outliers, because the objective is squared Euclidean distance to a single centre. Violate those and it fails in predictable ways (Figure 12.3).

Common mistake: clustering unscaled data

Distance is meaningless when one column is "total spend in dollars" (0–50,000) and another is "returns share" (0–1). Unscaled, k-means clusters on spend alone and the other columns are decoration. Scaling is mandatory before any distance-based method: StandardScaler by default, RobustScaler if there are outliers, and a log transform first for heavy-tailed money and count columns (Chapter 8). Also encode categoricals thoughtfully: one-hot with 30 categories injects 30 binary columns that swamp the numeric ones; consider dropping them or reducing with PCA first.

Mini-batch k-means for scale

Plain k-means touches every point in every iteration. With 50 million rows that is slow. MiniBatchKMeans updates centroids from random batches of a few thousand points and converges to nearly the same answer in a fraction of the time. Use it when the full version takes more than a few minutes; check that inertia and cluster sizes agree on a sample first.

3. Hierarchical clustering and the dendrogram

k-means asks you to fix k up front. Agglomerative (bottom-up) hierarchical clustering gives you every k at once: start with each point as its own cluster, then repeatedly merge the two closest clusters until one remains. Record the distance at which each merge happened and you get a dendrogram, a tree you can cut at any height to obtain any number of clusters.

Explain it like I'm 9

Everyone in the playground stands alone. Whistle: the two kids standing closest hold hands. Whistle again: the next-closest pair (or a pair and a kid) join. Keep going until the whole school is one chain. If you write down how far apart people were at each whistle, you can later say "everyone who joined before the 5-metre whistle is one team" and get teams of whatever size you like.

Linkage: what does "distance between two clusters" mean?

Between two points the distance is obvious. Between two groups of points you have to choose a rule, called the linkage, and it changes the result more than anything else (Figure 12.4).

The dendrogram and cutting it

Read a dendrogram from the bottom: leaves are points, each horizontal bar is a merge, and its height is the distance at which the merge happened. Long vertical lines mean "these groups stayed separate for a long time", which is the signature of real structure. To get clusters you cut with a horizontal line; the number of vertical lines it crosses is the number of clusters (Figure 12.5). Look for the biggest gap between consecutive merge heights and cut there.

import numpy as np
from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, dendrogram, fcluster
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.cluster import AgglomerativeClustering
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

X, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=60, centers=3, cluster_std=1.0, random_state=3)
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

for link in ["single", "complete", "average", "ward"]:
    agg = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=3, linkage=link).fit(X)
    print(link.ljust(8), "cluster sizes:", np.bincount(agg.labels_))

Z = linkage(X, method="ward")           # (n-1) x 4 merge table
print("last three merge distances:", np.round(Z[-3:, 2], 2))
labels_cut = fcluster(Z, t=3, criterion="maxclust")   # cut to get 3 groups
print("clusters after cutting:", np.bincount(labels_cut)[1:])

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 3.5))
dendrogram(Z, ax=ax, truncate_mode="lastp", p=12, color_threshold=Z[-2, 2])
ax.set_ylabel("merge distance"); fig.savefig("dendrogram.png", dpi=120, bbox_inches="tight")
# single   cluster sizes: [20 20 20]
# complete cluster sizes: [22 20 18]
# average  cluster sizes: [20 20 20]
# ward     cluster sizes: [20 20 20]
# last three merge distances: [ 1.9   6.54 13.44]   <- big jump: cut between 1.9 and 6.5
# clusters after cutting: [20 20 20]

The upside of hierarchical clustering: no k up front, any distance metric, a picture that non-technical people understand, and it works beautifully for things like grouping 300 products or 50 store locations. The downside: it is O(n²) memory and worse in time, so above roughly 20,000–50,000 rows you cluster a sample or switch to k-means. AgglomerativeClustering in sklearn gives the labels; scipy.cluster.hierarchy gives the tree.

4. DBSCAN: clusters as dense regions

k-means and Ward both assume a cluster has a centre. DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) drops that assumption: a cluster is a region where points are packed densely, whatever its shape, and points that sit alone are noise. Two hyperparameters define "dense": eps (ε), the radius of a neighbourhood, and min_samples, the number of points a neighbourhood must contain to count as crowded.

Explain it like I'm 9

At a festival, you and your friends stand in a crowd. If you can touch at least four people, you are "in the thick of it" (core). If you can only touch one person but that person is in the thick of it, you are on the edge of the crowd (border). If you are standing alone in the field, you are not in any crowd (noise). Crowds are wherever the thick-of-it people are connected, and they can be any shape, a ring around the stage or a line at the food truck.

What DBSCAN gives you that k-means cannot: arbitrary shapes, no k to choose, and an explicit outlier label, which is why it doubles as an anomaly detector. What it struggles with: clusters of different densities (one ε cannot be right for a tight cluster and a diffuse one), high-dimensional data (all distances become similar, so "dense" loses meaning; reduce with PCA first), and ε itself, which is fiddly. The standard way to set ε: compute each point's distance to its min_samples-th nearest neighbour, sort, plot, and take the knee. HDBSCAN (available as sklearn.cluster.HDBSCAN) removes ε entirely by trying all densities and keeping the clusters that persist longest; it is what I reach for first on messy real data.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN, KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score

X, y = make_moons(n_samples=400, noise=0.08, random_state=7)

km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X)
db = DBSCAN(eps=0.2, min_samples=5).fit(X)

n_noise = (db.labels_ == -1).sum()
n_clusters = len(set(db.labels_)) - (1 if n_noise else 0)
print("k-means ARI :", round(adjusted_rand_score(y, km.labels_), 3))
print("DBSCAN  ARI :", round(adjusted_rand_score(y, db.labels_), 3),
      "| clusters:", n_clusters, "| noise points:", n_noise)

# eps sensitivity sweep
for eps in [0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4]:
    lab = DBSCAN(eps=eps, min_samples=5).fit_predict(X)
    print(f"eps={eps:<5} clusters={len(set(lab)) - (1 if -1 in lab else 0):<3} noise={(lab==-1).sum()}")
# k-means ARI : 0.243
# DBSCAN  ARI : 1.0 | clusters: 2 | noise points: 0
# eps=0.05  clusters=19  noise=282
# eps=0.1   clusters=8   noise=32
# eps=0.2   clusters=2   noise=0
# eps=0.4   clusters=1   noise=0
Key idea

The eps sweep above is the whole DBSCAN experience: too small and everything is noise, too large and everything is one cluster. There is often only a narrow window that works. Always sweep, always print the number of clusters and the noise fraction, and be suspicious if more than 10–20% of points are noise, unless outliers are exactly what you were hunting.

5. Gaussian mixtures: soft clusters

k-means says every point belongs to exactly one cluster. A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) says the data was generated by k overlapping bell-shaped bumps (multivariate Gaussians), each with its own centre, its own shape (a full covariance matrix, so tilted ellipses are fine) and its own weight, and it assigns each point a probability of coming from each bump. Those probabilities are called responsibilities; they are the soft assignments.

EM in one paragraph. The GMM is fitted by Expectation–Maximisation, which is k-means with probabilities. E-step: given the current bumps, compute for every point the responsibility of each bump (how likely each bump was to have produced it). M-step: given those responsibilities, refit each bump's mean, covariance and weight as responsibility-weighted averages. Alternate until the log-likelihood stops improving. Like k-means it finds a local optimum, so use n_init of 3 or more. If you set every covariance to the same tiny sphere, the responsibilities become 0/1 and EM is k-means.

Choosing the number of components with BIC. Because a GMM is a probability model, it has a likelihood, and that means you can use information criteria. The Bayesian Information Criterion rewards fit and penalises the number of parameters:

BIC = −2 · log L + p · ln n   (lower is better)
The math (optional)

log L is the log-likelihood of the data under the fitted mixture (higher when the bumps fit well, and it always rises with more components). p is the number of free parameters: for k components in d dimensions with full covariances, p = k·d (means) + k·d(d+1)/2 (covariances) + (k − 1) (weights). n is the number of rows. The penalty p·ln n grows with data size, so BIC is stricter than AIC (whose penalty is 2p) and tends to pick fewer components. Pick the k with the lowest BIC, or the smallest k within a hair of the minimum.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=500, centers=3, cluster_std=[0.6, 1.4, 0.9], random_state=11)
X = X @ np.array([[1.0, 0.6], [0.0, 0.5]])       # shear -> elongated, non-spherical blobs
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

for n in range(1, 7):
    gmm = GaussianMixture(n_components=n, covariance_type="full", n_init=3, random_state=0).fit(X)
    print(f"components={n}  BIC={gmm.bic(X):8.1f}  AIC={gmm.aic(X):8.1f}")

gmm = GaussianMixture(n_components=3, covariance_type="full", n_init=3, random_state=0).fit(X)
proba = gmm.predict_proba(X)
unsure = np.where(proba.max(axis=1) < 0.9)[0]       # points the model is not sure about
print("points with max responsibility < 0.9:", len(unsure), "of", len(X))
print("soft assignments of three unsure points:\n", np.round(proba[unsure[:3]], 2))
print("mixing weights:", np.round(gmm.weights_, 2))
# components=1  BIC=  1674.5  AIC=  1653.5
# components=2  BIC=   781.3  AIC=   734.9
# components=3  BIC=   621.9  AIC=   550.2   <- lowest BIC
# components=4  BIC=   652.8  AIC=   555.8
# components=5  BIC=   685.5  AIC=   563.3
# components=6  BIC=   718.4  AIC=   570.9
# points with max responsibility < 0.9: 10 of 500

At work: when soft beats hard

Use a GMM when the "in-between" customers matter. Marketing rarely wants "customer 8812 is in segment 3"; they want "8812 is 60% value-seeker, 40% loyalist, so send the hybrid message". predict_proba gives that. GMMs are also a legitimate density model: score_samples returns a log-density, and the lowest few percent are anomalies, a trick we return to in Chapter 22.

With labels

Sometimes you do have labels: an old hand-made segmentation, or a benchmark like the digits where you know the truth. You cannot use accuracy because cluster ids are arbitrary (cluster "2" might be digit 7). Two metrics fix that. Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) counts pairs of points: are they together in both the truth and the clustering, or apart in both? "Adjusted" means chance agreement scores 0, perfect scores 1, and worse-than-random is negative. Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) asks how much knowing the cluster tells you about the label, on a 0–1 scale. ARI is stricter about splitting or merging true groups; NMI is more forgiving of a true class being split into two clusters. Report both when you can. Both are also what you use for the stability check above: ARI between two runs of the same algorithm.

Interpreting and naming clusters for stakeholders

A cluster id is useless until it has a name and a story. The tool is the profile table: one row per cluster, one column per feature, showing the cluster's mean (or median) next to the population's, ideally as a ratio or a z-score so the distinctive features jump out. Here is what ours looked like for the retailer, k = 4, after inverse-scaling back to original units:

Three habits make the table land. Sort features by how much they vary across clusters, and show only the top handful. Give each cluster a two-word name plus one sentence, and never name it after its id. And add the size: a 6% cluster with a dramatic profile is a niche, not a strategy. Then hand each cluster to a person who can say whether it matches their experience; if a cluster makes no sense to the people who talk to customers, it is probably an artefact of scaling or of k.

At work: four uses of clustering that pay
  • Segmentation (above): campaigns, pricing tiers, personas for product design.
  • Deduplication / entity resolution: cluster records by string-similarity distance (agglomerative with a precomputed distance) so "Acme Corp", "ACME Corporation" and "Acme Corp." land together.
  • Anomaly detection: DBSCAN noise points, GMM low density, or "far from every k-means centroid" flag odd transactions and sensor readings when you have no labelled failures yet.
  • Pre-labelling: cluster 100,000 unlabelled support tickets, label a handful per cluster, then train a supervised model (Chapter 6) on the propagated labels. Cuts labelling cost by 10× when clusters are clean.
Common mistake: clustering the id, then acting on it forever

Segments drift. The deal-hunter cluster of 2024 may not exist after you change the promo policy. Store the fitted scaler and model, re-assign customers weekly with predict, and re-fit quarterly, comparing the new profile table with the old one (ARI between old and new assignments). If ARI drops below about 0.7, the segmentation has changed and marketing needs to know before their dashboards silently mean something else.

7. Why reduce dimensions

Our customer table has 60 columns. Four reasons you might want fewer:

The curse of dimensionality

In high dimensions every point is far from every other, the nearest neighbour is barely nearer than the farthest, and distance-based methods (k-means, kNN, DBSCAN) lose their grip. Fewer, denser dimensions restore meaning to "close".

Visualization

Humans see two dimensions, three with effort. A 2-D map of 60-D data lets you spot clusters, outliers and mislabelled points in seconds.

Compression and speed

64 pixels → 20 components keeps 80% of the variance and makes every downstream model 3× faster to train, store and serve.

Noise removal

The small-variance directions are often measurement noise. Dropping them can improve a model, the way smoothing a photo removes grain.

Explain it like I'm 9

A 60-column table is like describing every kid in class with 60 facts. Most facts are related: taller kids are usually heavier and have bigger shoes. Dimensionality reduction finds the few "underlying" facts (like "overall size") that explain most of the 60, so you can describe each kid with 3 numbers instead of 60 and lose almost nothing.

8. PCA in full

Principal Component Analysis is the workhorse. It finds new axes, the principal components, that are (1) straight lines through the data, (2) perpendicular to each other, and (3) ordered so the first captures the most variance, the second the most remaining variance, and so on. Keeping the first few axes and dropping the rest is the reduction.

Step by step

  1. Centre every column (subtract its mean). sklearn does this for you. Also scale each column to unit variance unless the columns share a unit and you want big-variance columns to dominate; with mixed units, unscaled PCA just returns the biggest-unit column as PC1.
  2. Find the direction of maximum variance. Among all unit vectors, PC1 is the one along which the projected data spreads out most (Figure 12.8; play with the slider below).
  3. Repeat perpendicular to what you have. PC2 is the max-variance direction orthogonal to PC1, and so on, up to d components for d columns.
  4. Project. The new coordinates (scores) of a row are its dot products with each component. Keep the first m columns of scores.
Interactive: find the direction of maximum variance
Projected variance: – Share of total: –
Rotate the axis. The bar on the right is the variance of the dots after they are dropped onto the axis. It peaks at exactly one angle: that is PC1. Ninety degrees away is PC2, the minimum. PCA finds this maximum algebraically instead of by searching.
The math (optional): eigenvectors, gently

Let X be the centred data (n rows, d columns) and Σ = XᵀX / (n − 1) its d × d covariance matrix. For a unit direction w, the variance of the projections Xw is wᵀΣw. Maximising wᵀΣw subject to ‖w‖ = 1 gives the equation

Σ w = λ w

which says w is an eigenvector of Σ and λ its eigenvalue, and λ equals exactly the variance along w. Σ is symmetric, so it has d perpendicular eigenvectors. Sort them by λ from largest to smallest: those are PC1, PC2, …, and the λ's are their variances. The explained variance ratio of PCj is λj / ∑λ. In practice sklearn computes this via the SVD of X (X = U S Vᵀ, components are the rows of Vᵀ, λj = Sj² / (n − 1)), which is numerically nicer and never forms Σ.

Explained variance and the scree plot

Each component's eigenvalue divided by the total is its explained variance ratio. Plot them in order, the scree plot, with the cumulative sum, and you can see how many components carry the signal (Figure 12.9).

Choosing the number of components. Three rules, in order of how often I use them: (1) keep enough for a variance target, and PCA(n_components=0.9) does exactly that; (2) tune it like any hyperparameter against the downstream model's validation score (Chapter 13); (3) for visualization, 2 or 3, full stop. Reconstruction: inverse_transform maps scores back to the original space using only the kept components; the reconstruction error is a direct readout of what you threw away and is a fine anomaly score (rows that reconstruct badly are unlike the bulk). Whitening (whiten=True) divides each score by its standard deviation so all components have unit variance; some downstream algorithms (ICA, some kernels) want that, most do not, and it amplifies the noisy tail components, so leave it off unless you know you need it.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

digits = load_digits()
X, y = digits.data, digits.target          # 1797 images, 64 pixels each
Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

pca = PCA().fit(Xs)                        # all 64 components
evr = pca.explained_variance_ratio_
cum = np.cumsum(evr)
print("first 5 explained-variance ratios:", np.round(evr[:5], 3))
print("components for 80% / 90% / 95% variance:",
      np.argmax(cum >= .80) + 1, np.argmax(cum >= .90) + 1, np.argmax(cum >= .95) + 1)

Z2 = PCA(n_components=2).fit_transform(Xs)
print("2-D projection shape:", Z2.shape)
for d in [0, 1]:
    m = Z2[y == d].mean(axis=0)
    print(f"digit {d} centre in PC space: ({m[0]:.1f}, {m[1]:.1f})")

# reconstruction error vs number of components
for k in [2, 10, 30]:
    p = PCA(n_components=k).fit(Xs)
    rec = p.inverse_transform(p.transform(Xs))
    print(f"k={k:<3} reconstruction MSE={((Xs - rec)**2).mean():.3f}")

# PCA as preprocessing inside a supervised pipeline
for k in [None, 30, 10]:
    pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), PCA(n_components=k), LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000))
    acc = cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5).mean()
    print(f"PCA n_components={str(k):<5} -> 5-fold accuracy {acc:.3f}")
# first 5 explained-variance ratios: [0.12  0.096 0.084 0.065 0.049]
# components for 80% / 90% / 95% variance: 21 31 40
# 2-D projection shape: (1797, 2)
# digit 0 centre in PC space: (-1.6, -1.9)
# digit 1 centre in PC space: (-0.5, 0.5)
# k=2   reconstruction MSE=0.747
# k=10  reconstruction MSE=0.392
# k=30  reconstruction MSE=0.102
# PCA n_components=None  -> 5-fold accuracy 0.920
# PCA n_components=30    -> 5-fold accuracy 0.907
# PCA n_components=10    -> 5-fold accuracy 0.840
Common mistake: PCA is unsupervised, so it does not know what you care about

PCA keeps high-variance directions. Your label may live in a low-variance direction: a subtle pixel pattern, a small but consistent difference in return rate. In the code above, PCA to 10 components dropped accuracy from 0.92 to 0.84, because the discriminative information was spread across many small components. Never fix n_components in advance for a supervised task; put PCA inside the Pipeline and let cross-validation pick it, including the option "no PCA at all". And fit PCA on the training fold only, exactly like a scaler, or you leak test-set structure into the components.

Key idea

Scale first, then PCA, then whatever comes next, all in one Pipeline. make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), PCA(n_components=0.9), LogisticRegression()) is the idiom. The pipeline guarantees PCA is refit per fold and that the same transformation is applied at prediction time.

9. t-SNE and UMAP: maps for looking, not modelling

PCA is linear: it can only rotate and drop axes. When the structure is curved, a 2-D PCA plot of the digits is a smear where every class overlaps (silhouette by true class: 0.07 in the code below). t-SNE and UMAP are non-linear methods built for one job, drawing a 2-D picture in which points that were neighbours in high dimensions stay neighbours. They do that job spectacularly (Figure 12.10) and are terrible at everything else.

How t-SNE works, briefly. For every pair of points it computes a probability that they are neighbours in the original space, using a Gaussian whose width is set per point so that each has roughly perplexity effective neighbours (typical 5–50; think "how big a neighbourhood to preserve"). It then places points in 2-D and moves them by gradient descent until the 2-D neighbour probabilities (using a heavy-tailed t-distribution, hence the name) match the original ones as closely as possible. Only local neighbourhoods are matched, which is exactly why global distances mean nothing. UMAP does something similar with a graph-based construction, its n_neighbors playing the role of perplexity, runs 10× faster, preserves a bit more global structure, and has a transform method for new points. It is not in sklearn (install umap-learn), which is the only reason the code below uses t-SNE.

Explain it like I'm 9

Imagine every kid holds hands with their five best friends, then everyone is dropped onto a football field and told: "get next to your friends, and stay away from everyone else." Groups of friends end up in clumps. But how far the football clump is from the chess clump means nothing; they just drifted. And a big clump is not a bigger friend group, it just spread out more. t-SNE is that game played by dots.

Common mistake: reading a t-SNE plot like a scatter plot
  • Distances between clusters are not meaningful. Two islands far apart may be close in the data, or not. The algorithm does not try to preserve that.
  • Cluster sizes and densities are not meaningful. t-SNE expands dense regions and compresses sparse ones; every island ends up about the same density.
  • It can invent clusters. With small perplexity a uniform blob becomes a set of islands. Always try 2–3 perplexities and two seeds; trust only what survives.
  • Never use the coordinates as features or run k-means on them and call it a segmentation. Cluster in the original (or PCA-reduced) space; use the map to show the result.
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.manifold import TSNE
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

digits = load_digits()
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
idx = rng.choice(len(digits.data), size=500, replace=False)   # small subset: t-SNE is O(n^2)-ish
X, y = digits.data[idx], digits.target[idx]
Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

X30 = PCA(n_components=30, random_state=0).fit_transform(Xs)  # standard trick: PCA first, then t-SNE
emb = TSNE(n_components=2, perplexity=30, init="pca", random_state=0).fit_transform(X30)

print("embedding shape:", emb.shape)
print("silhouette by true digit, PCA-2D :", round(silhouette_score(PCA(n_components=2).fit_transform(Xs), y), 2))
print("silhouette by true digit, t-SNE  :", round(silhouette_score(emb, y), 2))
# embedding shape: (500, 2)
# silhouette by true digit, PCA-2D : 0.07
# silhouette by true digit, t-SNE  : 0.38   <- t-SNE separates the digits far better (for LOOKING, not modelling)

Autoencoders (Chapter 16) are the neural-network generalisation of PCA: an encoder network squeezes the input into a small bottleneck vector and a decoder tries to reconstruct the input from it. With linear layers and squared error, the bottleneck spans the same subspace as PCA; with non-linear layers it can learn curved manifolds, which is what makes it useful for images, text embeddings and sensor streams. The evaluation logic is identical to PCA's reconstruction error, and the same warning applies: it is unsupervised and does not know what you care about.

Key idea

Whatever you pick, the workflow is the same: log-transform skewed columns → scale → (PCA if > ~20 columns) → cluster → sweep k or ε → check stability → profile and name → validate downstream → store the fitted pipeline and monitor drift. Skipping the scale step or the stability step is where 90% of bad segmentations come from.

Quiz

Check your understanding

1. Inertia keeps decreasing as k increases. Why can't you pick k by minimising inertia?

Inertia is a training-fit measure with no penalty for complexity. Every point as its own cluster scores a perfect 0. The elbow looks for diminishing returns; the silhouette rewards separation and punishes splitting real clusters.

2. Your customer data has two crescent-shaped groups and a handful of extreme outliers. Which method handles both problems out of the box?

DBSCAN grows clusters through dense regions, so shapes do not matter, and points in sparse regions are labelled −1 (noise) instead of distorting a centroid. k-means and Ward both assume compact, centred groups.

3. A GMM gives a customer responsibilities [0.52, 0.48, 0.00]. What does that mean?

Responsibilities are posterior probabilities. Near-50/50 means the point is on the boundary between two bumps. k-means would silently pick one; the GMM tells you it is a coin flip, which is useful for a hybrid message.

4. You run PCA on unscaled columns: annual spend (0–50,000 dollars) and returns share (0–1). PC1 is…

PCA maximises variance in whatever units it is given. Dollars have variance in the millions; a share has variance below 0.25. Without scaling, PC1 ≈ spend and PCA has told you nothing. Scale first.

5. In a t-SNE plot, cluster A is far from cluster B and twice as large. What can you conclude?

t-SNE preserves local neighbourhoods and deliberately ignores global distances; it also equalises densities, so island size is an artefact. Check the true counts and distances in the original space.

6. You have an old hand-made segmentation and a new k-means one. Which metric compares them fairly, given the cluster ids are arbitrary?

ARI and NMI compare groupings regardless of how the groups are numbered, and both are adjusted or normalised so chance agreement scores about 0. Silhouette and inertia are label-free internal measures and cannot compare two partitions.

Exercises

Exercise 12.1 — Break k-means, then fix it

Generate make_blobs(n_samples=[400, 40, 40], cluster_std=[2.5, 0.4, 0.4], random_state=0) (one big diffuse blob, two tiny tight ones). Run k-means with k = 3, then a GMM with 3 components, then DBSCAN. Compare each to the true labels with ARI. Which wins and why? Now add three points at (30, 30) and repeat.

Show answer

k-means (ARI ≈ 0.5–0.7) carves the big blob and steals its edges for the small ones because it assumes equal spread; the GMM (ARI ≈ 0.9+) fits a wide Gaussian and two narrow ones, which is exactly the generating story; DBSCAN depends on ε: an ε right for the tight blobs makes most of the diffuse blob noise. With the three far outliers, k-means gives them their own centroid (the inertia saving is enormous) and merges two real groups; the GMM usually still recovers the three blobs because a component can put a tiny weight on the outliers; DBSCAN labels them −1, the correct answer. Lesson: shape assumptions matter more than the algorithm's popularity.

Exercise 12.2 — Stability audit

On the scaled load_wine features, run k-means (k = 3) with 10 different random_state values and compute the pairwise ARI between all 45 pairs of runs. Then repeat with n_init=1 versus n_init=10. Report the mean and minimum ARI. How does k-means++ plus restarts affect stability?

Show answer

With n_init=10 every run finds the same (best-inertia) solution and all pairwise ARIs are 1.0. With n_init=1 some seeds land in a worse local minimum and a few pairwise ARIs drop to roughly 0.6–0.8. The wine data has three well-separated varieties (ARI against the true cultivar ≈ 0.9 when scaled), which is why restarts fix it completely; on messier data you will see residual instability even with restarts, and that residual is the honest measure of how real your clusters are.

Exercise 12.3 — Do it at work: a segmentation memo

Take any customer, product, store or machine table you have access to (10+ numeric columns, 1,000+ rows). Log-transform skewed columns, scale, run k-means for k = 2…8 with silhouette on a 5,000-row sample, pick k with a stated rule, check stability across 5 seeds with ARI, and write a one-page memo containing: the profile table (top 6 distinguishing features, cluster sizes), a two-word name per cluster, one action per cluster, and one sentence on how you will detect when the segmentation drifts.

Show answer

There is no single right answer, but a good memo has: k justified by a rule chosen before looking at profiles; every cluster with a name a colleague would recognise; at least one cluster you decided not to act on because it is too small or too vague; a monitoring plan (weekly predict with the stored pipeline, quarterly refit, alert if ARI between old and new assignments < 0.7). If the silhouette never exceeds 0.2, the honest memo says "this data does not have segments; here is a continuous score instead", and that memo is more valuable than a forced one.

Chapter summary

  • Without labels there is no accuracy. Judge clusters by internal quality (silhouette), stability (ARI across seeds/bootstraps) and downstream usefulness.
  • k-means = assign to nearest centroid, move centroid to mean, repeat. It minimises inertia, finds local optima (use k-means++ and n_init ≥ 10), assumes round equal clusters, and hates outliers and unscaled data.
  • Pick k with the elbow (judgement) and the silhouette (a number), then let the business constraint have a vote, decided before you look at profiles.
  • Hierarchical clustering gives all k at once via a dendrogram; linkage (single/complete/average/Ward) determines cluster shape; O(n²), so sample above ~30k rows.
  • DBSCAN clusters dense regions of any shape and labels sparse points as noise; sweep ε; HDBSCAN removes the ε headache.
  • Gaussian mixtures give soft, elliptical clusters via EM; choose components with BIC; predict_proba and score_samples are the payoff.
  • A clustering is not done until it has a profile table, a name per cluster, a size, and a drift check.
  • PCA: scale, centre, take the top eigenvectors of the covariance; read the scree plot; tune n_components by CV inside a Pipeline; it may drop discriminative low-variance directions.
  • t-SNE/UMAP are for looking. Distances between islands and island sizes mean nothing; never feed their coordinates to a model.
