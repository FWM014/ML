A running example threads through the chapter. You work for a retailer that sells a cold-brew coffee maker. You have eight years of monthly unit sales (96 numbers) and procurement needs a 12-month-ahead forecast every quarter, with a range they can plan stock against. Every method below is fitted to this series, tested on the same final 12 months, and compared in one table at the end. The series is synthetic so you can regenerate it exactly, but its shape (growth, a summer peak, sticky noise) is what real demand looks like.

1. What makes time series different

In every previous chapter the rows of your table were exchangeable: shuffle the customers, nothing changes. A time series is the opposite. Row 37 is March 2020 and row 38 is April 2020, and the whole point is that April depends on March. Three consequences follow, and each one breaks a habit you learned in Chapter 7.

  1. Order carries information. The most predictive feature of next month is usually this month. Lose the order and you lose the signal.
  2. Observations are autocorrelated. Neighbouring values are similar. Errors are too. So "n = 96 independent samples" is a fiction; your effective sample size is much smaller, and your confidence in any result should be correspondingly humbler.
  3. You cannot shuffle. A random train/test split puts June 2023 in the training set and May 2023 in the test set. The model then "predicts" May from a June it already saw. Scores look wonderful. They are a lie.
Explain it like I'm 9

Guessing the weather for a day in the middle of last week is easy if you're told what happened the day before and the day after: you just say "somewhere in between." That's the random split. Forecasting is guessing next Tuesday when all you know is up to today. Every test you run has to look like that, or you're grading yourself on the easy game and then playing the hard one for real.

Key idea

Time only flows one way, and your validation must too. Every feature must be computable from information available at the moment the forecast is made, and every test set must lie strictly after its training set. Almost every time-series failure at work is a violation of one of those two sentences.

2. The four components

Before modelling anything, take the series apart. Nearly every business series is a sum (or a product) of four things:

Trend

The slow, long-run direction. Our coffee maker sells about 2.5 more units every month than the month before: the business is growing.

Seasonality

A pattern that repeats with a fixed, known period: 12 for monthly, 7 for daily, 24 for hourly. Cold brew peaks in June and troughs in December, every year.

Cycles

Ups and downs with no fixed period: business cycles, product hype waves, multi-year commodity swings. Harder to model because you can't set a calendar to them. Often folded into "trend."

Noise (residual)

What's left. Ideally unpredictable and small. If it still shows structure (autocorrelation, a leftover season), your decomposition missed something.

Additive or multiplicative? If the seasonal swings grow as the level grows (a 10% summer bump, so bigger every year), the components multiply: y = T × S × R. Take the log and it becomes additive again. Rule of thumb: plot the series; if the "envelope" widens over time, use model="multiplicative" or log-transform first.

# Build the running example: 8 years of monthly demand with trend, season and sticky noise
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
idx = pd.date_range("2017-01-01", periods=96, freq="MS")      # 8 years of month-starts
t = np.arange(96)
e = rng.normal(0, 10, size=96); noise = np.zeros(96)
for i in range(1, 96):
    noise[i] = 0.6 * noise[i - 1] + e[i]                        # autocorrelated noise (AR(1))
y = pd.Series(200 + 2.5*t + 40*np.sin(2*np.pi*(t - 2)/12) + noise, index=idx, name="units")

print(y.head(3).round(1).to_string())
print("mean =", round(y.mean(), 1), " std =", round(y.std(), 1))
dec = seasonal_decompose(y, model="additive", period=12)
print("seasonal profile (Jan..Dec):", dec.seasonal[:12].round(0).astype(int).tolist())
tr = dec.trend.dropna()
print("trend slope/month ≈", round(np.polyfit(np.arange(len(tr)), tr.values, 1)[0], 2))
print("residual std ≈", round(dec.resid.std(), 1))
# 2017-01-01    165.4
# 2017-02-01    172.1
# 2017-03-01    206.3
# Freq: MS
# mean = 318.5  std = 74.5
# seasonal profile (Jan..Dec): [-39, -22, -1, 22, 37, 43, 37, 19, 0, -22, -34, -38]
# trend slope/month ≈ 2.43
# residual std ≈ 7.3

The decomposition recovered what we put in: a slope of about 2.5 per month, a ±40 seasonal swing peaking in June, and residual noise around 7 to 10. On real data you don't know the answer, so this is your first diagnostic: how much of the variance is trend, how much is season, how much is noise? The noise share is roughly the floor on how good any forecast can get.

3. Stationarity, differencing and autocorrelation

Stationarity

A series is stationary if its statistical behaviour does not depend on when you look: same mean, same variance, same autocorrelation structure in 2017 as in 2024. Classical models (ARIMA in particular) assume it, because a model can only learn a pattern that stays put. Our coffee series is obviously not stationary: it drifts upward and it has a seasonal wave.

Explain it like I'm 9

Imagine trying to learn the rules of a game while the rules keep changing. Stationary means the rules stay the same, so what you learned from last year still works this year. Differencing is a trick: instead of tracking your score (which keeps climbing), track how much your score changed since last turn. The changes usually follow stable rules even when the score doesn't.

Two transforms fix most non-stationarity:

  • Differencing removes trend: replace yt with Δyt = yt − yt−1. A straight-line trend becomes a constant. Seasonal differencing removes a repeating pattern: yt − yt−12 ("same month last year"). You can apply both.
  • Log transform stabilises variance: if swings scale with the level, log(y) has constant-size swings. It also turns multiplicative seasonality into additive, and growth into a linear trend. Forecast in log space, then exp() back (and remember that exp of a mean forecast is a median, not a mean; add half the variance if you need the mean).

The ADF test in one paragraph

The Augmented Dickey-Fuller test asks: "does this series behave like a random walk (a unit root, non-stationary) or does it get pulled back toward a level?" Its null hypothesis is non-stationary; a small p-value (say < 0.05) lets you reject that and treat the series as stationary. Use it as a sanity check, not a verdict: it has low power on short series, it cannot see seasonality, and a series can pass ADF while still having a seasonal pattern that wrecks your model. Plot first, test second.

Autocorrelation: ACF and PACF

The autocorrelation function (ACF) at lag k is the correlation between yt and yt−k: how much does a value resemble the value k steps earlier? The partial autocorrelation (PACF) at lag k is the same correlation after removing the effect of lags 1 through k−1: the "direct" link at that distance.

Explain it like I'm 9

ACF at lag 2 asks "is today like the day before yesterday?" But today is like yesterday, and yesterday is like the day before, so of course it is, a bit. PACF at lag 2 asks the sharper question: "once I already know yesterday, does the day before yesterday tell me anything extra?"

# Stationarity check and autocorrelations on the running example
import numpy as np, pandas as pd, warnings; warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller, acf, pacf
rng = np.random.default_rng(42); idx = pd.date_range("2017-01-01", periods=96, freq="MS"); t = np.arange(96)
e = rng.normal(0, 10, size=96); noise = np.zeros(96)
for i in range(1, 96): noise[i] = 0.6 * noise[i - 1] + e[i]
y = pd.Series(200 + 2.5*t + 40*np.sin(2*np.pi*(t - 2)/12) + noise, index=idx, name="units")

for name, s in [("raw", y), ("diff(1)", y.diff().dropna()), ("diff(1)+diff(12)", y.diff().diff(12).dropna())]:
    stat, p = adfuller(s, autolag="AIC")[:2]
    print(f"{name:18s} ADF={stat:6.2f}  p={p:.3f}  {'stationary' if p < 0.05 else 'NOT stationary'}")
a, pa = acf(y, nlags=24), pacf(y, nlags=24)
print("ACF  lags 1,2,3,6,12,24:", [round(float(a[k]),2) for k in (1,2,3,6,12,24)])
print("PACF lags 1,2,3,12    :", [round(float(pa[k]),2) for k in (1,2,3,12)])
ad = acf(y.diff().dropna(), nlags=24)
print("ACF of diff(1) lags 1,6,12:", [round(float(ad[k]),2) for k in (1,6,12)])
# raw                ADF= -1.46  p=0.555  NOT stationary
# diff(1)            ADF= -8.13  p=0.000  stationary
# diff(1)+diff(12)   ADF= -4.80  p=0.000  stationary
# ACF  lags 1,2,3,6,12,24: [0.95, 0.86, 0.75, 0.52, 0.64, 0.34]
# PACF lags 1,2,3,12    : [0.96, -0.46, -0.06, -0.16]
# ACF of diff(1) lags 1,6,12: [0.64, -0.72, 0.68]
Key idea

The ACF is your feature-selection tool for time series. Big bars at lags 1, 2 and 12 are telling you exactly which lag features to build in Section 7 (lag_1, lag_2, lag_12) and which ARIMA orders to try in Section 6. Look at it before you model anything.

4. Baselines you must beat

Forecasting has a humbling secret: dumb methods are strong. In the M-competitions (the field's benchmark series, run since 1982), simple methods routinely beat sophisticated ones, and in industry a large share of "ML forecasting" projects never beat seasonal naive. So before any model, compute these four. They cost nothing and they are your units of progress: a model that is 30% better than seasonal naive is a result; a model with "MAE = 12.3" is a number without meaning.

The math: the four baselines
Naive: ŷT+h = yT     Seasonal naive: ŷT+h = yT+h−m     Drift: ŷT+h = yT + h · (yT − y1)/(T − 1)     Moving average: ŷT+h = (1/k) ∑i=0k−1 yT−i

T is the last observed time (the forecast origin); h is how many steps ahead (the horizon); m is the seasonal period (12 for monthly); k is the moving-average window. Naive repeats the last value forever. Seasonal naive repeats "the same month last year". Drift draws a straight line from the first to the last observation and extends it. Moving average predicts the recent mean. Combine them: seasonal naive + drift repeats last year's pattern shifted up by a year's worth of trend, and it is a formidable opponent.

# The baselines every forecast must beat, scored on the final 12 months
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
rng = np.random.default_rng(42); idx = pd.date_range("2017-01-01", periods=96, freq="MS"); t = np.arange(96)
e = rng.normal(0, 10, size=96); noise = np.zeros(96)
for i in range(1, 96): noise[i] = 0.6 * noise[i - 1] + e[i]
y = pd.Series(200 + 2.5*t + 40*np.sin(2*np.pi*(t - 2)/12) + noise, index=idx, name="units")

train, test = y[:-12], y[-12:]                    # chronological split, never shuffled
h = len(test)
def mae(actual, forecast): return float(np.mean(np.abs(np.asarray(actual) - np.asarray(forecast))))
slope = (train.iloc[-1] - train.iloc[0]) / (len(train) - 1)
baselines = {
  "naive (last value)":        np.repeat(train.iloc[-1], h),
  "moving average (12)":       np.repeat(train.iloc[-12:].mean(), h),
  "drift":                     train.iloc[-1] + slope * np.arange(1, h + 1),
  "seasonal naive":            train.iloc[-12:].values,
  "seasonal naive + drift":    train.iloc[-12:].values + 12 * slope,
}
for name, fc in baselines.items():
    print(f"{name:24s} MAE = {mae(test, fc):5.1f}")
# naive (last value)       MAE =  59.1
# moving average (12)      MAE =  38.4
# drift                    MAE =  47.4
# seasonal naive           MAE =  27.7
# seasonal naive + drift   MAE =   6.5
At work

Put the seasonal-naive number on the first slide of every forecasting review. Stakeholders anchor on it instantly ("we'd be within 28 units just by copying last year"), and every improvement you report afterwards has a denominator. If your fancy model loses to seasonal naive on a fair backtest, which happens more often than anyone admits, ship seasonal naive and go find better features. That is not a failure; it is the cheapest possible win.

5. Exponential smoothing

A moving average treats the last k points equally and everything older as worthless. That's crude. Exponential smoothing says: the most recent value matters most, the one before a bit less, and so on, with weights that decay geometrically. One parameter, α (alpha, between 0 and 1), controls how fast they decay.

Explain it like I'm 9

You're guessing how long your walk to school takes. Each morning you update your guess: "new guess = mostly my old guess, plus a little bit of today's actual time." If α is big you trust today a lot and your guess jumps around; if α is tiny you barely budge and your guess lags behind when the road works start. Smoothing is choosing how much to trust today.

The math: the smoothing family
Simple: ℓt = α yt + (1 − α) ℓt−1,   ŷt+h = ℓt
Holt (add trend): ℓt = α yt + (1 − α)(ℓt−1 + bt−1),   bt = β(ℓt − ℓt−1) + (1 − β) bt−1,   ŷt+h = ℓt + h bt
Holt-Winters (add season): ℓt = α(yt − st−m) + (1 − α)(ℓt−1 + bt−1),   st = γ(yt − ℓt) + (1 − γ) st−m,   ŷt+h = ℓt + h bt + st+h−m

ℓt is the smoothed level (where the series is now, with noise removed); bt is the smoothed slope (how much it moves per step); st is the seasonal offset for time t, one per position in the cycle of length m. α, β, γ (all in [0,1]) set how quickly level, slope and season adapt to new data: near 0 means "trust history", near 1 means "trust the latest point". statsmodels picks them by minimising in-sample squared error. Unrolling the simple version shows why it's called exponential: ℓt = α yt + α(1−α) yt−1 + α(1−α)2 yt−2 + …, weights that shrink by (1−α) per step.

# Simple, Holt, and Holt-Winters exponential smoothing (statsmodels)
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.holtwinters import SimpleExpSmoothing, Holt, ExponentialSmoothing
rng = np.random.default_rng(42); idx = pd.date_range("2017-01-01", periods=96, freq="MS"); t = np.arange(96)
e = rng.normal(0, 10, size=96); noise = np.zeros(96)
for i in range(1, 96): noise[i] = 0.6 * noise[i - 1] + e[i]
y = pd.Series(200 + 2.5*t + 40*np.sin(2*np.pi*(t - 2)/12) + noise, index=idx, name="units")
train, test = y[:-12], y[-12:]
def mae(a, f): return float(np.mean(np.abs(np.asarray(a) - np.asarray(f))))

ses  = SimpleExpSmoothing(train, initialization_method="estimated").fit()
holt = Holt(train, initialization_method="estimated").fit()
hw   = ExponentialSmoothing(train, trend="add", seasonal="add", seasonal_periods=12,
                            initialization_method="estimated").fit()
p = hw.params
print(f"SES          alpha={ses.params['smoothing_level']:.2f}                     MAE={mae(test, ses.forecast(12)):5.1f}")
print(f"Holt         alpha={holt.params['smoothing_level']:.2f} beta={holt.params['smoothing_trend']:.2f}           MAE={mae(test, holt.forecast(12)):5.1f}")
print(f"Holt-Winters alpha={p['smoothing_level']:.2f} beta={p['smoothing_trend']:.2f} gamma={p['smoothing_seasonal']:.2f} MAE={mae(test, hw.forecast(12)):5.1f}")
print(hw.forecast(3).round(1).to_string())
# SES          alpha=1.00                     MAE= 59.1
# Holt         alpha=0.96 beta=0.93           MAE= 93.6
# Holt-Winters alpha=0.79 beta=0.00 gamma=0.00 MAE= 10.0
# 2024-01-01    364.0
# 2024-02-01    380.5
# 2024-03-01    406.0

Read those parameters like a detective. Simple smoothing chose α = 1: it collapsed into the naive forecast, because on a strongly seasonal series "the last value" is the best a level-only model can do. Holt is worse than naive: with β = 0.93 it treats the December-to-January seasonal drop as a trend and extrapolates it downhill for a year. Holt-Winters, the only one with a seasonal component, is the only one that works. A model that lacks a component the data has will find a wrong way to fake it. Options to note: damped_trend=True flattens the trend as h grows (safer for long horizons, and a reliable default in the M-competitions), and seasonal="mul" for growing seasonal swings.

Interactive: smoothing and the lag it buys you
Avg lag: exp ≈ – steps, MA = – steps
The grey series has a step change at month 40. Small α or big k gives a beautifully smooth line that takes ages to notice the step (look at how far behind it lags). Large α or k = 1 follows the step instantly but copies every wiggle of noise. That trade-off, responsiveness versus noise, is the whole art of smoothing, and the reason statsmodels fits α from data rather than letting you guess.

6. ARIMA and SARIMA

ARIMA is the classical statistician's forecasting model, and it is worth understanding even though you may end up using boosting more often, because its vocabulary (AR, MA, differencing, orders) is how forecasting people talk. The name lists its three parts.

AR(p): AutoRegressive

Today is a weighted sum of the last p values plus noise. "Momentum." yt = c + φ1yt−1 + … + φpyt−p + εt. Picked from the PACF cut-off.

I(d): Integrated

Difference the series d times first so it's stationary, then model the differences and undo the differencing at forecast time. d is usually 0 or 1, rarely 2.

MA(q): Moving Average

Today is a weighted sum of the last q forecast errors. "Correcting for recent surprises." yt = c + εt + θ1εt−1 + … + θqεt−q. Picked from the ACF cut-off.

Explain it like I'm 9

Guessing tomorrow's temperature: AR says "it'll be like the last few days" (momentum). MA says "I was surprised by how warm it was yesterday, so I'll nudge my guess up a bit" (learning from recent mistakes). The I part says "the weather keeps warming all spring, so let me predict the change from today rather than the temperature itself." ARIMA does all three with knobs p, d, q.

The math: SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)m
φp(B) ΦP(Bm) (1 − B)d (1 − Bm)D yt = θq(B) ΘQ(Bm) εt

B is the backshift operator: B yt = yt−1, so (1 − B) yt is one difference and (1 − Bm) yt is a seasonal difference. φp(B) is the AR polynomial of order p and θq(B) the MA polynomial of order q, acting on adjacent steps. ΦP, ΘQ are the same ideas acting at multiples of the season m (lags 12, 24, …). d, D count ordinary and seasonal differences. εt is white noise. The famous "airline model" is SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)12: difference once, seasonally difference once, then one MA term at each scale. It fits an astonishing number of monthly business series.

Choosing the orders

The classical route is Box-Jenkins: difference until stationary (that gives d and D), look at ACF/PACF of what remains for p and q, fit, check that the residuals look like white noise, repeat. The modern route is auto-ARIMA (the pmdarima package, or a small grid in plain statsmodels): try many (p,d,q)(P,D,Q) combinations, keep the one with the lowest AIC (a fit score penalised for parameter count), then check the residuals. On our series a grid over p,q ∈ {0,1,2} and P,Q ∈ {0,1} picks (0,1,1)(0,1,1)12, the airline model, with AIC 552.7 versus 554.7 for the (1,1,1)(0,1,1)12 we use below. The difference is negligible; both forecast almost identically.

# ARIMA vs SARIMA, plus a 95% prediction interval from the seasonal model
import numpy as np, pandas as pd, warnings; warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")
from statsmodels.tsa.statespace.sarimax import SARIMAX
rng = np.random.default_rng(42); idx = pd.date_range("2017-01-01", periods=96, freq="MS"); t = np.arange(96)
e = rng.normal(0, 10, size=96); noise = np.zeros(96)
for i in range(1, 96): noise[i] = 0.6 * noise[i - 1] + e[i]
y = pd.Series(200 + 2.5*t + 40*np.sin(2*np.pi*(t - 2)/12) + noise, index=idx, name="units")
train, test = y[:-12], y[-12:]
def mae(a, f): return float(np.mean(np.abs(np.asarray(a) - np.asarray(f))))

arima  = SARIMAX(train, order=(1, 1, 1)).fit(disp=False)
sarima = SARIMAX(train, order=(1, 1, 1), seasonal_order=(0, 1, 1, 12)).fit(disp=False)
print(f"ARIMA(1,1,1)             AIC={arima.aic:6.1f}  MAE={mae(test, arima.forecast(12)):5.1f}")
print(f"SARIMA(1,1,1)(0,1,1)[12] AIC={sarima.aic:6.1f}  MAE={mae(test, sarima.forecast(12)):5.1f}")
print(sarima.params.round(3).to_string())
fc = sarima.get_forecast(12)
band = fc.conf_int(alpha=0.05)                       # 95% prediction interval
out = pd.concat([fc.predicted_mean.rename("forecast"), band], axis=1).round(1)
print(out.iloc[[0, 5, 11]].to_string())
print("share of actuals inside the 95% band:", float(((test >= band.iloc[:,0]) & (test <= band.iloc[:,1])).mean()))
# ARIMA(1,1,1)             AIC= 676.9  MAE= 64.0
# SARIMA(1,1,1)(0,1,1)[12] AIC= 527.1  MAE=  6.6
# ar.L1        0.560
# ma.L1       -0.970
# ma.S.L12    -0.672
# sigma2       76.240
#             forecast  lower units  upper units
# 2024-01-01     364.4        347.2        381.6
# 2024-06-01     463.3        441.6        485.0
# 2024-12-01     393.5        371.5        415.5
# share of actuals inside the 95% band: 1.0

Non-seasonal ARIMA is a disaster here (MAE 64, worse than naive) because it has no way to know about June. Add the seasonal part and it lands at 6.6, tied with seasonal naive plus drift. Two expert readings of the parameters: ar.L1 = 0.56 is the model finding the sticky AR(1) noise we built in, and ma.L1 ≈ −0.97, an MA coefficient near −1, is the classic signature of over-differencing: the trend in this series is a deterministic straight line, so first-differencing it was slightly too much and the MA term is undoing it. Harmless for the forecast, but it's why the interval barely widens across the horizon (≈ ±17 at h = 1, ±22 at h = 12); on a genuinely random-walk series the band would fan out much faster.

When ARIMA is the right tool

  • One or a few series, tens to a few hundred points, with clear seasonal structure and no rich external drivers: ARIMA/SARIMA and Holt-Winters are excellent, fast, and come with statistically grounded prediction intervals.
  • You need to explain the model to a statistician or an auditor: "AR(1) with seasonal MA" is a sentence; a boosted ensemble is not.
  • Not the right tool when you have hundreds of series, lots of exogenous features (price, promotions, weather), or intermittent counts with many zeros. That's Section 7's territory. (SARIMAX accepts exogenous regressors via exog=, which covers the middle ground of "a few known drivers".)
Common mistake

Fitting ARIMA on the raw series and reporting the in-sample fit. ARIMA's one-step-ahead fitted values always look great because each one uses the true previous value. The number that matters is the multi-step forecast error on a holdout. Always get_forecast(h) from a fixed origin and score against data the fit never saw.

7. The ML approach: lag features + boosting

Here is the idea that turned forecasting into a machine-learning problem and won most recent forecasting competitions (M5 was won by LightGBM ensembles): rewrite the series as a supervised table. Each row is one time step. The target is yt. The features are things you knew before t: previous values (lags), summaries of previous values (rolling statistics), the calendar (month, day of week, holidays), and external drivers (price, promotions, weather forecasts). Then any regressor from Chapters 5 to 10 can be trained on it. Gradient boosting (Chapter 10) is the usual winner because it handles nonlinearity, interactions and mixed feature types without fuss.

# Lag-feature table + gradient boosting, backtested with TimeSeriesSplit (expanding window)
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import TimeSeriesSplit
rng = np.random.default_rng(42); idx = pd.date_range("2017-01-01", periods=96, freq="MS"); t = np.arange(96)
e = rng.normal(0, 10, size=96); noise = np.zeros(96)
for i in range(1, 96): noise[i] = 0.6 * noise[i - 1] + e[i]
y = pd.Series(200 + 2.5*t + 40*np.sin(2*np.pi*(t - 2)/12) + noise, index=idx, name="units")
def mae(a, f): return float(np.mean(np.abs(np.asarray(a) - np.asarray(f))))

def make_features(s):
    df = pd.DataFrame({"y": s})
    for lag in (1, 2, 3, 12):
        df[f"lag_{lag}"] = df["y"].shift(lag)              # only the PAST
    df["roll_mean_3"]  = df["y"].shift(1).rolling(3).mean()  # shift(1) first: no peeking
    df["roll_mean_12"] = df["y"].shift(1).rolling(12).mean()
    df["month"] = df.index.month
    df["y_minus_lag12"] = df["y"] - df["lag_12"]           # de-trended target
    return df.dropna()
feat = make_features(y)
cols = ["lag_1", "lag_2", "lag_3", "lag_12", "roll_mean_3", "roll_mean_12", "month"]
print(feat[cols + ["y"]].head(2).round(1).to_string())
tscv = TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5, test_size=12)         # expanding window, 12-month test folds
for target in ("y", "y_minus_lag12"):
    scores = []
    for tr, te in tscv.split(feat):
        gbm = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(max_iter=300, learning_rate=0.05, max_depth=3, random_state=0)
        gbm.fit(feat.iloc[tr][cols], feat.iloc[tr][target])
        pred = gbm.predict(feat.iloc[te][cols])
        if target == "y_minus_lag12":
            pred = pred + feat.iloc[te]["lag_12"].values     # add the anchor back
        scores.append(mae(feat.iloc[te]["y"], pred))
    print(f"target={target:14s} fold MAEs={[round(s,1) for s in scores]}  mean={np.mean(scores):.1f}")

# 2018-01-01  196.2  191.8  190.3   165.4        192.7         208.5      1  201.2
# 2018-02-01  201.2  196.2  191.8   172.1        196.4         211.5      2  227.3
# target=y              fold MAEs=[43.8, 56.5, 36.2, 35.1, 32.3]  mean=40.8
# target=y_minus_lag12  fold MAEs=[9.8, 11.8, 14.4, 8.0, 5.7]  mean=9.9

Look at that first line: boosting on the raw target scores 40.8, worse than seasonal naive. This is the single most common failure of ML forecasting on a growing series, and you need to understand it cold. Trees cannot extrapolate. A tree's prediction is always an average of training targets in some leaf. If every training value is below 430 and next June's true value is 470, the model can at best say "about 430". Linear models and ARIMA extend a trend; trees flatten at the edge of what they have seen. The cure is to make the target stationary: predict the change from the same month last year (y − lag_12), let the trees model that, then add lag_12 back. Mean MAE drops to 9.9, and on the last fold, our holdout, to 5.7, the best of anything so far. Alternatives that do the same job: predict y − lag_1, predict y / rolling_mean_12, or fit a linear trend first and boost the residuals.

Key idea

Give the tree a target that lives in a stable range. Growth, inflation, and expanding user bases all push tomorrow's values outside yesterday's range. Differences, ratios and detrended residuals live in a range the model has seen. This one transform separates the forecasting projects that work from the ones that quietly under-forecast every peak.

8. Multi-step forecasts and honest backtesting

Forecasting more than one step

A lag-feature model naturally predicts one step ahead: to predict February you need January's actual value as lag_1. Procurement wants twelve months. Three strategies exist and you must consciously pick one.

Backtesting: the only evaluation that counts

A single train/test split gives you one number from one forecast origin. Was December 2023 a lucky origin? You can't tell. Backtesting (also called rolling-origin evaluation or time-series cross-validation) repeats the experiment from several origins and averages, exactly mirroring how the model will be used: refit, forecast h steps, move forward, repeat.

Three parameters matter and each maps to something operational:

  • Horizon (test_size). Make it the horizon the business needs. A 1-step backtest tells you nothing about 12-step accuracy; error typically grows with h, sometimes sharply.
  • Gap. If sales data arrives with a 2-week lag and you forecast on the 1st of the month, the most recent lag you actually have is older than lag_1. Set gap= to that delay (in steps) or your features will use data that won't exist at forecast time.
  • Window. Expanding uses everything; sliding (max_train_size) uses the last N steps only. If the business changed (new pricing, new channel, pandemic), sliding often wins. Try both; the backtest will tell you.
Common mistake

Tuning hyperparameters with GridSearchCV and its default KFold. It shuffles. Always pass cv=TimeSeriesSplit(...) to any sklearn search on time-series data, and remember that even then the fold scores are correlated (each fold shares most of its training data with the next), so don't read the standard deviation across folds as if they were independent.

9. Metrics and prediction intervals

Which error to report

The math: forecast metrics
MAE = (1/h) ∑ |yt − ŷt|      RMSE = √[(1/h) ∑ (yt − ŷt)2]
MAPE = (100/h) ∑ |yt − ŷt| / |yt|      sMAPE = (100/h) ∑ 2|yt − ŷt| / (|yt| + |ŷt|)
MASE = MAE / [ (1/(n−m)) ∑t=m+1n |yt − yt−m| ]

Sums run over the h forecast steps; yt is the actual, ŷt the forecast. MAE is the average miss in the series' own units. RMSE squares before averaging, so it punishes big misses more and is pulled up by outliers. MAPE divides each miss by the actual: a percentage, easy to communicate, but undefined when yt = 0, explodes when yt is tiny, and penalises over-forecasts more than under-forecasts (an error of 10 on an actual of 50 is 20%; the same error on an actual of 30 is 33%). sMAPE divides by the average of actual and forecast, which bounds it at 200% and treats over/under more symmetrically, but it is still unstable near zero. MASE divides MAE by the in-sample MAE of the seasonal naive forecast (m = 12 for monthly, use m = 1 for the plain naive): MASE < 1 means you beat the naive benchmark, and it is scale-free, so you can average it across products with very different volumes.

MetricUse it when…Avoid it when…
MAEYou want the plain answer "how many units are we typically off by". Optimising it targets the median.Series have wildly different scales and you need one number across them.
RMSEBig misses are disproportionately costly (stock-outs, capacity). Optimising it targets the mean.Data has spikes you can't predict; a few outliers will dominate.
MAPEExecutives want a percentage and every actual is comfortably above zero (revenue, traffic).Any actual can be zero or near zero: intermittent demand, new products, daily counts.
sMAPEComparing to published benchmarks that use it (M3, M4 competitions).Zeros are still common; also hard to explain to non-specialists.
MASEComparing across many series of different scales; reporting "× better than naive". The safest default for global models.Your audience needs units or percentages; translate it for them.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd, warnings; warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")
from statsmodels.tsa.holtwinters import ExponentialSmoothing
from statsmodels.tsa.statespace.sarimax import SARIMAX
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor
rng = np.random.default_rng(42); idx = pd.date_range("2017-01-01", periods=96, freq="MS"); t = np.arange(96)
e = rng.normal(0, 10, size=96); noise = np.zeros(96)
for i in range(1, 96): noise[i] = 0.6 * noise[i - 1] + e[i]
y = pd.Series(200 + 2.5*t + 40*np.sin(2*np.pi*(t - 2)/12) + noise, index=idx, name="units")
train, test = y[:-12], y[-12:]

def mae(a, f):   a, f = np.asarray(a), np.asarray(f); return np.mean(np.abs(a - f))
def rmse(a, f):  a, f = np.asarray(a), np.asarray(f); return np.sqrt(np.mean((a - f) ** 2))
def mape(a, f):  a, f = np.asarray(a), np.asarray(f); return 100 * np.mean(np.abs((a - f) / a))
def smape(a, f): a, f = np.asarray(a), np.asarray(f); return 100 * np.mean(2 * np.abs(f - a) / (np.abs(a) + np.abs(f)))
def mase(a, f, train, m=12):
    scale = np.mean(np.abs(train.values[m:] - train.values[:-m]))   # in-sample seasonal-naive MAE
    return mae(a, f) / scale

fc = {}
slope = (train.iloc[-1] - train.iloc[0]) / (len(train) - 1)
fc["seasonal naive"]         = train.iloc[-12:].values
fc["seasonal naive + drift"] = train.iloc[-12:].values + 12 * slope
fc["Holt-Winters"] = ExponentialSmoothing(train, trend="add", seasonal="add", seasonal_periods=12,
                                          initialization_method="estimated").fit().forecast(12).values
fc["SARIMA"] = SARIMAX(train, order=(1,1,1), seasonal_order=(0,1,1,12)).fit(disp=False).forecast(12).values
# GBM on de-trended target, recursive 12-step forecast
df = pd.DataFrame({"y": train})
for k in (1, 2, 3, 12): df[f"lag_{k}"] = df["y"].shift(k)
df["roll_mean_3"] = df["y"].shift(1).rolling(3).mean(); df["roll_mean_12"] = df["y"].shift(1).rolling(12).mean()
df["month"] = df.index.month; df = df.dropna()
cols = ["lag_1","lag_2","lag_3","lag_12","roll_mean_3","roll_mean_12","month"]
gbm = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(max_iter=300, learning_rate=0.05, max_depth=3, random_state=0)
gbm.fit(df[cols], df["y"] - df["lag_12"])
hist = train.copy()
for d in test.index:                                    # recursive: feed each forecast back in
    row = {f"lag_{k}": hist.iloc[-k] for k in (1, 2, 3, 12)}
    row.update(roll_mean_3=hist.iloc[-3:].mean(), roll_mean_12=hist.iloc[-12:].mean(), month=d.month)
    hist.loc[d] = gbm.predict(pd.DataFrame([row])[cols])[0] + row["lag_12"]
fc["GBM (lags, recursive)"] = hist[test.index].values

    print(f"{name:24s} {mae(test,f):6.1f} {rmse(test,f):6.1f} {mape(test,f):5.1f}% {smape(test,f):5.1f}% {mase(test,f,train):6.2f}")

Three lessons from the table. First, the ranking is consistent across metrics here, but it will not always be; when MAE and RMSE disagree, one method is making a few large misses. Second, the enhanced baseline is within a whisker of the best model. On a clean series like this one, that's expected: there is only so much signal, and the noise floor (residual std ≈ 7) caps everyone. The boosting model earns its keep on messier real data with promotions, price changes and multiple series, where its features can carry information the classical models cannot see. Third, this table is from one origin. Before you believe the 5.7 versus 6.5 ordering, run the five-fold backtest of Section 8 for every method. (The fold-by-fold GBM numbers were 9.8, 11.8, 14.4, 8.0, 5.7: the last fold was its easiest.)

Why point forecasts aren't enough

Procurement does not need "463 units in June". They need "somewhere between 440 and 485, and we'll size safety stock to the top of that range". A forecast without a range is a guess wearing a suit. Prediction intervals express how wrong you expect to be, and they must widen with the horizon, because uncertainty accumulates.

Where intervals come from, by method:

  • Statistical models (ARIMA, ETS): analytic, from the fitted noise variance; get_forecast(h).conf_int() or simulate(). Free, but only as good as the model's assumptions (Gaussian, constant variance).
  • Backtest residuals: collect the h-step errors from your rolling-origin backtest and use their empirical quantiles per horizon. Model-agnostic, honest, and it widens naturally with h. This is what I recommend for boosting models.
  • Quantile regression: HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss="quantile", quantile=0.9) trains a model that predicts the 90th percentile directly; fit one for 0.1 too and you have an 80% band. Scored with the pinball loss.
  • Conformal prediction: a modern wrapper that calibrates any model's intervals on a held-out block to guarantee coverage. Worth knowing the name; Chapter 21 touches on it.
At work

Always report coverage alongside accuracy: "the 80% band contained 78% of actuals over the last 24 months". A band that captures 100% is too wide (someone is over-ordering); one that captures 50% is theatre. And translate the band into the decision: stock to the upper quantile that matches the cost ratio of stock-outs versus overstock (the newsvendor rule), don't stock to the mean.

10. Leakage traps, global models, deep learning, anomalies

Leakage traps specific to time

Chapter 8 covered leakage in general. Time series adds its own species, and they are sneaky because the code looks reasonable.

rolling(…, center=True)

A centred window averages the 3 values after t with the 3 before. It's a great smoother for plotting and a catastrophic feature: it contains the future. Use trailing windows on the shifted series: y.shift(1).rolling(k).

Features derived from the target

"Average sales this month" as a feature for daily sales, "yearly total" as a feature for monthly. Any aggregate that includes the row's own y, or later rows, is leakage. Aggregate only over strictly earlier rows.

Scaling on the whole series

StandardScaler().fit(y) before splitting uses the test period's mean and variance. On a trending series the future mean is higher, and that alone tells the model "values will rise". Fit scalers on the training window inside each fold, as always.

Exogenous actuals

Training with actual temperature or actual ad spend, then forecasting with… actual values you won't have. Train with the forecast or planned version of every driver, or lag the driver enough that it's known.

Data revisions

Finance and public data get revised. The value you see for March today is not the value you'd have had in April. If revisions are large, backtest on the vintages that were actually available (a "point-in-time" dataset).

Dropping NaN rows after lagging, then shuffling

Even with lag features built correctly, a train_test_split without shuffle=False puts adjacent rows on both sides. Rows 40 and 41 share 11 of 12 lag values.

Global models: one model, many series

Real forecasting problems are rarely one series. They're 2,000 SKUs × 50 stores, or every customer's monthly usage. Fitting 100,000 SARIMAs is slow, and most of those series are short and noisy on their own. The modern answer is a global model: stack all series into one long table (Figure 14.7 with an extra column for series ID), add the ID and its attributes (category, store size, region) as features, and train one boosting model. The model learns "how a series with these lags and this month typically moves" from all series at once, and short series borrow strength from long ones. This is the M5-winning recipe. Practical notes: scale each series' target (divide by its own mean, or predict y − lag_12 as before) so a blockbuster and a niche product live in the same range; use the series ID as a categorical feature so the model can still learn per-series quirks; and evaluate with MASE so that big and small series count equally.

Hierarchical forecasting is the related problem of consistency: your SKU forecasts should sum to the category forecast, which should sum to the company total. Forecasting each level independently gives numbers that don't add up, which finance will notice. The standard fixes are bottom-up (forecast the leaves, sum), top-down (forecast the total, split by historical shares), or reconciliation (forecast every level, then adjust all of them by least squares so they cohere; the hierarchicalforecast library does this). Reconciled forecasts are usually more accurate at every level, not just consistent.

Deep learning for time series: an honest section

LSTMs (Chapter 18) were the first neural models to gain traction for sequences; N-BEATS and N-HiTS are pure-MLP architectures that won on M4-style benchmarks; the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) mixes attention with gating and handles static, known-future and observed covariates elegantly; and since 2023 foundation models for time series (TimesFM, Chronos, Moirai, TimeGPT) offer zero-shot forecasting: point them at a series they've never seen and they produce a reasonable forecast with no training at all.

When is this worth it? The evidence, including the M5 results and several careful 2023–2024 benchmark papers, says: deep models win when you have many related series with long histories (thousands of series, hundreds of points each), rich covariates, and the engineering budget to tune them. On a single monthly series with 96 points, they lose to Holt-Winters. Boosting on lag features is the sweet spot for most companies: nearly deep-learning accuracy on large panels, a fraction of the complexity, and it runs on a laptop. Foundation models are genuinely useful as a fast baseline for a new panel (zero-shot, no feature engineering) and for cold-start series with no history, but you should still backtest them against seasonal naive before trusting them, and most of them cannot use your exogenous features.

Anomaly detection on time series

Once you have a forecast with an interval, you have an anomaly detector: any actual that lands outside the 99% band is an anomaly. This "forecast-then-flag" approach is how most production monitoring works (traffic drops, sensor drift, fraud spikes). Its quality is the quality of your forecast: a model that doesn't know about Black Friday will flag Black Friday every year. Alternatives: robust decomposition (STL) followed by a threshold on the residual; isolation forests over windows of lag features (Chapter 22); and, for change-point detection (the level shifted permanently, rather than one point being odd), dedicated algorithms such as PELT or Bayesian online change-point detection. Set thresholds by the cost of a missed anomaly versus a false alarm, never by "3 sigma" reflex.

The forecasting project checklist

  1. Pin the decision. Who consumes the forecast, at what granularity (SKU-week? region-month?), how far ahead, how often is it refreshed, and what does an error cost in each direction? This decides horizon, metric and whether you need intervals. For demand: stock-out cost versus holding cost. For capacity: cost of idle servers versus outage. For finance: the board wants a range and a story, not a point.
  2. Assemble a point-in-time dataset. Reconstruct what was knowable at each forecast origin: data latency, revisions, planned promotions. This is where most of the effort goes.
  3. Plot everything. Decompose. Check the ACF. Look at outliers and level shifts (the pandemic months?). Decide additive vs multiplicative. Decide whether to model the whole history or only the recent regime.
  4. Compute the baselines with a rolling-origin backtest at the real horizon and gap. Write the seasonal-naive MASE on the wall.
  5. Fit the classical models (Holt-Winters, SARIMA). Often good enough; always a useful second opinion.
  6. Build the lag table with a detrended target and calendar/driver features. Boost. Check feature importance for leaks (a suspiciously perfect feature is a leak).
  7. Backtest all candidates identically, compare per horizon, and check interval coverage. Prefer the simplest model within noise of the best.
  8. Ship with monitoring. Log every forecast and its interval; compare with actuals as they arrive; alert when coverage or MASE drifts (Chapter 21). Retrain on a schedule matched to how fast the process changes.
At work

The forecast will be judged by people who remember the one month it was badly wrong. Pre-empt that: publish the backtest, publish the intervals, and publish the comparison with "what we'd have gotten by copying last year". A team that can say "our 12-month MASE is 0.2 against 0.9 for the old method, and 82% of actuals fell inside our 80% band" has an answer for every difficult meeting.

Quiz

Check your understanding

1. A colleague reports 98% R² on a demand-forecasting model evaluated with a shuffled 80/20 split. The most likely explanation is…

Shuffling gives each test month training neighbours on both sides (Figure 14.1). A chronological split with a rolling origin is the only honest evaluation.

2. The ACF of a series decays very slowly and has a bump at lag 7 on daily data. What should you do?

Slow decay = trend, so difference it. A bump at the seasonal lag = weekly seasonality, so add a seasonal difference or a lag-7 feature (or the day-of-week calendar feature in a GBM).

3. Your gradient-boosting forecaster fits the training years beautifully but under-forecasts every summer peak in the backtest by about 10%, and the business is growing 10% a year. The fix is…

Trees cannot predict outside the range of training targets. A stationary target (a difference or ratio) lives in a range the model has seen. Option B is leakage.

4. You forecast daily orders for 800 products, a third of which have many zero-order days. Which metric should you report across products?

MAPE divides by the actual and is undefined at zero. RMSE is dominated by the high-volume products. MASE scales each product's error by its own naive error, so products are comparable and zeros are fine.

5. Sales data arrives with a 3-week reporting delay, and you forecast on the 1st of each month for the next 6 months. The correct TimeSeriesSplit is…

Horizon = 6 (what the business needs), gap ≈ the data latency so the backtest never uses a lag you won't have in production. Shuffling and KFold are wrong for time series.

6. Your Holt model (trend, no season) forecasts a strongly seasonal monthly series and does worse than the naive forecast. Why?

A model lacking a component the data has will fake it with the components it does have. The December-to-January drop becomes a "trend" and gets extended for 12 months. Use Holt-Winters or seasonal naive.

Exercise 14.1 — Break the GBM, then fix it

In the lag-feature block of Section 7, change df["roll_mean_3"] = df["y"].shift(1).rolling(3).mean() to df["y"].rolling(3, center=True).mean() and re-run the backtest on the raw target. Show answer

With center=True the rolling mean for row t includes yt and yt+1: the feature contains the answer, and fold MAEs collapse to a few units even on the raw target. That would never be available at forecast time, so the score is meaningless. Restoring shift(1) brings the honest numbers back. The y − lag_1 target also removes the trend and typically backtests in the same neighbourhood as y − lag_12 (around 10 mean MAE); it relies more on the momentum features and less on the seasonal anchor, so it tends to be a little worse at the turning points (the June peak) and a little better right after a level shift. Try both on your own data; the backtest decides.

Exercise 14.2 — Backtest everything, per horizon

Extend the comparison table so that every method (both baselines, Holt-Winters, SARIMA, GBM) is evaluated with the same five expanding-window folds, and report MAE per horizon step (h = 1 … 12) averaged over folds. Show answer

Wrap each method in a function forecast(train, h), loop over TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5, test_size=12).split(y), collect a 5 × 12 matrix of absolute errors per method, and average over axis 0. On this series the recursive GBM's error grows with h because prediction errors are fed back as lags; SARIMA and seasonal naive + drift grow slowly because they use only actual history; Holt-Winters is roughly flat but higher. Damping shrinks the trend contribution at long horizons; on a series with a genuinely linear trend it slightly hurts at h = 12, on real series it usually helps. The point of the exercise: a single-origin table (Section 9) can reorder when you average over origins and horizons.

Chapter summary

  • Time series are ordered and autocorrelated: never shuffle, always split chronologically, and backtest from multiple origins with TimeSeriesSplit at the real horizon and gap.
  • Decompose first (trend, season, cycle, noise); the noise share is the floor on forecast accuracy. Difference or log-transform to reach stationarity; ADF is a sanity check, the plot and ACF are the diagnosis.
  • The ACF/PACF tell you which lags carry information: they choose your lag features and your ARIMA orders.
  • Baselines are the unit of progress: naive, seasonal naive, drift, moving average, and the surprisingly strong seasonal naive + drift. Report MASE against them.
  • Holt-Winters handles level, trend and season with three smoothing weights; SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)m models momentum, differencing and error-correction at two scales. Both give intervals for free. A model missing a component the data has will fake it badly.
  • The ML route: lag matrix (shift, then roll; never centre), calendar, holidays, known-in-advance drivers, then gradient boosting on a detrended target, because trees cannot extrapolate.
  • Multi-step: recursive (one model, compounding error), direct (one model per horizon), or multi-output. Choose consciously.
  • Metrics: MAE for units, RMSE when big misses hurt, MAPE only when actuals are far from zero, sMAPE for benchmarks, MASE across series. Ship prediction intervals and report their coverage.
  • Many series → one global GBM with the series ID as a feature; hierarchies → reconcile. Deep learning earns its cost only on large panels with rich covariates; foundation models are a quick zero-shot baseline.
  • A good forecast with an interval is also an anomaly detector. Monitor coverage and MASE after deployment.
