A running example threads through the chapter. You work for a retailer that sells a cold-brew coffee maker. You have eight years of monthly unit sales (96 numbers) and procurement needs a 12-month-ahead forecast every quarter, with a range they can plan stock against. Every method below is fitted to this series, tested on the same final 12 months, and compared in one table at the end. The series is synthetic so you can regenerate it exactly, but its shape (growth, a summer peak, sticky noise) is what real demand looks like.

1. What makes time series different

In every previous chapter the rows of your table were exchangeable: shuffle the customers, nothing changes. A time series is the opposite. Row 37 is March 2020 and row 38 is April 2020, and the whole point is that April depends on March. Three consequences follow, and each one breaks a habit you learned in Chapter 7.

  1. Order carries information. The most predictive feature of next month is usually this month. Lose the order and you lose the signal.
  2. Observations are autocorrelated. Neighbouring values are similar. Errors are too. So "n = 96 independent samples" is a fiction; your effective sample size is much smaller, and your confidence in any result should be correspondingly humbler.
  3. You cannot shuffle. A random train/test split puts June 2023 in the training set and May 2023 in the test set. The model then "predicts" May from a June it already saw. Scores look wonderful. They are a lie.
Explain it like I'm 9

Guessing the weather for a day in the middle of last week is easy if you're told what happened the day before and the day after: you just say "somewhere in between." That's the random split. Forecasting is guessing next Tuesday when all you know is up to today. Every test you run has to look like that, or you're grading yourself on the easy game and then playing the hard one for real.

Key idea

Time only flows one way, and your validation must too. Every feature must be computable from information available at the moment the forecast is made, and every test set must lie strictly after its training set. Almost every time-series failure at work is a violation of one of those two sentences.

2. The four components

Before modelling anything, take the series apart. Nearly every business series is a sum (or a product) of four things:

Trend

The slow, long-run direction. Our coffee maker sells about 2.5 more units every month than the month before: the business is growing.

Seasonality

A pattern that repeats with a fixed, known period: 12 for monthly, 7 for daily, 24 for hourly. Cold brew peaks in June and troughs in December, every year.

Cycles

Ups and downs with no fixed period: business cycles, product hype waves, multi-year commodity swings. Harder to model because you can't set a calendar to them. Often folded into "trend."

Noise (residual)

What's left. Ideally unpredictable and small. If it still shows structure (autocorrelation, a leftover season), your decomposition missed something.

Additive or multiplicative? If the seasonal swings grow as the level grows (a 10% summer bump, so bigger every year), the components multiply: y = T × S × R. Take the log and it becomes additive again. Rule of thumb: plot the series; if the "envelope" widens over time, use model="multiplicative" or log-transform first.

# Build the running example: 8 years of monthly demand with trend, season and sticky noise
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
idx = pd.date_range("2017-01-01", periods=96, freq="MS")      # 8 years of month-starts
t = np.arange(96)
e = rng.normal(0, 10, size=96); noise = np.zeros(96)
for i in range(1, 96):
    noise[i] = 0.6 * noise[i - 1] + e[i]                        # autocorrelated noise (AR(1))
y = pd.Series(200 + 2.5*t + 40*np.sin(2*np.pi*(t - 2)/12) + noise, index=idx, name="units")

print(y.head(3).round(1).to_string())
print("mean =", round(y.mean(), 1), " std =", round(y.std(), 1))
dec = seasonal_decompose(y, model="additive", period=12)
print("seasonal profile (Jan..Dec):", dec.seasonal[:12].round(0).astype(int).tolist())
tr = dec.trend.dropna()
print("trend slope/month ≈", round(np.polyfit(np.arange(len(tr)), tr.values, 1)[0], 2))
print("residual std ≈", round(dec.resid.std(), 1))
# 2017-01-01    165.4
# 2017-02-01    172.1
# 2017-03-01    206.3
# Freq: MS
# mean = 318.5  std = 74.5
# seasonal profile (Jan..Dec): [-39, -22, -1, 22, 37, 43, 37, 19, 0, -22, -34, -38]
# trend slope/month ≈ 2.43
# residual std ≈ 7.3

The decomposition recovered what we put in: a slope of about 2.5 per month, a ±40 seasonal swing peaking in June, and residual noise around 7 to 10. On real data you don't know the answer, so this is your first diagnostic: how much of the variance is trend, how much is season, how much is noise? The noise share is roughly the floor on how good any forecast can get.

3. Stationarity, differencing and autocorrelation

Stationarity

A series is stationary if its statistical behaviour does not depend on when you look: same mean, same variance, same autocorrelation structure in 2017 as in 2024. Classical models (ARIMA in particular) assume it, because a model can only learn a pattern that stays put. Our coffee series is obviously not stationary: it drifts upward and it has a seasonal wave.

Explain it like I'm 9

Imagine trying to learn the rules of a game while the rules keep changing. Stationary means the rules stay the same, so what you learned from last year still works this year. Differencing is a trick: instead of tracking your score (which keeps climbing), track how much your score changed since last turn. The changes usually follow stable rules even when the score doesn't.

Two transforms fix most non-stationarity:

  • Differencing removes trend: replace yt with Δyt = yt − yt−1. A straight-line trend becomes a constant. Seasonal differencing removes a repeating pattern: yt − yt−12 ("same month last year"). You can apply both.
  • Log transform stabilises variance: if swings scale with the level, log(y) has constant-size swings. It also turns multiplicative seasonality into additive, and growth into a linear trend. Forecast in log space, then exp() back (and remember that exp of a mean forecast is a median, not a mean; add half the variance if you need the mean).

The ADF test in one paragraph

The Augmented Dickey-Fuller test asks: "does this series behave like a random walk (a unit root, non-stationary) or does it get pulled back toward a level?" Its null hypothesis is non-stationary; a small p-value (say < 0.05) lets you reject that and treat the series as stationary. Use it as a sanity check, not a verdict: it has low power on short series, it cannot see seasonality, and a series can pass ADF while still having a seasonal pattern that wrecks your model. Plot first, test second.

Autocorrelation: ACF and PACF

The autocorrelation function (ACF) at lag k is the correlation between yt and yt−k: how much does a value resemble the value k steps earlier? The partial autocorrelation (PACF) at lag k is the same correlation after removing the effect of lags 1 through k−1: the "direct" link at that distance.

Explain it like I'm 9

ACF at lag 2 asks "is today like the day before yesterday?" But today is like yesterday, and yesterday is like the day before, so of course it is, a bit. PACF at lag 2 asks the sharper question: "once I already know yesterday, does the day before yesterday tell me anything extra?"

# Stationarity check and autocorrelations on the running example
import numpy as np, pandas as pd, warnings; warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller, acf, pacf
rng = np.random.default_rng(42); idx = pd.date_range("2017-01-01", periods=96, freq="MS"); t = np.arange(96)
e = rng.normal(0, 10, size=96); noise = np.zeros(96)
for i in range(1, 96): noise[i] = 0.6 * noise[i - 1] + e[i]
y = pd.Series(200 + 2.5*t + 40*np.sin(2*np.pi*(t - 2)/12) + noise, index=idx, name="units")

for name, s in [("raw", y), ("diff(1)", y.diff().dropna()), ("diff(1)+diff(12)", y.diff().diff(12).dropna())]:
    stat, p = adfuller(s, autolag="AIC")[:2]
    print(f"{name:18s} ADF={stat:6.2f}  p={p:.3f}  {'stationary' if p < 0.05 else 'NOT stationary'}")
a, pa = acf(y, nlags=24), pacf(y, nlags=24)
print("ACF  lags 1,2,3,6,12,24:", [round(float(a[k]),2) for k in (1,2,3,6,12,24)])
print("PACF lags 1,2,3,12    :", [round(float(pa[k]),2) for k in (1,2,3,12)])
ad = acf(y.diff().dropna(), nlags=24)
print("ACF of diff(1) lags 1,6,12:", [round(float(ad[k]),2) for k in (1,6,12)])
# raw                ADF= -1.46  p=0.555  NOT stationary
# diff(1)            ADF= -8.13  p=0.000  stationary
# diff(1)+diff(12)   ADF= -4.80  p=0.000  stationary
# ACF  lags 1,2,3,6,12,24: [0.95, 0.86, 0.75, 0.52, 0.64, 0.34]
# PACF lags 1,2,3,12    : [0.96, -0.46, -0.06, -0.16]
# ACF of diff(1) lags 1,6,12: [0.64, -0.72, 0.68]
Key idea

The ACF is your feature-selection tool for time series. Big bars at lags 1, 2 and 12 are telling you exactly which lag features to build in Section 7 (lag_1, lag_2, lag_12) and which ARIMA orders to try in Section 6. Look at it before you model anything.

4. Baselines you must beat

Forecasting has a humbling secret: dumb methods are strong. In the M-competitions (the field's benchmark series, run since 1982), simple methods routinely beat sophisticated ones, and in industry a large share of "ML forecasting" projects never beat seasonal naive. So before any model, compute these four. They cost nothing and they are your units of progress: a model that is 30% better than seasonal naive is a result; a model with "MAE = 12.3" is a number without meaning.

The math: the four baselines
Naive: ŷT+h = yT     Seasonal naive: ŷT+h = yT+h−m     Drift: ŷT+h = yT + h · (yT − y1)/(T − 1)     Moving average: ŷT+h = (1/k) ∑i=0k−1 yT−i

T is the last observed time (the forecast origin); h is how many steps ahead (the horizon); m is the seasonal period (12 for monthly); k is the moving-average window. Naive repeats the last value forever. Seasonal naive repeats "the same month last year". Drift draws a straight line from the first to the last observation and extends it. Moving average predicts the recent mean. Combine them: seasonal naive + drift repeats last year's pattern shifted up by a year's worth of trend, and it is a formidable opponent.

# The baselines every forecast must beat, scored on the final 12 months
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
rng = np.random.default_rng(42); idx = pd.date_range("2017-01-01", periods=96, freq="MS"); t = np.arange(96)
e = rng.normal(0, 10, size=96); noise = np.zeros(96)
for i in range(1, 96): noise[i] = 0.6 * noise[i - 1] + e[i]
y = pd.Series(200 + 2.5*t + 40*np.sin(2*np.pi*(t - 2)/12) + noise, index=idx, name="units")

train, test = y[:-12], y[-12:]                    # chronological split, never shuffled
h = len(test)
def mae(actual, forecast): return float(np.mean(np.abs(np.asarray(actual) - np.asarray(forecast))))
slope = (train.iloc[-1] - train.iloc[0]) / (len(train) - 1)
baselines = {
  "naive (last value)":        np.repeat(train.iloc[-1], h),
  "moving average (12)":       np.repeat(train.iloc[-12:].mean(), h),
  "drift":                     train.iloc[-1] + slope * np.arange(1, h + 1),
  "seasonal naive":            train.iloc[-12:].values,
  "seasonal naive + drift":    train.iloc[-12:].values + 12 * slope,
}
for name, fc in baselines.items():
    print(f"{name:24s} MAE = {mae(test, fc):5.1f}")
# naive (last value)       MAE =  59.1
# moving average (12)      MAE =  38.4
# drift                    MAE =  47.4
# seasonal naive           MAE =  27.7
# seasonal naive + drift   MAE =   6.5
At work

Put the seasonal-naive number on the first slide of every forecasting review. Stakeholders anchor on it instantly ("we'd be within 28 units just by copying last year"), and every improvement you report afterwards has a denominator. If your fancy model loses to seasonal naive on a fair backtest, which happens more often than anyone admits, ship seasonal naive and go find better features. That is not a failure; it is the cheapest possible win.

5. Exponential smoothing

A moving average treats the last k points equally and everything older as worthless. That's crude. Exponential smoothing says: the most recent value matters most, the one before a bit less, and so on, with weights that decay geometrically. One parameter, α (alpha, between 0 and 1), controls how fast they decay.

Explain it like I'm 9

You're guessing how long your walk to school takes. Each morning you update your guess: "new guess = mostly my old guess, plus a little bit of today's actual time." If α is big you trust today a lot and your guess jumps around; if α is tiny you barely budge and your guess lags behind when the road works start. Smoothing is choosing how much to trust today.

The math: the smoothing family
Simple: ℓt = α yt + (1 − α) ℓt−1,   ŷt+h = ℓt
Holt (add trend): ℓt = α yt + (1 − α)(ℓt−1 + bt−1),   bt = β(ℓt − ℓt−1) + (1 − β) bt−1,   ŷt+h = ℓt + h bt
Holt-Winters (add season): ℓt = α(yt − st−m) + (1 − α)(ℓt−1 + bt−1),   st = γ(yt − ℓt) + (1 − γ) st−m,   ŷt+h = ℓt + h bt + st+h−m

ℓt is the smoothed level (where the series is now, with noise removed); bt is the smoothed slope (how much it moves per step); st is the seasonal offset for time t, one per position in the cycle of length m. α, β, γ (all in [0,1]) set how quickly level, slope and season adapt to new data: near 0 means "trust history", near 1 means "trust the latest point". statsmodels picks them by minimising in-sample squared error. Unrolling the simple version shows why it's called exponential: ℓt = α yt + α(1−α) yt−1 + α(1−α)2 yt−2 + …, weights that shrink by (1−α) per step.

# Simple, Holt, and Holt-Winters exponential smoothing (statsmodels)
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.holtwinters import SimpleExpSmoothing, Holt, ExponentialSmoothing
rng = np.random.default_rng(42); idx = pd.date_range("2017-01-01", periods=96, freq="MS"); t = np.arange(96)
e = rng.normal(0, 10, size=96); noise = np.zeros(96)
for i in range(1, 96): noise[i] = 0.6 * noise[i - 1] + e[i]
y = pd.Series(200 + 2.5*t + 40*np.sin(2*np.pi*(t - 2)/12) + noise, index=idx, name="units")
train, test = y[:-12], y[-12:]
def mae(a, f): return float(np.mean(np.abs(np.asarray(a) - np.asarray(f))))

ses  = SimpleExpSmoothing(train, initialization_method="estimated").fit()
holt = Holt(train, initialization_method="estimated").fit()
hw   = ExponentialSmoothing(train, trend="add", seasonal="add", seasonal_periods=12,
                            initialization_method="estimated").fit()
p = hw.params
print(f"SES          alpha={ses.params['smoothing_level']:.2f}                     MAE={mae(test, ses.forecast(12)):5.1f}")
print(f"Holt         alpha={holt.params['smoothing_level']:.2f} beta={holt.params['smoothing_trend']:.2f}           MAE={mae(test, holt.forecast(12)):5.1f}")
print(f"Holt-Winters alpha={p['smoothing_level']:.2f} beta={p['smoothing_trend']:.2f} gamma={p['smoothing_seasonal']:.2f} MAE={mae(test, hw.forecast(12)):5.1f}")
print(hw.forecast(3).round(1).to_string())
# SES          alpha=1.00                     MAE= 59.1
# Holt         alpha=0.96 beta=0.93           MAE= 93.6
# Holt-Winters alpha=0.79 beta=0.00 gamma=0.00 MAE= 10.0
# 2024-01-01    364.0
# 2024-02-01    380.5
# 2024-03-01    406.0

Read those parameters like a detective. Simple smoothing chose α = 1: it collapsed into the naive forecast, because on a strongly seasonal series "the last value" is the best a level-only model can do. Holt is worse than naive: with β = 0.93 it treats the December-to-January seasonal drop as a trend and extrapolates it downhill for a year. Holt-Winters, the only one with a seasonal component, is the only one that works. A model that lacks a component the data has will find a wrong way to fake it. Options to note: damped_trend=True flattens the trend as h grows (safer for long horizons, and a reliable default in the M-competitions), and seasonal="mul" for growing seasonal swings.

Interactive: smoothing and the lag it buys you
Avg lag: exp ≈ – steps, MA = – steps
The grey series has a step change at month 40. Small α or big k gives a beautifully smooth line that takes ages to notice the step (look at how far behind it lags). Large α or k = 1 follows the step instantly but copies every wiggle of noise. That trade-off, responsiveness versus noise, is the whole art of smoothing, and the reason statsmodels fits α from data rather than letting you guess.

6. ARIMA and SARIMA

ARIMA is the classical statistician's forecasting model, and it is worth understanding even though you may end up using boosting more often, because its vocabulary (AR, MA, differencing, orders) is how forecasting people talk. The name lists its three parts.

AR(p): AutoRegressive

Today is a weighted sum of the last p values plus noise. "Momentum." yt = c + φ1yt−1 + … + φpyt−p + εt. Picked from the PACF cut-off.

I(d): Integrated

Difference the series d times first so it's stationary, then model the differences and undo the differencing at forecast time. d is usually 0 or 1, rarely 2.

MA(q): Moving Average

Today is a weighted sum of the last q forecast errors. "Correcting for recent surprises." yt = c + εt + θ1εt−1 + … + θqεt−q. Picked from the ACF cut-off.

Explain it like I'm 9

Guessing tomorrow's temperature: AR says "it'll be like the last few days" (momentum). MA says "I was surprised by how warm it was yesterday, so I'll nudge my guess up a bit" (learning from recent mistakes). The I part says "the weather keeps warming all spring, so let me predict the change from today rather than the temperature itself." ARIMA does all three with knobs p, d, q.

The math: SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)m
φp(B) ΦP(Bm) (1 − B)d (1 − Bm)D yt = θq(B) ΘQ(Bm) εt

B is the backshift operator: B yt = yt−1, so (1 − B) yt is one difference and (1 − Bm) yt is a seasonal difference. φp(B) is the AR polynomial of order p and θq(B) the MA polynomial of order q, acting on adjacent steps. ΦP, ΘQ are the same ideas acting at multiples of the season m (lags 12, 24, …). d, D count ordinary and seasonal differences. εt is white noise. The famous "airline model" is SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)12: difference once, seasonally difference once, then one MA term at each scale. It fits an astonishing number of monthly business series.

Choosing the orders

The classical route is Box-Jenkins: difference until stationary (that gives d and D), look at ACF/PACF of what remains for p and q, fit, check that the residuals look like white noise, repeat. The modern route is auto-ARIMA (the pmdarima package, or a small grid in plain statsmodels): try many (p,d,q)(P,D,Q) combinations, keep the one with the lowest AIC (a fit score penalised for parameter count), then check the residuals. On our series a grid over p,q ∈ {0,1,2} and P,Q ∈ {0,1} picks (0,1,1)(0,1,1)12, the airline model, with AIC 552.7 versus 554.7 for the (1,1,1)(0,1,1)12 we use below. The difference is negligible; both forecast almost identically.

# ARIMA vs SARIMA, plus a 95% prediction interval from the seasonal model
import numpy as np, pandas as pd, warnings; warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")
from statsmodels.tsa.statespace.sarimax import SARIMAX
rng = np.random.default_rng(42); idx = pd.date_range("2017-01-01", periods=96, freq="MS"); t = np.arange(96)
e = rng.normal(0, 10, size=96); noise = np.zeros(96)
for i in range(1, 96): noise[i] = 0.6 * noise[i - 1] + e[i]
y = pd.Series(200 + 2.5*t + 40*np.sin(2*np.pi*(t - 2)/12) + noise, index=idx, name="units")
train, test = y[:-12], y[-12:]
def mae(a, f): return float(np.mean(np.abs(np.asarray(a) - np.asarray(f))))

arima  = SARIMAX(train, order=(1, 1, 1)).fit(disp=False)
sarima = SARIMAX(train, order=(1, 1, 1), seasonal_order=(0, 1, 1, 12)).fit(disp=False)
print(f"ARIMA(1,1,1)             AIC={arima.aic:6.1f}  MAE={mae(test, arima.forecast(12)):5.1f}")
print(f"SARIMA(1,1,1)(0,1,1)[12] AIC={sarima.aic:6.1f}  MAE={mae(test, sarima.forecast(12)):5.1f}")
print(sarima.params.round(3).to_string())
fc = sarima.get_forecast(12)
band = fc.conf_int(alpha=0.05)                       # 95% prediction interval
out = pd.concat([fc.predicted_mean.rename("forecast"), band], axis=1).round(1)
print(out.iloc[[0, 5, 11]].to_string())
print("share of actuals inside the 95% band:", float(((test >= band.iloc[:,0]) & (test <= band.iloc[:,1])).mean()))
# ARIMA(1,1,1)             AIC= 676.9  MAE= 64.0
# SARIMA(1,1,1)(0,1,1)[12] AIC= 527.1  MAE=  6.6
# ar.L1        0.560
# ma.L1       -0.970
# ma.S.L12    -0.672
# sigma2       76.240
#             forecast  lower units  upper units
# 2024-01-01     364.4        347.2        381.6
# 2024-06-01     463.3        441.6        485.0
# 2024-12-01     393.5        371.5        415.5
# share of actuals inside the 95% band: 1.0

Non-seasonal ARIMA is a disaster here (MAE 64, worse than naive) because it has no way to know about June. Add the seasonal part and it lands at 6.6, tied with seasonal naive plus drift. Two expert readings of the parameters: ar.L1 = 0.56 is the model finding the sticky AR(1) noise we built in, and ma.L1 ≈ −0.97, an MA coefficient near −1, is the classic signature of over-differencing: the trend in this series is a deterministic straight line, so first-differencing it was slightly too much and the MA term is undoing it. Harmless for the forecast, but it's why the interval barely widens across the horizon (≈ ±17 at h = 1, ±22 at h = 12); on a genuinely random-walk series the band would fan out much faster.

When ARIMA is the right tool

  • One or a few series, tens to a few hundred points, with clear seasonal structure and no rich external drivers: ARIMA/SARIMA and Holt-Winters are excellent, fast, and come with statistically grounded prediction intervals.
  • You need to explain the model to a statistician or an auditor: "AR(1) with seasonal MA" is a sentence; a boosted ensemble is not.
  • Not the right tool when you have hundreds of series, lots of exogenous features (price, promotions, weather), or intermittent counts with many zeros. That's Section 7's territory. (SARIMAX accepts exogenous regressors via exog=, which covers the middle ground of "a few known drivers".)
Common mistake

Fitting ARIMA on the raw series and reporting the in-sample fit. ARIMA's one-step-ahead fitted values always look great because each one uses the true previous value. The number that matters is the multi-step forecast error on a holdout. Always get_forecast(h) from a fixed origin and score against data the fit never saw.
