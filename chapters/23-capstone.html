1. The project canvas

Every ML project that failed in my experience failed for a reason that was knowable on day one: the target did not match the decision, nobody had agreed what "good" meant, the data did not exist at prediction time, or nobody was going to act on the output. The project canvas is a one-page form that makes those reasons visible before you spend a month. Fill it in with the stakeholder in the room; the arguments it provokes are the point.

Explain it like I'm 9

Before you build a treehouse you draw a picture, decide which tree, check you have wood and nails, and agree with your friends what "done" looks like (a floor? a roof? a rope ladder?). Otherwise you spend all summer sawing and end up with something nobody wants to climb. The canvas is the drawing.

A filled example: churn at a subscription company

Key idea

The two hardest rows are target definition and decision, and they must agree. "Predict churn" is not a target; "cancels within 90 days of the scoring date, excluding card failures" is. "Reduce churn" is not a decision; "call these 1,000 people this month" is. A model that predicts the wrong target perfectly is worth less than a rule that predicts the right one badly.

Common mistake

Writing the canvas after the model. Teams that start in the notebook discover in month three that the "label" was computed from a field that only exists after the customer has left, that the prediction is needed on day 1 but the best features arrive on day 30, or that legal forbids the top feature. The canvas costs two hours. Fill it in first, and revise it when reality disagrees.

2. Phases, timeboxes and definitions of done

An ML project is a sequence of phases, each with a timebox (a deadline that forces a decision) and a definition of done (the artifact that lets you move on). The timeboxes below are for a typical tabular project with existing data; scale them, but keep the proportions. The most important property is that the baseline arrives in the first two weeks: it tells you whether the problem is easy, hard, or impossible before you have invested a quarter.

Interactive: project timeline planner
Baseline due: – Ship gate: –
Phases scale proportionally, but two things do not: scoping never drops below a week (the canvas takes real conversations) and the baseline never takes more than three days. Notice that in a 4-week project, production and monitoring are still a third of the time; teams that plan only the modeling half ship late by exactly that half.
  1. Scoping (1 week). Fill the canvas. Interview the decision owner and the data owner. Write the target definition as SQL. Done: canvas signed by the sponsor; success criterion is a number.
  2. Data audit (1 week). Pull the data, profile every column (Chapter 2), reconstruct the label, check volumes over time, list columns that could leak (Chapter 8). Done: a data report with row counts by month, missingness, the leakage list, and a go/no-go on data sufficiency.
  3. Baseline (2 days). Dummy model, current business rule, one linear model on the obvious features, all evaluated with the project metric on a proper split. Done: a table of three numbers. If the linear model does not beat the dummy meaningfully, stop and revisit the target or the data before building anything else.
  4. Iteration (2–4 weeks, hard cap). Feature engineering, model comparison, tuning, error analysis, one change at a time with an experiment log (Section 5). Done: a frozen pipeline, its cross-validated score, and a log that explains every decision.
  5. Validation (1 week). One look at the test set with confidence intervals, threshold chosen on validation data with the cost matrix, slice analysis (regions, plans, tenure buckets), fairness checks, explanations reviewed by the domain expert. Done: the model report (Section 8) and a ship/no-ship recommendation.
  6. Productionization (1–2 weeks). Package the pipeline, batch job or endpoint, input validation, shadow run against the current process, rollback plan (Chapter 21). Done: predictions flowing to the consumer, with an owner on call.
  7. Monitoring (ongoing). Drift, performance once labels arrive, business metric, alert thresholds and a retraining trigger. Done: never; it has a dashboard and a review cadence.
At work

Announce the timeboxes at the kickoff and put the kill gate in the calendar. A project that is allowed to iterate indefinitely will; a project with a "baseline review on the 14th" produces a baseline. Stakeholders respect a team that says "we will know by week three whether this is worth continuing" far more than one that promises a model in a quarter.

3. Which model when, which metric when

Every chapter in Parts II and III ended with a "which one at work" table. This section is the master version: one flowchart that routes any problem to a first and a second model, and one that routes it to a metric. The first model is always the one you can build and explain in a day; the second is the one that usually wins the leaderboard. Build both; the gap between them tells you how much the problem rewards complexity.

Key idea

Two metrics per project, never one: a ranking metric that is threshold-free (ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, RMSE) to compare models during iteration, and a decision metric (profit at the chosen threshold, cost-weighted error, precision at the review budget) to choose the operating point and to talk to the business. Tuning on the decision metric alone is noisy; reporting only the ranking metric is meaningless to everyone outside the team.

4. The worked project: customer churn in eight blocks

Everything above becomes concrete here. We build the churn project from the canvas in Section 1 on a synthetic dataset with named features, a couple of categorical and date columns, realistic missing values, and one deliberately leaky column. Every block is self-contained (re-reads the data, re-does the split with the same seed) so you can run any one of them alone; together they run in about a minute. The pattern (ColumnTransformer + Pipeline + cross-validation + one test-set look) is the pattern you will reuse for every tabular project.

Block 1 — Build the dataset and look at it

In real life this block is a SQL query and a week of profiling (Chapter 2). Here we generate 5,000 customers whose churn depends on six informative signals, then add a plan derived from charges, a region, two dates, some missing NPS scores, and the trap: cancellation_reason, which is only ever filled in after a customer has churned.

# Block 1 — build the synthetic churn dataset, save it, first EDA pass
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=5000, n_features=10, n_informative=6, n_redundant=2,
                           weights=[0.8, 0.2], flip_y=0.03, class_sep=0.9, random_state=42)
num_cols = ["tenure_months", "monthly_charges", "support_tickets", "logins_30d", "avg_session_min",
            "late_payments", "discount_pct", "products_held", "nps_score", "days_since_contact"]
df = pd.DataFrame(X, columns=num_cols)
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
# engineered categoricals / dates, some correlated with the target so they matter
df["plan"] = np.where(df["monthly_charges"] > 0.5, "premium", np.where(df["monthly_charges"] > -0.5, "standard", "basic"))
df["region"] = rng.choice(["north", "south", "east", "west"], size=len(df))
df["signup_date"] = pd.Timestamp("2022-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(0, 900, len(df)), unit="D")
df["last_login"] = df["signup_date"] + pd.to_timedelta(rng.integers(30, 900, len(df)), unit="D")
df["churned"] = y
# a leaky column that would not exist at prediction time
df["cancellation_reason"] = np.where(df["churned"] == 1, rng.choice(["price", "service", "moved"], len(df)), "n/a")
df.loc[rng.random(len(df)) < 0.05, "nps_score"] = np.nan   # some missing values, as in real life
df.to_csv("churn.csv", index=False)

print(df.shape)
print(df["churned"].value_counts(normalize=True).round(3).to_dict())
print(df.isna().sum()[df.isna().sum() > 0].to_dict())
print(df.groupby("plan")["churned"].mean().round(3).to_dict())
# (5000, 16)
# {0: 0.791, 1: 0.209}
# {'nps_score': 225}
# {'basic': 0.435, 'premium': 0.124, 'standard': 0.212}

Three facts to record in the data report: 21% positive rate (imbalanced but not extreme, so stratified splits and PR-AUC), 4.5% missing NPS (impute inside the pipeline, never before the split), and churn varies strongly by plan (basic 44%, premium 12%), a categorical feature that clearly belongs in the model.

Block 2 — Hunt for leakage

The cheapest leakage detector: fit a tiny model on each column alone and rank by cross-validated AUC. Anything that predicts the target almost perfectly by itself either is the target in disguise or is recorded after the fact.

# Block 2 — leakage check: a column that predicts the target too well is suspect
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.preprocessing import OrdinalEncoder

df = pd.read_csv("churn.csv", parse_dates=["signup_date", "last_login"])
y = df["churned"]
scores = {}
for col in df.columns.drop("churned"):
    x = df[[col]]
    if pd.api.types.is_datetime64_any_dtype(x[col]):
        x = (x[col].astype("int64") // 10**9).to_frame()        # seconds since epoch
    elif not pd.api.types.is_numeric_dtype(x[col]):
        x = pd.DataFrame(OrdinalEncoder().fit_transform(x))
    x = x.fillna(-999)
    scores[col] = cross_val_score(DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=3, random_state=0), x, y, cv=5, scoring="roc_auc").mean()
ranked = pd.Series(scores).sort_values(ascending=False)
print(ranked.round(3).head(6))
print("SUSPICIOUS (AUC > 0.95):", list(ranked[ranked > 0.95].index))
# cancellation_reason    1.000
# late_payments          0.781
# monthly_charges        0.705
# products_held          0.683
# plan                   0.682
# nps_score              0.643
# dtype: float64
# SUSPICIOUS (AUC > 0.95): ['cancellation_reason']

cancellation_reason scores a perfect 1.000: it is the label wearing a hat. It would have produced a spectacular model that is useless at prediction time, because on the scoring date no live customer has a cancellation reason yet. The next columns (late payments, charges) score 0.70–0.78, which is what a genuine strong signal looks like. Drop the leak and write down why in the log.

Common mistake

Leakage that is not a single column: a feature computed over a window that extends past the scoring date (logins in the "last 30 days" measured from today rather than from the scoring date), a row duplicated across train and test (the same customer in several months), or a target encoding fitted on the full data. The single-column check catches the first family only; for the rest, the discipline is "every feature is computed as of the scoring timestamp" and "split by customer, not by row" (Chapter 8).

Block 3 — Split once, and beat the dummies

Stratified 80/20 split with a fixed seed; the test set is now sealed until Block 7. Two baselines: the prior (predict the base rate for everyone) and a logistic regression on the numeric columns with median imputation and scaling in a pipeline.

# Block 3 — drop the leak, split once (stratified), fit two baselines
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.dummy import DummyClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score, average_precision_score

df = pd.read_csv("churn.csv").drop(columns=["cancellation_reason"])   # the leak goes first
num = ["tenure_months", "monthly_charges", "support_tickets", "logins_30d", "avg_session_min",
       "late_payments", "discount_pct", "products_held", "nps_score", "days_since_contact"]
X, y = df.drop(columns=["churned"]), df["churned"]
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=42)
print("train", X_tr.shape, "test", X_te.shape, "churn rate", round(y_tr.mean(), 3), round(y_te.mean(), 3))

dummy = DummyClassifier(strategy="prior").fit(X_tr[num], y_tr)
logreg = make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="median"), StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)).fit(X_tr[num], y_tr)
for name, m in [("dummy (prior)", dummy), ("logistic (numeric only)", logreg)]:
    p = m.predict_proba(X_te[num])[:, 1]
    print(f"{name:<24} AUC={roc_auc_score(y_te, p):.3f}  PR-AUC={average_precision_score(y_te, p):.3f}")
# train (4000, 14) test (1000, 14) churn rate 0.209 0.209
# dummy (prior)            AUC=0.500  PR-AUC=0.209
# logistic (numeric only)  AUC=0.775  PR-AUC=0.485

The dummy's PR-AUC equals the positive rate (0.209), which is the number every later PR-AUC must be compared against. The plain logistic model reaches 0.485, so the signal is real and the kill gate is passed. From here on, every model is reported as lift over these two lines.

Block 4 — Feature engineering in a ColumnTransformer, then compare three models

Numeric columns: impute and scale. Categoricals: one-hot with handle_unknown="ignore" so a new region does not crash production. Dates: a FunctionTransformer that turns two timestamps into account age and signup month. All of it lives inside the pipeline, so cross-validation fits the imputer and the encoder on each training fold only. Then the three candidates from Figure 23.2 by 5-fold stratified CV on the training set.

# Block 4 — feature engineering in a ColumnTransformer; compare 3 models by CV
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, StratifiedKFold, cross_validate
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, OneHotEncoder, FunctionTransformer
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier, GradientBoostingClassifier

df = pd.read_csv("churn.csv").drop(columns=["cancellation_reason"])
num = ["tenure_months", "monthly_charges", "support_tickets", "logins_30d", "avg_session_min",
       "late_payments", "discount_pct", "products_held", "nps_score", "days_since_contact"]
cat = ["plan", "region"]

def date_features(d):                       # dates → numbers the model can use
    d = d.apply(pd.to_datetime)
    return pd.DataFrame({"account_age_days": (d["last_login"] - d["signup_date"]).dt.days,
                         "signup_month": d["signup_date"].dt.month})
def date_feature_names(transformer, input_cols):   # a named function: lambdas cannot be pickled later
    return ["account_age_days", "signup_month"]

prep = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num",  Pipeline([("imp", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")), ("sc", StandardScaler())]), num),
    ("cat",  OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), cat),
    ("date", Pipeline([("fx", FunctionTransformer(date_features, feature_names_out=date_feature_names)), ("sc", StandardScaler())]), ["signup_date", "last_login"]),
])
X, y = df.drop(columns=["churned"]), df["churned"]
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=42)

models = {
    "logreg": LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000),
    "rf":     RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=100, min_samples_leaf=5, random_state=0, n_jobs=1),
    "gbm":    GradientBoostingClassifier(n_estimators=100, max_depth=3, random_state=0),
}
cv = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
print(f"{'model':<8}{'ROC-AUC':>14}{'PR-AUC':>14}{'fit s':>8}")
for name, m in models.items():
    r = cross_validate(Pipeline([("prep", prep), ("model", m)]), X_tr, y_tr, cv=cv,
                       scoring=["roc_auc", "average_precision"])
    print(f"{name:<8}{r['test_roc_auc'].mean():>8.3f} ±{r['test_roc_auc'].std():.3f}"
          f"{r['test_average_precision'].mean():>8.3f} ±{r['test_average_precision'].std():.3f}{r['fit_time'].mean():>8.1f}")
# model          ROC-AUC        PR-AUC   fit s
# logreg     0.812 ±0.020   0.515 ±0.030     0.0
# rf         0.907 ±0.015   0.789 ±0.027     0.5
# gbm        0.912 ±0.019   0.794 ±0.043     1.0

The engineered categoricals and dates lift logistic regression from 0.485 to 0.515 PR-AUC; the tree ensembles jump to about 0.79. That gap (0.28 of PR-AUC) says the signal is non-linear and interactive, which is typical of behavioural data. RF and GBM tie within noise (the ± is the fold standard deviation; with five folds, differences under one standard deviation are not real). We pick the GBM because it is faster to score and tunes better, and we would say exactly that in the log.

At work

We use scikit-learn's classic GradientBoostingClassifier here because it runs identically on every machine; on a table above ~50k rows switch to HistGradientBoostingClassifier or LightGBM (Chapter 10), which are 10–100× faster and support categorical features natively. The pipeline code around them does not change, which is the whole point of the pipeline pattern.

Block 5 — Tune the winner with a small random search

Random search, 6 configurations, 3 folds, scored on PR-AUC: 18 fits, about 20 seconds. This is deliberately modest. Tuning usually buys a few points; feature work usually buys more; and a huge search on a small dataset finds noise (Chapter 13). Note that the search space includes subsample, which regularizes, and that n_jobs is explicit so the run is reproducible on a shared machine.

# Block 5 — tune the winner with RandomizedSearchCV (6 configs, 3 folds, PR-AUC)
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from scipy.stats import randint, uniform
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, StratifiedKFold, RandomizedSearchCV
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, OneHotEncoder, FunctionTransformer
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingClassifier

df = pd.read_csv("churn.csv").drop(columns=["cancellation_reason"])
num = ["tenure_months", "monthly_charges", "support_tickets", "logins_30d", "avg_session_min",
       "late_payments", "discount_pct", "products_held", "nps_score", "days_since_contact"]
cat = ["plan", "region"]
def date_features(d):
    d = d.apply(pd.to_datetime)
    return pd.DataFrame({"account_age_days": (d["last_login"] - d["signup_date"]).dt.days,
                         "signup_month": d["signup_date"].dt.month})
def date_feature_names(transformer, input_cols):   # a named function: lambdas cannot be pickled later
    return ["account_age_days", "signup_month"]
prep = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num",  Pipeline([("imp", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")), ("sc", StandardScaler())]), num),
    ("cat",  OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), cat),
    ("date", Pipeline([("fx", FunctionTransformer(date_features, feature_names_out=date_feature_names)), ("sc", StandardScaler())]), ["signup_date", "last_login"]),
])
X, y = df.drop(columns=["churned"]), df["churned"]
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=42)

pipe = Pipeline([("prep", prep), ("model", GradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0))])
space = {"model__n_estimators": randint(60, 200), "model__max_depth": randint(2, 5),
         "model__learning_rate": uniform(0.03, 0.15), "model__subsample": uniform(0.6, 0.4)}
search = RandomizedSearchCV(pipe, space, n_iter=6, cv=StratifiedKFold(3, shuffle=True, random_state=0),
                            scoring="average_precision", random_state=0, n_jobs=1).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print("best PR-AUC (cv):", round(search.best_score_, 3))
print("best params:", {k.replace("model__", ""): round(float(v), 3) for k, v in search.best_params_.items()})
# best PR-AUC (cv): 0.811
# best params: {'learning_rate': 0.152, 'max_depth': 4.0, 'n_estimators': 148.0, 'subsample': 0.757}

Tuning lifts CV PR-AUC from 0.794 to 0.811, about two points, with a modest tree depth and a slightly aggressive learning rate. Write those parameters into the code as a named constant (next block) rather than re-running the search: the frozen model should be reproducible from the file alone.

Block 6 — Choose the threshold with the cost matrix, on out-of-fold predictions

A probability is not a decision. The retention team needs a list, and the length of the list is set by economics: an offer costs $20; a saved churner is worth $150; historically, an offer saves about 40% of the churners it reaches. That gives a profit for every threshold. We compute it on out-of-fold predictions from the training set (cross_val_predict) so the test set stays sealed and the probabilities are honest.

# Block 6 — choose the threshold with a cost matrix on OUT-OF-FOLD probabilities
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, StratifiedKFold, cross_val_predict
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, OneHotEncoder, FunctionTransformer
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingClassifier

df = pd.read_csv("churn.csv").drop(columns=["cancellation_reason"])
num = ["tenure_months", "monthly_charges", "support_tickets", "logins_30d", "avg_session_min",
       "late_payments", "discount_pct", "products_held", "nps_score", "days_since_contact"]
cat = ["plan", "region"]
def date_features(d):
    d = d.apply(pd.to_datetime)
    return pd.DataFrame({"account_age_days": (d["last_login"] - d["signup_date"]).dt.days,
                         "signup_month": d["signup_date"].dt.month})
def date_feature_names(transformer, input_cols):   # a named function: lambdas cannot be pickled later
    return ["account_age_days", "signup_month"]
prep = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num",  Pipeline([("imp", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")), ("sc", StandardScaler())]), num),
    ("cat",  OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), cat),
    ("date", Pipeline([("fx", FunctionTransformer(date_features, feature_names_out=date_feature_names)), ("sc", StandardScaler())]), ["signup_date", "last_login"]),
])
X, y = df.drop(columns=["churned"]), df["churned"]
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=42)
BEST = dict(n_estimators=148, max_depth=4, learning_rate=0.152, subsample=0.757)   # from Block 5
pipe = Pipeline([("prep", prep), ("model", GradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0, **BEST))])

p_oof = cross_val_predict(pipe, X_tr, y_tr, cv=StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0), method="predict_proba")[:, 1]

# business numbers: an offer costs $20; a saved churner is worth $150; the offer saves 40% of true churners
COST_OFFER, VALUE_SAVED, SAVE_RATE = 20, 150, 0.40
def profit(threshold):
    flagged = p_oof >= threshold
    tp = (flagged & (y_tr == 1)).sum()
    return SAVE_RATE * tp * VALUE_SAVED - flagged.sum() * COST_OFFER

grid = np.arange(0.05, 0.95, 0.05)
prof = np.array([profit(t) for t in grid])
best_t = grid[prof.argmax()]
print(f"{'thr':>5}{'flagged':>9}{'profit $':>10}")
for t, pr in zip(grid[1::3], prof[1::3]):
    print(f"{t:>5.2f}{(p_oof >= t).sum():>9}{pr:>10.0f}")
print(f"best threshold = {best_t:.2f}  (profit ${prof.max():.0f} per {len(y_tr)} customers)")
print(f"contact everyone: ${profit(0.0):.0f}   contact no one: $0")

The math (optional)

For a threshold θ, flag every customer whose out-of-fold probability p̂ ≥ θ. With TP(θ) true churners and FP(θ) non-churners among the flagged, the expected profit is

Profit(θ) = s · TP(θ) · V − ( TP(θ) + FP(θ) ) · C

s is the save rate (fraction of contacted churners the offer retains), V the value of a saved customer, C the cost of one contact. The threshold that maximizes it is θ* = argmaxθ Profit(θ). Equivalently, a contact is worth making when the customer's probability satisfies p̂ · s · V > C, i.e. p̂ > C / (s·V) = 20 / (0.4 × 150) = 0.33 for a perfectly calibrated model; the empirical optimum (0.30) is close, which is a small calibration check in itself.

Contacting everyone loses $29,780 per 4,000 customers; contacting nobody makes $0; the model at threshold 0.30 makes about $20,900. The profit curve is flat between 0.25 and 0.45, which is good news: the decision is robust to the exact threshold. That flatness, and the three numbers, go on slide two (Section 6). The widget in Section 6 lets you move the business assumptions and watch the threshold move.

Block 7 — One look at the test set, with confidence intervals, then explain

Fit the frozen pipeline on the full training set, score the test set once, and bootstrap the test set (resample its rows 300 times) to get 95% confidence intervals for every reported number. Then two views of what drives predictions: permutation importance on the raw columns through the full pipeline (which respects the imputation and encoding), and mean |SHAP| on the transformed features (Chapter 20).

])

The math (optional)

The bootstrap estimates how much a metric would vary if we had drawn a different test set of the same size. Resample the n test rows with replacement B times; for each resample b compute the metric mb on the fixed predictions; report the percentile interval:

CI95% = [ percentile2.5(m1, …, mB), percentile97.5(m1, …, mB) ]

n is the test-set size (1,000 here, with 209 positives), B the number of resamples (300 is enough for a rough interval; 1,000+ for a report). The model is not refit; only the evaluation rows are resampled, so this captures evaluation uncertainty, not training variance. For rare positives the interval is driven by the number of positives, which is why 209 positives give ± 4–5 points of PR-AUC.

Test PR-AUC is 0.834 with a CI of roughly [0.79, 0.88]; the CV estimate (0.811) sits inside it, so nothing was over-tuned. At the 0.30 threshold, precision 0.72 and recall 0.81: of the customers we contact, 72% would have churned, and we reach 81% of all churners. Both views of importance agree on the drivers (late payments, NPS, discount, session time, tenure), which is what the call script needs. The widths of the intervals are the honest part: with 209 positives in the test set, ± 4–5 points is the resolution we have, and a colleague's model that scores 0.845 is not better than this one.

Block 8 — Save the artifact and prove the reload

The deliverable is not a notebook; it is a file that turns a raw customer row into a decision, plus a metadata record of what it is. joblib serializes the whole pipeline (imputer statistics, encoder categories, trees). The JSON sidecar holds the threshold, the training snapshot, the library version and the feature list, everything a reviewer or a monitoring job needs (Chapter 21).

In production, custom functions like date_features live in an importable module, not in the training script, so the unpickler can find them.

5. The experiment log and "one change at a time"

Iteration without a log is a random walk with a good memory for wins and a bad memory for losses. The experiment log is a table with one row per run; it takes thirty seconds per run and saves days of "did we already try that?". The discipline that makes it useful is one change at a time: if a run changes the features and the model and the seed, and the score goes up, you have learned nothing you can reuse.

Explain it like I'm 9

In a science-fair experiment you change one thing at a time: more water or more sunlight, never both, or you cannot tell which one made the plant grow. The experiment log is the notebook where you write down each change and what happened, including the ones that made the plant droop. Especially those.

Three conventions make this table work. Every row names its parent (the run it modifies) so the Δ column is meaningful. The data snapshot column is a version, not a filename, so a result can be reproduced after the table is refreshed. And "no" rows stay in the table: the failed ideas are the most valuable part, because they stop the next person from repeating them. Row 7 is deliberately in the log; a jump of 0.2 in one step is not a discovery, it is an alarm.

At work

Keep the log in the repository next to the code, as a Markdown table or a CSV, and reference its run number in commit messages ("run 8: random search, frozen"). Tools like MLflow do this automatically once the team is bigger than two; for a first project, a table you actually fill in beats a tracking server you do not. When someone asks in six months why the threshold is 0.30, the answer is one grep away.

6. Communicating results in money

A model is adopted or ignored at a meeting, and the meeting is decided by whether the audience can see the decision in units they own. Nobody owns AUC. Everybody owns dollars, hours, customers and risk. The translation is mechanical once you have a threshold and a cost matrix, and it is the most valuable slide you will ever make.

StrategyContactedChurners reachedSavedRevenueCostProfit
Precision and recall come from Block 7; the rest are business inputs. Move offer cost up to $60 and watch "contact everyone" collapse while the model still profits; move save rate down to 15% and the model's profit goes negative too, which is the honest signal that no targeting can rescue an offer that does not work. This is the conversation the widget exists to start.

The five-slide structure

  1. The problem and the decision (1 slide). "21% of customers churn per year; the retention team contacts 1,000 per month by gut feel." One number, one decision, one owner.
  2. Baseline vs model, in business units (1 slide). Figure 23.7's bar chart: nobody / everyone / current rule / model, in dollars per month, with confidence intervals. Below it, in small type, the ML metrics for the technical reader.
  3. What drives the predictions (1 slide). Top five drivers in plain words ("customers with two or more late payments are 3× more likely to leave"), one partial-dependence or SHAP plot, and the sentence "these are associations the model uses, not causes" (Chapter 22).
  4. Risks and limitations (1 slide). Where the model is weak (new customers with < 2 months of history), what it cannot see, the fairness check result, how fast it will decay, and what happens if it is wrong (a wasted $20, not a denied loan).
  5. Rollout plan (1 slide). Shadow mode for a month, A/B test with a 10% holdout, the metric that decides, the owner, the retraining cadence, and the kill criterion. End with the ask: "approve the A/B test".

What not to show

Raw AUC or PR-AUC without translation ("0.83" means nothing to a director and invites the question "is that good?"). A ROC curve (it is a research plot). Hyperparameters. The list of models you tried (that goes in the appendix). Any number without a confidence interval, because the first challenge will be "how sure are you?" and "quite" is not an answer. And never a slide of code.

Explain it like I'm 9

If you tell your parents "my model has an AUC of 0.83" they will nod politely and change nothing. If you say "this list of 235 people will save us about $5,000 a month compared to what we do now, and here is what would make that number wrong", they will ask you to start on Monday. Same model; different sentence.

8. Handoff to production and the model card

Chapter 21 covers the engineering of deployment and monitoring; this section is the handoff checklist from the modeling side and the document that travels with the model. A model without a card is a liability the moment its author is on holiday.

Handoff checklist (before "done")

  • Pipeline artifact + metadata JSON committed and versioned (Block 8); reload test passes in a clean environment.
  • Inference code takes a raw row and returns score + decision + top reasons; no notebook dependencies.
  • Input validation: schema, ranges, category sets, missing-rate limits, with alerts.
  • Shadow run for at least one cycle: predictions logged next to the current process, compared.
  • Monitoring dashboard: score distribution, feature drift, business metric, label-based performance once labels arrive.
  • Retraining trigger defined (calendar or drift); retraining is a script, not a person.
  • Rollback: the previous artifact is one command away; the consumer knows what "model off" looks like.
  • Random holdout in production for the feedback-loop problem and for future uplift work.
  • Owner, on-call, and a runbook with the three most likely failures.

Model card template

  • Name / version / date / owner and the run number in the experiment log.
  • Intended use: the decision, the unit, the cadence; explicit non-uses ("not for credit decisions").
  • Training data: source systems, snapshot id, date range, row counts, exclusions, known gaps.
  • Target definition: the SQL, the window, the label delay.
  • Features: list with availability-at-scoring-time notes; excluded features and why (leaks, legal).
  • Model: algorithm, frozen hyperparameters, preprocessing, threshold and the cost matrix behind it.
  • Evaluation: CV and test metrics with CIs; slice metrics (region, plan, tenure); fairness checks; calibration.
  • Explanations: global drivers and how per-customer reasons are produced.
  • Limitations & risks: where it is weak, expected decay, feedback loop, misuse scenarios.
  • Operations: serving mode, latency, monitoring links, retraining policy, rollback, contacts.
At work

Write the model card while you build, not after; Blocks 1–8 each produce one section of it. A one-page card that exists beats a ten-page one that is promised. Reviewers, auditors and your future self all read the same three lines first: what it is for, what it was trained on, and how good it is with error bars.

9. Your 30-60-90-day plan

You do not become the ML person at a company by knowing the most algorithms. You become it by delivering one small thing that works, visibly, and then another. This plan assumes a day job and about five focused hours a week, and it uses this book as the reference shelf.

Days 1–30: one baseline, in public

  • Week 1. Pick the problem: recurring, decision-driving, with data you can already reach. Fill the canvas (Section 1) with the decision owner. Re-read Chapters 1 and 2.
  • Week 2. Data audit: profile every column, reconstruct the label, run the leakage check (Block 2). Write the one-page data report and send it to the owner. Most projects reveal their fate here.
  • Week 3. Baseline: dummy, business rule, logistic regression in a pipeline (Block 3). Report three numbers with the metric from Figure 23.3. Start the experiment log.
  • Week 4. One improvement (features or a GBM, Block 4), then the expected-value translation (Section 6). Present two slides to the owner: problem/decision and baseline-vs-model in money. Ask for the A/B test.

Days 31–60: ship it, and start the second

  • Week 5. Tune modestly (Block 5), pick the threshold with the cost matrix (Block 6), one test-set look with CIs (Block 7). Draft the model card.
  • Week 6. Package and shadow-run (Block 8, Chapter 21). Set up the monitoring dashboard even if it is a scheduled notebook that emails a chart.
  • Week 7. Launch the A/B test with a random holdout. Write the five slides. Present to the sponsor.
  • Week 8. Start the second canvas, ideally in a different family: an anomaly queue, a forecast, or a recommendation list (Chapters 14, 22). Reuse the pipeline skeleton.

Days 61–90: results, and a habit

  • Week 9. Read the first A/B results honestly (Chapter 22 rules: no peeking, one metric, CI). If it worked, write it up in money; if it did not, write up what you learned. Both are wins for credibility.
  • Week 10. Second project's baseline. Teach a 30-minute session to colleagues on the canvas and the leakage check; the people who explain a method own it.
  • Week 11. Retrain the first model on the new month of data; check drift; update the card. This is the unglamorous step that separates people who ship models from people who demo them.
  • Week 12. Write your own failure-mode entries (there will be new ones). Propose the next quarter: two projects, one experiment, one piece of infrastructure (a feature table, a holdout policy, a label pipeline).

Keeping the edge

Read papers selectively: one a week, chosen from the citations of tools you already use, and read the experiments section first; if the baselines are weak the result is weak. Kaggle is the best free source of tabular technique (feature engineering, validation schemes, ensembling); read winning solution write-ups rather than competing, unless competing is the only way you will practise. Read code: scikit-learn's source for the estimators you use, one library internals walkthrough per month; understanding what fit actually does is what makes you fast at debugging. Keep a personal failure log. The catalogue in Section 7 was built from one. Yours will be more useful to you than any course.

Key idea

Expertise at work is a portfolio of shipped, measured, documented decisions. The canvas makes the decision explicit, the timeboxes make the baseline early, the eight blocks make the model honest, the expected-value slide makes it adopted, and the card makes it survive you. Repeat quarterly.

Quiz

Check your understanding

1. Which pair of canvas fields must be checked against each other before any modeling?

A target that does not match the decision produces a perfect answer to the wrong question. "Cancels within 90 days of scoring" matches "who to call this month"; "ever cancelled" does not.

2. In Block 2, a column scores a cross-validated AUC of 1.000 on its own. The right response is…

Real signals score 0.6–0.8 alone. A perfect single column is the label in disguise (here, cancellation reason, recorded after churn).

3. Why is the decision threshold chosen on out-of-fold predictions rather than on the test set?

Every choice made using the test set (threshold, features, model) leaks into it and inflates the final number. Out-of-fold predictions give each training row a prediction from a model that never saw it.

4. Model A has test PR-AUC 0.834 with 95% CI [0.79, 0.88]; a colleague's model B scores 0.845 on the same test set. What can you conclude?

With ~200 positives the resolution is ± 4–5 points. Choose between A and B on other grounds (simplicity, speed, explainability) or collect more evaluation data.

5. Which slide belongs in the five-slide stakeholder deck?

The audience owns money, customers and risk, not AUC. Show the expected-value comparison and let them argue with the assumptions.

6. A retention model is launched and, from then on, only the customers it flags receive offers. What failure mode is being set up, and what prevents it?

Without a random holdout you never again observe what un-contacted high-risk customers do, so you cannot retrain honestly or measure uplift (Chapter 22).

Exercises

Exercise 23.1 — Change one assumption

In Block 6, the save rate is 40%. Re-run with save rates of 20% and 60% and record the best threshold and profit for each. Then set the offer cost to $60 with the original save rate. Which assumption moves the threshold more, and what does that tell you to validate first with the retention team?

Show answer

At a 20% save rate the profit per true positive halves ($30 vs $60), so the best threshold rises sharply (the model contacts only the surest churners) and total profit falls to a few thousand dollars; at 60% the threshold drops and profit roughly doubles. Raising the offer cost to $60 has a similar effect to halving the save rate. The threshold is most sensitive to the ratio (save rate × value) / cost, so the save rate, which is an empirical claim about the offer rather than a price, is the number to validate first, ideally with the A/B test's holdout.

Exercise 23.2 — Break the split

Modify Block 3 so that the split is a plain random split without stratify, then duplicate 10% of the rows before splitting (as happens when a customer appears in several monthly snapshots). Re-run Blocks 4 and 7. Explain what happens to the CV and test scores and why the duplicated version is dangerous even though its numbers look better.

Show answer

Without stratification the positive rate differs slightly between train and test, adding noise but no bias. Duplicating rows before the split places copies of the same customer on both sides: the models (especially the tree ensembles) partially memorize those customers, and both CV and test scores rise by a few points that will not exist in production. The fix is a group-aware split (GroupKFold, GroupShuffleSplit) keyed on customer id, and a time-based split when snapshots span months (Chapter 8).

Exercise 23.3 — Do it at work: the canvas and the kill gate

Take the problem you wrote down in Exercise 1.2 (or pick one now). Fill the blank canvas in Section 1 completely, including the success criterion as a number and the three baselines. Then schedule a 30-minute "baseline review" with the decision owner exactly three weeks from today and put the kill-gate question on the invitation: "If the linear model does not beat the current rule by X, we stop." Bring the data report and the three-number baseline table to that meeting.

Show answer

There is no single right answer; the deliverable is a signed canvas and a calendar invitation. A good canvas has a target that two analysts could compute identically, a decision with a named owner and a cadence, and a success criterion in business units. A good kill-gate question names the number that would make the project not worth continuing. Most people who do this exercise discover that either the data does not exist at scoring time or nobody will act on the prediction, which is the exercise working as intended.

Chapter summary

  • Fill the project canvas first: problem, decision, exact target, unit, metric with a money translation, baselines, constraints, data, success criterion, risks, owner.
  • Timebox the phases and put the baseline kill gate in week three; plan production and monitoring from the start.
  • Route by data type, size and interpretability: linear first, gradient boosting second on tables; pretrained models for images and text; seasonal naive then GBM for forecasts.
  • Two metrics per project: a threshold-free ranking metric to compare models and a decision metric to choose the operating point and talk to the business.
  • The eight blocks: EDA, single-column leakage check, sealed stratified split, dummy and linear baselines, ColumnTransformer pipeline and CV comparison, small random search, cost-matrix threshold on out-of-fold predictions, one test look with bootstrap CIs plus explanations, saved and reloaded artifact with metadata.
  • Keep an experiment log, one change per row, failures included; treat gains smaller than a fold standard deviation as noise.
  • Present in money: confusion matrix × cost matrix, compared with nobody / everyone / current rule, assumptions on the slide, five slides, no raw AUC.
  • Pre-mortem with the failure-mode catalogue; the fatal ones (wrong target, leakage, no baseline, no owner, nobody acts) are visible in week one.
  • Hand off with a checklist and a model card; keep a random holdout in production forever.
  • Become the ML person by shipping one measured, documented decision per quarter and teaching what you learned.
