1. Programs vs. learners

For seventy years, software worked one way: a human writes rules, the computer applies them to data, and out come answers. Want to flag spam? Write rules: "if the subject contains FREE MONEY, it's spam." This works until the spammers write "FR33 M0NEY", and then you write another rule, and another, forever.

Machine learning flips the arrows. You give the computer data and the answers (thousands of emails, each labeled spam or not), and it figures out the rules on its own. Those learned rules are the model.

Explain it like I'm 9

Imagine teaching a little brother to tell cats from dogs. You could try writing rules: "cats have pointy ears" (but so do some dogs), "dogs are bigger" (but not Chihuahuas). It never works. Instead, you just show him 100 pictures and say "cat, dog, cat, cat, dog…" Eventually he gets it, even though neither of you could write down the rule. That "getting it" from examples is machine learning.

Key idea

A model is a function learned from data. It takes an input (an email, an image, a customer record) and returns an output (spam/not spam, a price, a category). Everything in this book is about how to learn that function well and how to know whether it's any good.

2. The three families of ML

Every ML problem you will ever meet belongs to one of three families. The family is decided by what kind of feedback the data gives you.

Supervised learning — learning with an answer key

You have inputs and the correct outputs for each. The model learns the mapping. Examples: predicting house prices from square footage (you know past sale prices), detecting fraud from transaction details (you know which past ones were fraudulent). This is the workhorse of industry and the focus of Parts I and II.

Unsupervised learning — learning without an answer key

You have inputs but no labels. The model finds structure: clusters of similar customers, unusual transactions that don't look like the rest, or a compressed representation of the data. Chapter 12 covers this.

Reinforcement learning — learning by doing

An agent takes actions in an environment and receives rewards. Nobody hands it labels; it discovers good behavior by trial and error. This powers game-playing AIs and parts of modern LLM training. It is rare in typical business settings, so we cover it as a primer in Chapter 22.

At work

When someone brings you a problem, your first question is: "Do we have historical examples where we know the right answer?" If yes → supervised. If no, but we have lots of data → unsupervised (or go collect labels!). If the problem is sequential decision-making with feedback → reinforcement. Sorting the problem correctly in the first five minutes saves weeks.

Where ML sits: AI, data science, statistics

People use these words interchangeably in meetings. They are not the same, and knowing the difference makes you sound (and think) like a professional.

Explain it like I'm 9

AI is the wish: "make the computer smart." Machine learning is the trick that mostly makes the wish come true: let it learn from examples. Deep learning is one very powerful kind of that trick, using "brain-inspired" layers. Data science is the whole detective job: asking the question, finding clues in the data, and explaining who did it. Statistics is the detective's rulebook for not fooling yourself.

Key idea

Statistics asks "is this effect real?" Machine learning asks "does this prediction hold up on new data?" Both matter at work. A great practitioner uses ML to predict and statistics to avoid fooling themselves about how good the predictions are (Chapter 13).

3. Regression vs. classification

Within supervised learning, there are two sub-types, decided by what kind of answer you want:

Regression → a number

"How much?" "How many?" "When?"

House price, tomorrow's demand, time until a machine fails, a customer's lifetime value.

Classification → a category

"Which one?" "Yes or no?"

Spam or not, will this customer churn, which of 10 product categories, is this tumor malignant.

Common mistake

"Predict how many days until the customer churns" is regression. "Predict whether the customer churns within 30 days" is classification. Same business question, different framing, different algorithms, different metrics. Choose the framing that matches the decision someone will make with the prediction.

Deep dive: one question, two framings 6 min
Explain it like I'm 9

"When will the ice cream melt?" needs a stopwatch: the answer is a number. "Will it melt before we get home?" only needs to know how far home is: the answer is yes or no. Same ice cream, different question, different answer sheet. And notice that you chose where home is. Move home closer and more ice creams "survive"; move it further and more "melt".

Precisely. Let T be the number of days until a customer cancels. The regression framing predicts T itself. The classification framing picks a horizon H and predicts the label y = 1 if T ≤ H, else 0. Every horizon manufactures a different label column from the same raw data, and the consequence that nobody warns you about is that the class balance (and therefore the "predict the majority" baseline you must beat) is a choice you made, not a property of the customers:

# The same churn question framed at three horizons: the horizon defines the label AND the class balance
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
days_until_churn = rng.exponential(scale=200, size=10_000).astype(int) + 1   # regression target: a number
for horizon in (30, 90, 180):
    y = (days_until_churn <= horizon).astype(int)                             # classification target: yes/no
    print(f"churns within {horizon:3d} days: positive rate = {y.mean():5.1%}   "
          f"majority-class baseline accuracy = {max(y.mean(), 1 - y.mean()):.1%}")
# churns within  30 days: positive rate = 14.2%   majority-class baseline accuracy = 85.8%
# churns within  90 days: positive rate = 36.2%   majority-class baseline accuracy = 63.8%
# churns within 180 days: positive rate = 58.9%   majority-class baseline accuracy = 58.9%

Three practical consequences follow.

  1. The horizon must match the decision. If retention agents call customers a month before they leave, the useful label is "churns within 30 days". If finance plans headcount per quarter, it is "churns within 90 days". Pick H from the calendar of the decision, then read every accuracy number against the baseline that horizon implies (85.8% is the floor at 30 days above, 58.9% at 180 days).
  2. The regression framing has a hidden trap: censoring. "Days until churn" is only known for customers who have already churned. The loyal ones have no number yet, so a naive regression is trained only on leavers and learns what leavers look like, not who will leave. The classification framing avoids this cleanly, provided you keep only customers who were observed for at least H days after the prediction date (Chapter 2's "known when?" rule) and label the ones who stayed as 0.
  3. The framing fixes the metric. A number gets error in days (Chapter 5); a yes/no gets precision, recall and a threshold (Chapter 6). Decide the framing before the first model, because switching later means redoing every evaluation.
Key idea

Write the label as one sentence with a source and a window before any modelling: "churned = no successful payment in the 60 days after the renewal date, per the billing table." If you cannot write that sentence, you do not have a supervised-learning problem yet; you have a conversation to schedule.

Explain it like I'm 9

Think of a spreadsheet of your classmates. Each row is one classmate (an example). The columns "height", "shoe size", "favorite subject" are features. Common mistake

Confusing training score with real performance. A model can memorize its training examples and score 100% on them while being useless on new customers. The only score that counts is on data the model never saw. Chapter 7 is entirely about this.

5. The 7-step ML workflow

Here is a truth that no course tells you early enough: the algorithm is about 10% of the work. Real ML projects follow a loop, and most of the effort (and most of the failures) live in the steps around the model.

  1. Define the problem. What decision will the prediction drive? What is the target exactly? How will we measure success in business terms (money saved, hours freed)? What does "good enough" look like? Write this down.
  2. Get data. Where does it live? Is it labeled? Is there enough? Is it legally and ethically usable? Do the labels mean what we think they mean?
  3. Explore and clean. Look at distributions, missing values, outliers, duplicates, and weird encodings. You will find surprises every single time. (Chapter 2)
  4. Engineer features. Transform raw columns into signals the model can use: scale numbers, encode categories, build ratios and time-based features. (Chapter 8)
  5. Train models. Start with the simplest thing that could work (a baseline). Then try stronger models. (Chapters 5–19)
  6. Evaluate. Measure on data the model has never seen, with the metric that matches the business goal. Compare to the baseline. (Chapters 6, 7, 13)
  7. Deploy and monitor. Put the model where it makes decisions. Watch it, because the world changes and models rot. (Chapter 21)
At work: the baseline rule

Before any fancy model, build a baseline: predict the average (regression) or the most common class (classification), or use whatever rule the business uses today. Every model you build must beat it convincingly. A shocking number of "ML projects" fail to beat a one-line rule, and it's better to find out on day two than month six.

Deep dive: baselines that are hard to beat 7 min
Explain it like I'm 9

A bag holds 9 blue marbles and 1 red one. If you shout "blue!" every single time, you are right 90% of the time without looking. Anyone who claims their marble-guessing machine is "88% accurate" has built something worse than shouting. Before you admire any machine, ask what shouting would score.

Precisely. Call the fraction of positive examples p. Three no-skill strategies have accuracies you can write down in advance, and scikit-learn's DummyClassifier implements all of them so you never have to argue about the floor:

The math (optional)
most_frequent: max(p, 1−p) stratified: p² + (1−p)² uniform: 0.5

p is the positive rate in the training labels. most_frequent always predicts the bigger class, so it is right exactly as often as that class occurs. stratified guesses "positive" with probability p, independently of the features; it is right when it guesses positive on a positive (probability p·p) or negative on a negative ((1−p)·(1−p)). uniform flips a fair coin. With p = 0.103 these give 0.897, 0.815 and 0.5; the code reports 0.897, 0.806 and 0.503, the small differences being sampling noise on 1,500 test rows.

# Three dumb baselines on an imbalanced problem, plus a one-line business rule. Beat all four or go home.
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.dummy import DummyClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=5000, n_features=10, n_informative=4, weights=[0.9, 0.1], random_state=0)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, stratify=y, random_state=0)
print(f"positive rate = {y_tr.mean():.3f}")
for strategy in ("most_frequent", "stratified", "uniform"):
    dummy = DummyClassifier(strategy=strategy, random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
    print(f"{strategy:14s} accuracy = {dummy.score(X_te, y_te):.3f}")

# a rule a domain expert might propose: flag the top 10% of the single most correlated feature
corr = [abs(np.corrcoef(X_tr[:, j], y_tr)[0, 1]) for j in range(X.shape[1])]
j = int(np.argmax(corr)); cut = np.quantile(X_tr[:, j], 0.9)
rule_pred = (X_te[:, j] > cut).astype(int)
print(f"one-feature rule accuracy = {(rule_pred == y_te).mean():.3f}   (feature {j}, |corr| = {corr[j]:.2f})")
# positive rate = 0.103
# most_frequent  accuracy = 0.897
# stratified     accuracy = 0.806
# uniform        accuracy = 0.503
# one-feature rule accuracy = 0.850   (feature 1, |corr| = 0.21)

Now the uncomfortable line: the one-feature rule, the kind of thing a domain expert proposes in the first meeting, scores 0.850, below the majority baseline, even though it actually catches some positives while "always negative" catches none. That is not a flaw in the rule; it is a flaw in accuracy as a yardstick on imbalanced data, and Chapter 6 replaces it with precision and recall. The lesson for now is narrower: a baseline is a model, and it must be evaluated with the same metric, on the same test rows, as the candidate it is meant to floor.

For regression the same idea is DummyRegressor(strategy="mean") (or "median"): predicting the training mean for every row gives R² = 0 by construction (Chapter 5), which is why a negative R² on the test set means "worse than a constant".

At work

The first slide of every model review is a four-row table with identical columns: (1) majority class or mean, (2) the rule the business uses today, (3) the simplest model (logistic or linear regression), (4) the candidate. If row 4 does not beat row 2 by a margin that changes a decision, the honest recommendation is to keep row 2 and save the engineering budget. Rows 1 and 2 take ten minutes to build and prevent the most expensive mistake in applied ML: shipping something that is worse than doing nothing.

6. When NOT to use ML

The expert's superpower is knowing when to say no. ML is the right tool when all of these are true:

✅ Use ML when…

  • The rule is too complex or unknown to write by hand (recognizing images, ranking search results).
  • You have (or can get) lots of relevant historical data.
  • The pattern is reasonably stable over time.
  • Being wrong sometimes is acceptable, and you can measure it.
  • The prediction feeds a decision that has value.

❌ Don't use ML when…

  • A simple rule or lookup table works ("if balance < 0, send reminder").
  • You have a few dozen examples.
  • Every decision must be 100% explainable and auditable by law and a rule would do.
  • The thing you want to predict isn't in the data (no signal → no model).
  • Nobody will act on the prediction.

7. Try it: your first "model"

Let's make the idea of learning a rule from data physical. Below are 20 fruits with their weights: apples (blue) and oranges (orange). A one-number model, a threshold, says: "if weight > threshold, call it an orange." Drag the threshold and watch the accuracy. You are doing by hand what training does automatically: searching for the parameter that minimizes mistakes.

Interactive: find the best threshold
Accuracy: – Mistakes: –
Blue = apple, orange = orange. Filled with a red ring = misclassified by the current threshold. Try to reach the maximum accuracy, then notice that several thresholds tie. That ambiguity is real: data rarely pins the model down exactly.

Now the same idea in Python. This is a real (tiny) learning algorithm: try every threshold, keep the best.

# Your first learning algorithm: search for the threshold with the fewest mistakes
import numpy as np

weights = np.array([120, 135, 142, 150, 158, 163, 170, 176, 181, 190,
                    168, 185, 195, 205, 210, 218, 226, 235, 244, 252])
labels  = np.array([0]*10 + [1]*10)   # 0 = apple, 1 = orange

best_thr, best_acc = None, 0
for thr in range(100, 261):
    preds = (weights > thr).astype(int)        # the "model": 1 if heavier than thr
    acc = (preds == labels).mean()
    if acc > best_acc:
        best_thr, best_acc = thr, acc

print(f"best threshold = {best_thr} g, accuracy = {best_acc:.0%}")
# best threshold = 181 g, accuracy = 90%
Key idea

Every algorithm in this book is a more sophisticated version of exactly this loop: propose parameters → measure error → adjust → repeat. Linear regression adjusts a slope and intercept. A neural network adjusts millions of weights. The idea never changes.

Explain it like I'm 9

Training a model is like playing "hot or cold." The model guesses, you tell it how wrong it was (that's the loss), and it nudges its guess to be less wrong. Do that a million times very fast and you get a model.

Your first real model in 12 lines

Here is a preview of the exact pattern you will use for the rest of the book and at work. Don't worry about every line yet; Chapter 4 explains the library and Chapters 5–7 explain what's inside the model. The point is to see how little code separates you from a working classifier — and to see the baseline rule in action.

# The universal scikit-learn pattern: split → baseline → model → evaluate on unseen data
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.dummy import DummyClassifier
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)           # 569 tumors, 30 features, label: malignant/benign
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=0, stratify=y)

baseline = DummyClassifier(strategy="most_frequent").fit(X_tr, y_tr)   # always predicts the common class
model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)).fit(X_tr, y_tr)

print(f"baseline accuracy on unseen data: {baseline.score(X_te, y_te):.3f}")
print(f"model    accuracy on unseen data: {model.score(X_te, y_te):.3f}")
# baseline accuracy on unseen data: 0.629
# model    accuracy on unseen data: 0.958

Read it top to bottom:

  1. Load data. X is the feature table (569 rows × 30 columns), y is the label column. Exactly Figure 1.5.
  2. Split. A quarter of the rows are locked away as the test set. The model never sees them during training. stratify=y keeps the class mix the same in both halves.
  3. Baseline. The dumbest possible model: always predict the most common class. It scores 63% simply because 63% of tumors in this dataset are benign. Any real model must beat 63%, not 0%.
  4. Model. Scale the features, then fit a logistic regression (Chapter 6). The .fit() call is the "propose, measure, adjust, repeat" loop running automatically.
  5. Evaluate on unseen data. Both models are scored on the test rows only. 96% versus 63%: the model learned something real.
Explain it like I'm 9

Imagine studying for a test with a practice booklet (training data). If the teacher then gives you the exact same booklet as the exam, a perfect score proves nothing; you might have memorized it. So the teacher keeps some questions secret (test data) and only those count. That is why we split.

Common mistake

Peeking at the test set more than once. If you tweak the model, look at the test score, tweak again, look again — you have quietly turned the test set into training data and your final number is inflated. Use a separate validation set or cross-validation for tuning (Chapter 7) and touch the test set exactly once at the end.

At work

This 12-line skeleton is the first thing to build on any new project, before any meeting about "which advanced model to use." It answers the two questions that matter on day one: Is there signal at all? (does anything beat the baseline?) and How hard is this? (how far is the simple model from good enough?). It also produces the first honest number you can put in front of a stakeholder.

One more thing you can do right now: swap the model. Because every scikit-learn model shares the same fit/score interface, trying a random forest (Chapter 10) is a one-line change:

from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=0, stratify=y)

forest = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(f"random forest accuracy on unseen data: {forest.score(X_te, y_te):.3f}")
# random forest accuracy on unseen data: 0.944

Notice the forest did not beat the simpler model here. That happens constantly. "More powerful" does not mean "better on your data," and the only way to know is to measure honestly. That habit — measure, don't assume — is the whole discipline in one sentence.

Key idea

The scikit-learn pattern is always the same: split → fit(train) → score(test). Models are interchangeable parts. What changes between projects is the data work around them, the metric you choose, and how honestly you validate. That is what the next 23 chapters teach.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
days_until_churn = rng.integers(1, 365, size=8)          # a regression target: a number

print("classification target:", churns_within_30)
# regression target:     [310 232 187  99 113  15  28   7]
# classification target: [0 0 0 0 0 1 1 1]
Deep dive: why memorizing looks like learning 6 min
Explain it like I'm 9

A kid with a photographic memory gets 100% on the practice sheet by remembering "question 7 → answer C". Another kid understands the material and gets 96% on the practice sheet because she makes a couple of honest slips. On the real exam, with new questions, the first kid has nothing to remember from. The practice-sheet score told you which kid memorized, not which kid learned.

Precisely. Some algorithms score 100% on their training rows by construction, whatever the data. A 1-nearest-neighbour classifier predicts the label of the closest training row; asked about a training row, the closest row is itself, so it is always "right". An unpruned decision tree (Chapter 9) keeps splitting until every leaf contains rows of one class, so it too reproduces every training label. Their training accuracy is a property of the algorithm, not of your data, and it carries zero information. The same split as in Section 8 (25% test, random_state=0, stratified) shows what happens where it counts:

# A model that memorizes scores 100% on its training rows and gives the game away on the test rows
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=0, stratify=y)
models = {
    "1-nearest neighbour":  make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=1)),
    "unpruned tree":        DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0),
    "logistic regression":  make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)),
}
for name, m in models.items():
    m.fit(X_tr, y_tr)
    tr, te = m.score(X_tr, y_tr), m.score(X_te, y_te)
    print(f"{name:20s} train = {tr:.3f}   test = {te:.3f}   gap = {tr - te:+.3f}")
# 1-nearest neighbour  train = 1.000   test = 0.951   gap = +0.049
# unpruned tree        train = 1.000   test = 0.902   gap = +0.098
# logistic regression  train = 0.991   test = 0.958   gap = +0.033

Read the three columns. The two memorizers are perfect on training rows and lose 5 and 10 points on unseen rows; logistic regression, which cannot memorize 426 rows with 31 parameters, is imperfect on training rows and best on the test rows (the 0.958 is the same number you saw in Section 8). The gap column is the quantity to watch: it measures how much of the training score was memorization. Chapter 7 gives it a name (variance), a diagnosis (the learning curve) and a cure (regularization, more data, simpler models).

Common mistake

Comparing algorithms by training score, or reporting "our model is 100% accurate" because the notebook printed model.score(X_train, y_train). A perfect training score should make you more suspicious, not less. The reportable number is always the one on rows the model never saw, and even that number is only honest if you looked at it once (Chapter 7's test-set rule).

Quiz

Check your understanding

1. A hospital wants to predict how many days a patient will stay after surgery. Which type of problem is this?

"How many days" is a number, so this is regression. If the question were "will the stay exceed 7 days?", it would become classification.

2. You have 2 million customer records but no information about which ones are "good" or "bad." A manager asks you to "find groups of similar customers." This is…

No labels, and the goal is to discover structure (segments). That is unsupervised learning — clustering, specifically (Chapter 12).

3. What is the difference between a parameter and a hyperparameter?

The slope of a regression line is a parameter (learned). The maximum depth of a decision tree is a hyperparameter (chosen). Chapter 13 shows how to choose hyperparameters systematically.

4. Which step of the workflow do experienced practitioners say consumes the most time?

Data work is routinely 50–70% of a project. Modeling is a small slice. This is why Chapters 2 and 8 exist.

5. A colleague proposes an ML model to decide whether to send a reminder when an invoice is 30 days overdue. What should you say?

"If overdue > 30 days, send reminder" is a rule. The first box of Figure 1.7 exits here. Save ML for problems where the rule is unknown or too complex.

6. Your model scores 99% on the rows it was trained on and 71% on rows it has never seen. What is the most accurate description?

Only performance on unseen data counts. A large gap between training and test scores is the signature of overfitting (Chapter 7). The reportable number is 71%, and the next job is to close the gap.

The best accuracy drops (to about 82%). A single threshold on weight can only split the number line once; if a heavy apple and a light orange exist, at least those two are always wrong. To fix it you need more features (color, texture) or a model that can carve more complex boundaries.

Chapter summary

  • Traditional code: rules + data → answers. ML: data + answers → rules (a model).
  • Three families: supervised (labels), unsupervised (no labels), reinforcement (rewards). Business ML is mostly supervised.
  • Regression predicts a number; classification predicts a category. Frame to match the decision.
  • Features = X, label = y, model = learned function, loss = how wrong, generalization = performance on unseen data.
  • The 7-step loop: define → data → explore → features → train → evaluate → deploy/monitor. Data is most of the work.
  • Always build a baseline first. Always ask whether a simple rule would do.
  • All learning = propose parameters, measure error, adjust, repeat.
  • AI ⊃ ML ⊃ deep learning; data science uses ML to drive decisions; statistics keeps everyone honest.
  • The scikit-learn pattern is split → fit(train) → score(test); models are interchangeable parts, and "more powerful" is not "better" until measured.
