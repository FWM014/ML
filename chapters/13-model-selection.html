Tuning, Imbalance & Model Selection

You can now train a dozen kinds of model. This chapter is about the discipline that turns "I tried some things" into a defensible choice: searching hyperparameters without fooling yourself, handling the 2%-positive datasets that make up most business classification, producing probabilities you can put a dollar value on, and comparing models with honest error bars.

1. Parameters, hyperparameters and the search problem

Quick recap from Chapter 1. Parameters are the numbers training learns from data: the weights of a logistic regression, the split thresholds of a tree. Hyperparameters are the settings you fix before training: the regularisation strength C, the depth of a tree, the number of trees, the learning rate. Training minimises a loss over parameters; nothing minimises anything over hyperparameters unless you build the loop yourself. That loop is hyperparameter search, and it is the subject of the first half of this chapter.

The running example: you work on the risk team at a payments company. Of 400,000 transactions a month, about 2% are later charged back as fraud. A model scores each transaction; above some threshold the transaction is held for a human review that costs about $4; a missed fraud costs on average $120. You need to (a) pick and tune a model, (b) deal with the 2% imbalance, (c) trust the probabilities enough to compute expected costs, and (d) prove to a sceptical risk manager that the new model beats the old rule set. Every section below is a step in that project.

Explain it like I'm 9

Baking a cake: the recipe's ingredients get mixed by the oven, that is training. But the oven temperature and the baking time are dials you set before you start. Nobody tells you the right dial settings; you bake a few cakes at different settings and taste them. Hyperparameter search is baking a lot of small cakes in a smart order, and tasting them fairly (not just the top of the one that looks prettiest).

Why is it a problem? Three reasons. Each evaluation is expensive (a full cross-validated fit). The space is big: a gradient-boosting model has a dozen knobs, several continuous, and their effects interact. And evaluation is noisy: with 5-fold CV on 5,000 rows, two settings that differ by 0.003 in AUC are indistinguishable, so a search that "finds the best" is partly finding the luckiest.

Key idea

Tuning is a noisy optimisation of an expensive black box. Every good strategy in this chapter follows from that sentence: spend evaluations where they matter (random and Bayesian search), stop bad candidates early (halving), never trust the winning score as an estimate of future performance (nested CV), and never let the test set into the loop.

2. Search strategies: grid, random, halving, Bayesian

Grid search

GridSearchCV evaluates every combination of the values you list. For two hyperparameters with 4 and 3 values that is 12 fits × 5 folds = 60 model fits. It is exhaustive, reproducible, and embarrassingly parallel. It is also wasteful in a specific way that Figure 13.1 makes visible: in most models only one or two hyperparameters matter much, and a grid spends most of its budget re-testing the same few values of the important one.

Random search

RandomizedSearchCV draws each hyperparameter independently from a distribution you specify (uniform, log-uniform, a list) and tries n_iter combinations. With the same 9-trial budget, a random search tests 9 distinct values of every hyperparameter instead of 3, so if the learning rate is what matters, random search has explored it three times more finely. Bergstra and Bengio's 2012 paper made this point and it has held up: for more than two or three hyperparameters, random search beats grid search at equal budget nearly always. Use log-uniform distributions for anything that spans orders of magnitude (C, learning rate, gamma, alpha).

The grid uses the largest square lattice that fits the budget (leftover trials are wasted), so its distinct-x count grows as the square root of the budget while random search's grows linearly. Press re-draw a few times: random search occasionally gets unlucky too, which is why 20–60 trials, not 9, is the practical minimum.

Successive halving

Most bad configurations reveal themselves early. Successive halving (HalvingRandomSearchCV, HalvingGridSearchCV) starts many candidates on a small budget (a fraction of the rows, or a few boosting rounds), keeps the best third, triples their budget, and repeats until one survives. With factor=3 and 27 candidates you evaluate 27 cheap fits, then 9 medium, then 3 full-size, then 1: far cheaper than 27 full fits, and in practice nearly as good, because a configuration that is terrible on 500 rows is rarely great on 5,000. The catch is exactly that assumption: for models whose ranking changes with data size (heavily regularised versus flexible ones), early rounds can eliminate the eventual winner. Use a generous min_resources.

Bayesian optimisation

Grid, random and halving all ignore what earlier trials taught them. Bayesian optimisation uses the results so far to decide where to look next. It fits a cheap surrogate model of "score as a function of hyperparameters" (classically a Gaussian process; Optuna's default TPE uses kernel density estimates) and picks the next trial by an acquisition function that balances exploitation (where the surrogate predicts a high score) and exploration (where the surrogate is uncertain). Figure 13.3 shows one step.

Explain it like I'm 9

Finding the deepest spot in a lake with a weighted rope. Grid search drops the rope at every metre mark. Random search drops it at random spots. Bayesian search remembers every depth it measured, sketches a guess of the lake bottom, and drops the next rope where the sketch says "probably deep, and I'm not sure yet". After a few drops the sketch is good and it homes in.

In practice Bayesian methods win when each trial is expensive (minutes to hours: gradient boosting on millions of rows, neural networks) and you can afford 30–200 sequential trials. For cheap models with a small search space, random search with 30–60 trials is just as good and simpler to parallelise. The Optuna block in §4 shows the API; the library also gives you pruning (halving-style early stopping of bad trials), visualisation of which hyperparameters mattered, and a study you can resume.

At work: budget before strategy

Decide the compute budget first ("40 trials, each ≤ 3 minutes, done by lunch"), then choose the strategy that uses it best: ≤ 10 trials and ≤ 2 hyperparameters → a small grid; 20–100 trials → random or halving; expensive models with many hyperparameters → Optuna with pruning. Whatever you pick, log every trial (parameters, fold scores, time) to a table you keep. Half the value of a search is the record of what did not work.

Key idea

For boosted trees, the learning rate and the number of trees are one knob, not two. Fix the learning rate, let early stopping on a validation fold choose the number of trees, and tune everything else around that. Searching n_estimators on a grid is the most common waste of compute in tabular ML.

4. Doing it right: search inside CV, pipeline inside search, nested CV

The mechanics matter more than the strategy. Three rules, each protecting you from a specific leak.

  1. The search runs inside cross-validation. Every candidate is scored by k-fold CV on the training data (cv= argument). A candidate scored on the test set has already spent it.
  2. The pipeline goes inside the search, not around it. Pass a Pipeline as the estimator so the scaler, imputer, encoder and PCA are refit on each training fold. Fit a scaler on all the data first and every fold has seen the statistics of its own validation rows (Chapter 8). Hyperparameters of pipeline steps are addressed as step__param.
  3. Report an honest score with nested CV, then refit and test once. The winning CV score is optimistically biased: you picked the maximum of many noisy numbers. To estimate what "tune, then predict" really delivers, wrap the whole search in an outer CV (Figure 13.4). Then refit the search on all training data (sklearn does this by default: refit=True), and evaluate the refit model on the held-out test set exactly once.
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, StratifiedKFold, GridSearchCV, RandomizedSearchCV
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from scipy.stats import loguniform

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=42)

pipe = Pipeline([("scale", StandardScaler()), ("svm", SVC())])   # scaler is INSIDE -> refit per fold
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)

grid = GridSearchCV(pipe, {"svm__C": [0.1, 1, 10, 100], "svm__gamma": [1e-3, 1e-2, 1e-1]},
                    scoring="roc_auc", cv=cv, n_jobs=1).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print("grid   best:", grid.best_params_, "CV AUC = %.4f" % grid.best_score_, "| fits:", len(grid.cv_results_["params"]) * 5)

rand = RandomizedSearchCV(pipe, {"svm__C": loguniform(1e-2, 1e3), "svm__gamma": loguniform(1e-4, 1e0)},
                          n_iter=12, scoring="roc_auc", cv=cv, random_state=0, n_jobs=1).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print("random best:", {k: round(float(v), 4) for k, v in rand.best_params_.items()}, "CV AUC = %.4f" % rand.best_score_, "| fits:", 12 * 5)

# the ONE test-set evaluation, using the refit-on-all-training-data winner
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score
print("test AUC (grid winner):", round(roc_auc_score(y_te, grid.decision_function(X_te)), 4))
# grid   best: {'svm__C': 10, 'svm__gamma': 0.01} CV AUC = 0.9963 | fits: 60
# random best: {'svm__C': 10.3233, 'svm__gamma': 0.0151} CV AUC = 0.9961 | fits: 60
# test AUC (grid winner): 0.9977

Notice the numbers: the grid's best CV AUC (0.9963) and the one test-set AUC (0.9977) are close here because breast-cancer data is easy and the search is small. With 200 random trials on a noisy problem the gap grows, and only nested CV tells you how much. The next block shows halving and nested CV on the same data; note that the honest nested estimate (0.990 ± 0.009) is lower than the inner winners would claim and comes with a standard deviation you can quote.

import optuna
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, StratifiedKFold
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingClassifier

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)

def objective(trial):
    params = {
        "n_estimators":  trial.suggest_int("n_estimators", 50, 500),
        "learning_rate": trial.suggest_float("learning_rate", 1e-3, 0.3, log=True),
        "max_depth":     trial.suggest_int("max_depth", 2, 6),
        "subsample":     trial.suggest_float("subsample", 0.5, 1.0),
    }
    model = GradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0, **params)
    return cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=cv, scoring="roc_auc").mean()   # maximise

study = optuna.create_study(direction="maximize", sampler=optuna.samplers.TPESampler(seed=0))
study.optimize(objective, n_trials=40, timeout=600)      # stop after 40 trials or 10 minutes

print("best CV AUC:", round(study.best_value, 4))
print("best params:", study.best_params)
# best CV AUC: 0.995            <- typical; exact value depends on the trials drawn
# best params: {'n_estimators': 312, 'learning_rate': 0.062, 'max_depth': 3, 'subsample': 0.71}
Common mistake: tuning on the test set by accident

Symptoms: "I tried a few more settings and test AUC went from 0.91 to 0.93." If you looked at the test score more than once while changing anything (features, hyperparameters, the threshold, the random seed), the test set has become a validation set and its score is no longer an estimate of anything. The fix is procedural: hold out the test set at the start, do all selection with CV on the training data, and evaluate on the test set once, ideally in a separate script you run last. If you must iterate afterwards, you need a fresh test set (Chapter 7).

5. Imbalanced data in depth

Why accuracy lies

Two percent of transactions are fraud. A model that flags nothing is 98% accurate. A model that catches half the fraud with a 1:1 false-alarm rate is 98% accurate too. Accuracy cannot distinguish "useless" from "useful" when one class dominates, because it is dominated by the majority class by construction. The code in §5 below shows a real case: the always-zero model scores acc = 0.945 and a real logistic regression scores 0.950, a difference of half a point for going from nothing to something.

Explain it like I'm 9

A weather forecaster in the desert says "no rain" every single day and is right 99% of the time. Is she a good forecaster? No: she has never once predicted the thing anyone cares about. Accuracy rewards her anyway. You need a score that asks "of the rainy days, how many did you call?" (recall) and "of the days you called rain, how many rained?" (precision).

The math (optional): F1 and why the harmonic mean
F1 = 2 · P · R / (P + R) = 2TP / (2TP + FP + FN)

P is precision, R is recall, TP / FP / FN are true positives, false positives and false negatives. The harmonic mean punishes imbalance between P and R: precision 1.0 with recall 0.1 gives F1 = 0.18, not 0.55 as the arithmetic mean would. Note that true negatives do not appear anywhere in F1, which is exactly why it is immune to the majority class. The general Fβ = (1+β²)PR / (β²P + R) weights recall β times more than precision; F2 for fraud, F0.5 for spam filtering where false positives (lost real mail) hurt more.

Threshold moving: usually the best first move

A classifier outputs a score; predict applies the threshold 0.5 to it. On imbalanced data 0.5 is almost never the right threshold, and nothing about the model has to change to fix it. Compute predict_proba, then choose the threshold on a validation split that maximises F1, or hits the required precision, or minimises expected cost using the business numbers ($4 review vs $120 loss). Figure 13.5 shows what the threshold does on the PR curve: it slides you along a curve the model already defines.

Class weights

class_weight="balanced" (sklearn) or scale_pos_weight (XGBoost/LightGBM) multiplies the loss of each minority example by roughly (negatives / positives), so the model is penalised for ignoring them. It changes the fitted model, not just the threshold, which can help tree ensembles find splits that isolate rare positives. It also shifts the probabilities upward for the positive class, so the model becomes miscalibrated (§6) and the default 0.5 threshold becomes a different, not necessarily better, operating point. In the code below, weighting raised recall from 0.18 to 0.85 but pushed precision down to 0.22 and, interestingly, lowered the threshold-free PR-AUC; the tuned threshold on the plain model did better on F1. That pattern is common with linear models: weighting is mostly threshold moving in disguise, plus some distortion.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import (accuracy_score, f1_score, balanced_accuracy_score,
                             average_precision_score, precision_recall_curve, recall_score, precision_score)

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=20000, n_features=20, n_informative=6, weights=[0.95, 0.05],
                           class_sep=0.8, flip_y=0.01, random_state=0)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, stratify=y, random_state=0)
print("positive rate:", round(y.mean(), 3))

def report(name, model, thr=0.5):
    p = model.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]; pred = (p >= thr).astype(int)
    print(f"{name:<28} acc={accuracy_score(y_te, pred):.3f}  bal_acc={balanced_accuracy_score(y_te, pred):.3f} "
          f"prec={precision_score(y_te, pred):.3f} rec={recall_score(y_te, pred):.3f} F1={f1_score(y_te, pred):.3f} "
          f"PR-AUC={average_precision_score(y_te, p):.3f}")

base = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
report("plain LR @0.5", base)
report("always predict 0", type("Z", (), {"predict_proba": lambda self, X: np.c_[np.ones(len(X)), np.zeros(len(X))]})())

bal = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000, class_weight="balanced").fit(X_tr, y_tr)
report("class_weight=balanced @0.5", bal)

# threshold moving on the PLAIN model: pick the threshold maximising F1 on a validation split
X_fit, X_val, y_fit, y_val = train_test_split(X_tr, y_tr, test_size=0.25, stratify=y_tr, random_state=1)
m = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000).fit(X_fit, y_fit)
prec, rec, thr = precision_recall_curve(y_val, m.predict_proba(X_val)[:, 1])
f1 = 2 * prec[:-1] * rec[:-1] / np.clip(prec[:-1] + rec[:-1], 1e-9, None)
best_thr = thr[np.argmax(f1)]
print("best threshold on validation: %.3f" % best_thr)
report(f"plain LR @{best_thr:.2f}", m, thr=best_thr)

Resampling: random, SMOTE, and the caveats

Random under-sampling throws away majority rows until classes balance; fast, and fine when you have millions of negatives, but it discards information. Random over-sampling duplicates minority rows; cheap, but trees will happily memorise a row that appears 20 times. SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) creates new minority points by interpolating between a minority point and one of its k nearest minority neighbours (Figure 13.6), which fills in the minority region instead of stacking copies.

The caveats are real and under-taught. SMOTE assumes the space between two minority points is minority territory, which is false for categorical features, for high-dimensional data, and for minority points that are actually noise. It destroys calibration by design. And in careful benchmarks it rarely beats "a strong model plus a tuned threshold" on the threshold-free metrics; what it changes is where 0.5 lands. My honest recommendation: reach for threshold moving and class weights first; try resampling when the model is a tree ensemble that fails to split out the minority at all, and measure the effect with PR-AUC, not with F1 at 0.5.

Common mistake: resampling before the split

If you SMOTE the whole dataset and then split, synthetic points in the test set are interpolations of training points, so the model is tested on near-copies of what it trained on. Reported recall can jump to 0.95 while real recall stays at 0.5. The same applies to random over-sampling (exact duplicates land on both sides of the split) and to cross-validation folds. Never resample the validation or test set. Resample only the training fold, inside the CV loop. Figure 13.7 is the correct wiring; the code after it implements it with plain sklearn.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.utils import resample
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors
from sklearn.metrics import f1_score, average_precision_score

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=6000, n_features=20, n_informative=6, weights=[0.95, 0.05],
                           class_sep=0.8, flip_y=0.01, random_state=0)

def smote_like(X_min, n_new, k=5, rng=None):
    """Synthetic minority points: pick a minority point, one of its k neighbours, and interpolate."""
    nn = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=k + 1).fit(X_min)
    _, idx = nn.kneighbors(X_min)
    i = rng.integers(0, len(X_min), n_new)          # anchor points
    j = idx[i, rng.integers(1, k + 1, n_new)]       # a random neighbour (skip column 0 = self)
    lam = rng.random((n_new, 1))
    return X_min[i] + lam * (X_min[j] - X_min[i])

cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
scores = {"none": [], "oversample": [], "smote-like": []}
for tr, va in cv.split(X, y):
    X_tr, y_tr, X_va, y_va = X[tr], y[tr], X[va], y[va]      # validation fold is NEVER touched
    X_min, X_maj = X_tr[y_tr == 1], X_tr[y_tr == 0]
    variants = {"none": (X_tr, y_tr)}
    X_up = resample(X_min, n_samples=len(X_maj), random_state=0)          # random duplication
    variants["oversample"] = (np.vstack([X_maj, X_up]), np.r_[np.zeros(len(X_maj)), np.ones(len(X_up))])
    X_syn = smote_like(X_min, len(X_maj) - len(X_min), rng=np.random.default_rng(0))
    variants["smote-like"] = (np.vstack([X_tr, X_syn]), np.r_[y_tr, np.ones(len(X_syn))])
    for name, (Xa, ya) in variants.items():
        p = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000).fit(Xa, ya).predict_proba(X_va)[:, 1]
        scores[name].append((f1_score(y_va, p >= 0.5), average_precision_score(y_va, p)))

for name, s in scores.items():
    s = np.array(s)
    print(f"{name:<11} F1@0.5 = {s[:,0].mean():.3f}   PR-AUC = {s[:,1].mean():.3f}")
# none        F1@0.5 = 0.515   PR-AUC = 0.607
# oversample  F1@0.5 = 0.281   PR-AUC = 0.545
# smote-like  F1@0.5 = 0.303   PR-AUC = 0.575

Read those numbers carefully: both resampling variants lowered PR-AUC and F1 at 0.5 relative to doing nothing (the plain model at its own tuned threshold, as in the previous block, would win again). This is not a fluke of the seed; for logistic regression on well-behaved data it is the usual outcome, and it is why the recommendation is threshold first. Resampling earns its place when a tree ensemble refuses to grow any leaf for the minority, or when the positive class is so rare (< 0.1%) that most batches contain none.

Extreme imbalance: reframe as anomaly detection

Below roughly 0.1% positives, or when you have a few dozen positives in total, supervised learning starts to fail for lack of examples. Reframe: model what "normal" looks like (Isolation Forest, a Gaussian mixture density, PCA reconstruction error, an autoencoder), score every row by its strangeness, and use the few labelled positives only to evaluate the ranking (precision in the top 100) and to pick the alert threshold. Chapter 22 covers this properly.

At work: the imbalance checklist
  1. Headline metric: PR-AUC (threshold-free) plus recall at the precision the business can afford. Never accuracy.
  2. Stratify every split and every CV fold (StratifiedKFold, stratify=y).
  3. Train a strong model as-is; tune the threshold on validation for the business cost. Ship that if it is good enough.
  4. Try class_weight / scale_pos_weight; compare on PR-AUC.
  5. Try resampling only inside the training fold; compare on PR-AUC.
  6. Whatever you ship, recalibrate (§6) before anyone uses the probabilities as probabilities.

6. Probability calibration

A model is calibrated when its predicted probabilities mean what they say: among all transactions it scores 0.30, about 30% are fraud. Calibration is separate from ranking quality. A model can have AUC 0.95 and say 0.99 for everything it is confident about, or 0.6 for everything, and the AUC will not notice; the reliability diagram will (Figure 13.8).

Why boosted trees and SVMs are miscalibrated

Gradient boosting minimises log-loss, which sounds like it should calibrate, but hundreds of additive trees keep pushing the margins of easily-classified rows outward, so scores pile up near 0 and 1: over-confident. Random forests average many noisy votes, which can rarely reach 0 or 1 for a row unless every tree agrees, so their probabilities are pulled toward the middle: under-confident. SVMs do not produce probabilities at all; the signed distance to the margin is a ranking score. Naive Bayes multiplies many not-actually-independent likelihoods and overshoots wildly. Logistic regression is the exception, because its loss is minimised exactly when the predicted probabilities match observed frequencies, as long as you did not distort it with class weights or resampling.

Platt scaling vs isotonic regression

Both fit a mapping from the model's raw score to a calibrated probability, on data the model did not train on. Platt scaling (method="sigmoid") fits a one-dimensional logistic regression p = σ(a·s + b): two parameters, hard to overfit, assumes the miscalibration is a smooth S-shape. Isotonic regression (method="isotonic") fits a monotone non-decreasing step function: more flexible, corrects any monotone distortion, needs more data (a few thousand rows, more if positives are rare) or it overfits the steps. CalibratedClassifierCV(estimator, method, cv=5) does the honest thing: for each fold it fits the model on 4/5 and the calibrator on the held-out 1/5, then averages the five calibrated models at prediction time.

Brier score

Brier = (1/n) ∑i (pi − yi)²
The math (optional)

pi is the predicted probability for row i and yi ∈ {0, 1} the outcome, so the Brier score is the mean squared error of the probabilities; lower is better, 0 is perfect, and predicting the base rate for everyone scores r(1 − r) where r is the positive rate (0.0475 for r = 5%). It decomposes as Brier = reliability − resolution + uncertainty: reliability is the calibration gap (the distance of each bin from the diagonal, squared and weighted), resolution rewards spreading predictions away from the base rate, uncertainty is r(1 − r) and is out of your hands. Calibration lowers the reliability term without touching resolution, which is why in the code below Brier improves while AUC barely moves. Log-loss is the other proper scoring rule; it punishes confident mistakes much more harshly.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.calibration import CalibratedClassifierCV, calibration_curve
from sklearn.metrics import brier_score_loss, roc_auc_score

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=3000, n_features=20, n_informative=8, flip_y=0.15, random_state=1)

                                 method="isotonic", cv=5).fit(X_tr, y_tr)

Why calibration matters when probabilities feed decisions

If the risk team holds every transaction with p ≥ t for a $4 review, and a missed fraud costs $120, the expected-value rule is: hold when p × 120 > 4, i.e. when p > 0.033. That threshold is only right if p is a real probability. An over-confident model that says 0.02 when it means 0.05 lets $6 of expected loss through to save $4; an under-confident one floods the reviewers. The same holds for ranking with costs (which 500 alerts to send to the reviewers today), for combining model outputs with other probabilities, for setting SLAs, and for anything that reports "probability" to a human. Calibrate, then compute.

Key idea

Ranking metrics (AUC) tell you the model orders cases well. Calibration tells you the numbers can be used in arithmetic. Threshold-based decisions need only ranking plus one tuned threshold; expected-value decisions need calibration. Know which you are making.

7. Comparing models fairly

"Model B got 0.986 and model A got 0.981, so B is better." On 171 test rows that difference is noise, and the tools to prove it are simple.

  1. Repeated CV on the training data. RepeatedStratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, n_repeats=5) gives 25 fold scores per model; look at the spread, not just the mean. If the standard deviation across folds is 0.01, a 0.005 difference in means is not a finding.
  2. Paired comparisons. Score both models on the same folds (same random_state) and look at the per-fold differences. Pairing removes the fold-to-fold difficulty variation, so a consistent small edge shows up as 25 positive differences even when the two score distributions overlap heavily. (The classic paired t-test on CV folds is over-optimistic because folds share data; the Nadeau–Bengio corrected t-test fixes the variance; in practice "did B win on 22 of 25 folds" is convincing enough.)
  3. Bootstrap confidence intervals on the test metric. Resample the test rows with replacement 1,000–2,000 times, recompute the metric for both models on the same resampled rows, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the difference (Figure 13.9). If the interval includes zero, the test set cannot separate the models.
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, stratify=y, random_state=4)
lr = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000)).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
rf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
p_lr, p_rf = lr.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1], rf.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
B = 2000; n = len(y_te)
auc_lr, auc_rf, diff = [], [], []
for _ in range(B):
    idx = rng.integers(0, n, n)                     # resample test rows WITH replacement
    if len(np.unique(y_te[idx])) < 2: continue
    a, b = roc_auc_score(y_te[idx], p_lr[idx]), roc_auc_score(y_te[idx], p_rf[idx])
    auc_lr.append(a); auc_rf.append(b); diff.append(a - b)   # paired: same rows for both models

def ci(v): lo, hi = np.percentile(v, [2.5, 97.5]); return f"{np.mean(v):.3f}  [{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f}]"
print("LogReg AUC:", ci(auc_lr))
print("RF     AUC:", ci(auc_rf))
print("LR - RF   :", ci(diff), "<- CI includes 0? then the data can't separate them")
# LogReg AUC: 0.988  [0.969, 0.999]
# RF     AUC: 0.986  [0.967, 0.999]
# LR - RF   : 0.002  [-0.011, 0.017] <- CI includes 0? then the data can't separate them
Common mistake: test-set p-hacking

Trying 15 model variants and reporting the one with the best test score is the same sin as tuning on the test set, at a larger scale. With 15 variants of equal quality and a noisy test set, the winner will look meaningfully better purely by selection. Defences: pre-register the comparison (write down which 2–3 candidates you will compare and on what metric before running them), make selections on CV, and quote the test result with its bootstrap interval. If someone shows you a leaderboard with 40 entries and no error bars, the top 5 are a coin toss.

Statistical vs practical significance. With 2 million test rows, a 0.001 AUC difference will be "significant". Whether it matters depends on the decision: at the risk team's operating point, 0.001 AUC might be 3 fraud cases a month, or 300. Translate the metric into the business unit (dollars, reviews, minutes) at the chosen threshold before arguing about it. Conversely, a practically large difference with a wide interval means you need more test data, not a decision.

Quiz

Check your understanding

1. You have a budget of 12 trials to tune a gradient-boosting model with 5 hyperparameters, of which (unknown to you) only the learning rate matters much. Which strategy is best?

Random search tests 12 distinct learning rates while covering the others; a 2-per-axis grid tests only 2. Grid over learning rate alone would be fine if you knew it was the only one that mattered, but you do not. Nested CV is an evaluation procedure, not a search strategy.

2. Why must the scaler be inside the Pipeline passed to GridSearchCV rather than applied to X before the search?

A scaler fit on all rows leaks the mean and variance of the validation rows into training. It is a small leak for scaling and a large one for target encoding, imputation from the target, or feature selection; the pipeline rule prevents all of them uniformly.

3. A fraud model has ROC-AUC 0.96. The business asks whether it can flag transactions with at least 40% precision. Which metric answers that?

ROC-AUC says the model ranks positives above negatives 96% of the time but says nothing about precision at any threshold when positives are rare. The PR curve is exactly the precision-vs-recall trade-off across thresholds.

4. A colleague applies SMOTE to the full dataset, then runs 5-fold CV and reports recall 0.94. Real-world recall turns out to be 0.55. What happened?

Resampling before splitting leaks: every synthetic validation point is an interpolation of training points. Resample only the training fold inside the CV loop.

5. You calibrate a gradient-boosting model with isotonic regression. Which statement is expected?

Calibration is a monotone (isotonic) remapping of scores, so ranking and AUC are essentially preserved while the probabilities become truthful, which lowers the Brier score. Because the mapping changes the numbers, the set of rows above 0.5 does change, so recall at 0.5 generally moves.

6. Model A: test AUC 0.912. Model B: 0.905. Paired bootstrap 95% interval for A − B: [−0.006, +0.020]. What should you conclude?

An interval containing zero means the observed difference is within the noise of the test set. Re-running until it "works" is p-hacking. The scoring table in §8 is how you decide when accuracy is a tie.

Exercises

Exercise 13.1 — Measure the optimism of the winning CV score

On make_classification(n_samples=600, n_features=30, n_informative=5, random_state=0), run RandomizedSearchCV over an RBF SVM pipeline with 60 trials and 5-fold CV. Record best_score_. Then wrap the same search in cross_val_score with an outer 5-fold split (nested CV). Compare the inner winner's score to the outer mean. Repeat with 5 trials instead of 60. What does the gap depend on?

Show answer

You should see the inner best_score_ exceed the nested estimate by roughly 0.01–0.03 with 60 trials and by less with 5 trials: the optimism grows with the number of candidates you take the maximum over, and with the noise of the fold scores (small n, small folds). With 600 rows the fold noise is large, so the effect is visible; with 60,000 rows it nearly disappears. This is the "winner's curse", and it is why the nested score, or the single test evaluation, is what you quote.

Exercise 13.2 — Cost-based threshold

Using the imbalanced make_classification setup from §5 (5% positives), fit a calibrated logistic regression and choose the threshold that minimises expected cost with a $4 review cost per flagged transaction and a $120 loss per missed positive. Compute the total cost on the test set at that threshold, at 0.5, and at the F1-optimal threshold. Then double the review cost to $8 and repeat. Which thresholds move, and by how much?

Show answer

The expected-cost threshold is p* = 4 / 120 ≈ 0.033 (flag when p × 120 > 4), far below both 0.5 and the F1-optimal ≈ 0.12, because a miss is 30× more expensive than a review. Total cost at 0.033 is the lowest of the three (many reviews, few misses); at 0.5 the cost is dominated by missed fraud. Doubling the review cost moves p* to 8/120 ≈ 0.067; the F1 threshold does not move at all, because F1 knows nothing about dollars. That is the argument for cost-based thresholds when you have the costs, and it only works if the probabilities are calibrated.

Exercise 13.3 — Do it at work: an honest comparison memo

Take a classification problem you have at work with at least two candidate models (one can be the current rule or baseline). Produce a one-page memo with: the pre-registered metric and why; the tuning procedure (search type, budget, CV scheme, what was inside the pipeline); nested-CV or repeated-CV scores with standard deviations; the single test-set evaluation with a paired bootstrap interval for the difference; a reliability diagram and Brier score for the model whose probabilities will be used; and a scoring table with latency, interpretability and maintenance columns weighted by the decision owner. State the decision and the one condition under which you would revisit it.

Show answer

A good memo is short, has an error bar on every number that will be used to decide, and names the weights before the scores. The commonest gaps reviewers find: the test set was evaluated more than once; the threshold was chosen on the test set; class weights were used and the probabilities were then reported as probabilities without recalibration; the metric that "won" was not the one agreed up front. If any of those apply, say so in the memo; an honest limitation is worth more than a clean-looking table.

Chapter summary

  • Hyperparameter tuning is noisy optimisation of an expensive black box: random or Bayesian search beats grid; halving saves compute; log-uniform ranges for scale-like knobs.
  • For boosted trees fix the learning rate and let early stopping choose the number of trees; tune depth/leaves, min leaf size, subsampling next.
  • Search inside CV, pipeline inside the search (step__param), nested CV for the honest number, refit on all training data, evaluate on the test set exactly once.
  • On imbalanced data accuracy lies: use PR-AUC and recall at a required precision; stratify every split.
  • Move the threshold first; class weights second; resample only the training fold, and judge resampling by PR-AUC, not by F1 at 0.5.
  • Below ~0.1% positives, reframe as anomaly detection.
  • Ranking (AUC) and calibration are different properties; read the reliability diagram and Brier score; fix with Platt or isotonic via CalibratedClassifierCV; calibrate before any expected-value decision.
  • Compare models with repeated, paired CV and a paired bootstrap interval on the test metric; an interval containing zero is a tie; never re-run until it is not.
  • Choose the final model with a written scoring table (accuracy, latency, interpretability, maintenance) and keep an experiment log that becomes the model card.
