1. Why explain a model at all?

Here is the situation this chapter is built around. A lender has trained a gradient-boosted model (Chapter 10) that predicts whether a loan applicant will repay. On the validation set it beats the old scorecard by four AUC points. The team is thrilled. Then five things happen in one week:

  1. The head of credit asks "why should I trust it?" Four AUC points is not an answer. She wants to know what the model looks at and whether that matches thirty years of lending intuition. Trust is the first reason to explain.
  2. A data engineer notices the top feature is days_since_last_statement. That column is only populated after a loan is issued. The model has been reading the answer off a column that will not exist at prediction time — leakage (Chapter 7). Feature importance found in ten minutes what a metric could never find.
  3. Legal reminds everyone that declined applicants are entitled to a reason. In the US the Equal Credit Opportunity Act requires "adverse action" notices with specific principal reasons; the EU's GDPR and AI Act push in the same direction for automated decisions. Regulation makes per-decision explanation mandatory, not optional.
  4. Someone plots the model's behaviour and sees approval probability drop as income rises above €120k. That is a data artefact (few rich applicants, several of whom defaulted on business loans), not a truth about the world. Debugging a model's logic is impossible without looking inside it.
  5. The branch staff who must act on the score ignore it. They do not understand it, so they override it. A model nobody uses has an ROI of zero. Adoption is the reason most projects need explanations most urgently.

Trust, leakage-hunting, regulation, debugging, adoption: five different reasons, and they need different tools. "Which feature matters most overall?" is a global question. "Why was this applicant declined?" is a local one. A regulator wants a stable, auditable answer; a data scientist debugging a model wants the fastest possible answer. The first skill is to recognise which question is being asked.

Explain it like I'm 9

Imagine a very good chess player who can't talk. She wins every game, but when you ask "why did you move the knight?" she just shrugs. Would you let her play your tournament games for money, without being able to check she isn't cheating, or about to blunder? Explanation tools are the translator that lets the silent expert say "the knight protects the king and attacks the queen — mostly the queen." Once she can say that, you can check her reasoning, catch her mistakes, and decide when to trust her.

Key idea

An explanation is an answer to a specific question about a model: what does it rely on overall? (global), why did it give this output? (local), what would have to change to flip the output? (counterfactual), does it treat groups differently? (fairness). No single plot answers all four. Choosing the tool starts with naming the question.

2. The map: four kinds of explanation

Every explanation method sits somewhere on two axes. Scope: does it describe the whole model (global) or one prediction (local)? Timing: is the model transparent by construction (intrinsic) or do we interrogate a finished black box (post-hoc)? A third, orthogonal distinction is whether the method needs to know what kind of model it is looking at (model-specific, like TreeSHAP or a neural net's gradients) or works on anything with a predict function (model-agnostic, like permutation importance, PDP, KernelSHAP and LIME).

3. Models that explain themselves

Before reaching for post-hoc tools, ask whether an intrinsically interpretable model is good enough. Surprisingly often it is — and when the accuracy gap to a boosted model is small, an interpretable model is the right engineering choice for a regulated decision.

Linear and logistic coefficients — with the scaling caveat

In a linear model ŷ = w0 + w1x1 + … + wnxn each coefficient says "one unit more of xj moves the prediction by wj, holding the others fixed." That sentence contains two traps. First, units: a coefficient of 0.002 on income_eur and 3.0 on num_dependents tells you nothing about which matters more — income spans 100,000 units, dependents span 5. Only after standardising the features (Chapter 8) are coefficients comparable, and then wj reads as "effect of one standard deviation." Second, "holding the others fixed" is a fiction when features are correlated: you cannot raise worst radius while holding worst perimeter fixed, so the individual coefficients of correlated features are unstable (they can even flip sign) even when their sum is perfectly stable.

The math (optional)
logit(p) = ln(p / (1 − p)) = w0 + ∑j wj xj   ⇒   odds ratio for +1 in xj = ewj

For logistic regression the coefficient wj is the change in log-odds per unit of xj; exponentiate it to get an odds ratio (e0.7 ≈ 2.0 means the odds of the positive class double). Log-odds are additive, probabilities are not — this is why SHAP for classifiers also works in log-odds (Section 6).

Decision trees

A depth-3 tree is a flowchart anyone can follow, and the root-to-leaf path is the local explanation: "declined because utilisation > 80% and months-on-book < 12." Past depth 4 or 5 the flowchart stops being readable, and Chapter 9 showed that single trees are unstable. Trees are best as the honest baseline you compare a black box against, and as surrogates (Section 7).

GAMs and EBMs

A generalised additive model keeps the additive structure of a linear model but lets each feature have its own learned shape function: ŷ = w0 + f1(x1) + … + fn(xn). Each fj is a curve you can plot, so the whole model is a small gallery of curves. Explainable Boosting Machines (EBM, in the interpret package) fit those curves with boosting and add a few pairwise interaction terms; on tabular data they typically land within a point or two of a tuned GBM while remaining exactly interpretable. If you work in a regulated domain, an EBM should be one of the candidates in every model bake-off.

At work

The question is never "interpretable model or accurate model?" It is "how much accuracy does interpretability cost here, and is that cost worth what interpretability buys?" Measure the gap. A 0.3-point AUC gap is worth paying for a model that legal can sign off in a week; a 6-point gap in fraud detection is not. Write the number down in the model card (Section 10) so the trade-off is a decision, not an accident.

4. Feature importance, done honestly

"Which features matter most?" is the first question everyone asks. Tree libraries answer it for free with impurity importance (also called gain or Gini importance): for each feature, add up how much every split on that feature reduced impurity (Gini, entropy or squared error, Chapter 9), across all trees, and normalise to sum to 1. It costs nothing, and it is biased in three predictable ways:

  • It favours high-cardinality features. A continuous feature or an ID-like column offers thousands of candidate split points, so it gets to reduce impurity somewhere by chance. A random noise column with many unique values will routinely out-rank a genuinely useful binary feature.
  • It is computed on training data. It measures how much the model used a feature while fitting — including while overfitting — not whether the feature helps on new data.
  • Correlated features are arbitrary. When two features carry the same signal, whichever the tree happened to pick at the top gets the credit; the other looks useless.

Permutation importance fixes the first two problems by asking a cleaner question: if I destroy the information in this column, how much worse does the model get?

  1. Score the model on held-out data. Compute the metric you actually care about (AUC, log-loss, RMSE) on the validation set: sbase.
  2. Shuffle one column. Randomly permute the values of feature j across rows. Every row keeps a realistic value of xj, just not its own — the marginal distribution is preserved, the link to the label is cut.
  3. Re-score. Predict with the shuffled data and compute the metric again: sshuffled,j.
  4. Importance = the drop. impj = sbase − sshuffled,j. Repeat the shuffle 10–30 times and report mean ± standard deviation, because one shuffle is noisy.
  5. Restore the column and move to the next feature. No retraining anywhere — this is why it is cheap enough to run on any model.
# Impurity importance vs permutation importance on a gradient-boosted model
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True, as_frame=True)
X_tr, X_va, y_tr, y_va = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=0, stratify=y)

gbm = GradientBoostingClassifier(n_estimators=200, max_depth=3, learning_rate=0.05, random_state=0)
gbm.fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(f"validation accuracy = {gbm.score(X_va, y_va):.3f}")

# 1) impurity ("gain") importance: free, computed on TRAINING data, sums to 1
imp = pd.Series(gbm.feature_importances_, index=X.columns).sort_values(ascending=False)

# 2) permutation importance: shuffle one column, measure the drop in a metric on VALIDATION data
perm = permutation_importance(gbm, X_va, y_va, scoring="neg_log_loss", n_repeats=20, random_state=0, n_jobs=1)
pi = pd.Series(perm.importances_mean, index=X.columns).sort_values(ascending=False)
sd = pd.Series(perm.importances_std, index=X.columns)

print("\ntop-5 impurity importance        top-5 permutation importance (log-loss increase)")
for (a, va), (b, vb) in zip(imp.head(5).items(), pi.head(5).items()):
    print(f"{a:<24}{va:5.3f}        {b:<24}{vb:5.3f} ± {sd[b]:.3f}")
print(f"\nfeatures whose permutation importance is <= 0: {(pi <= 0).sum()} of {len(pi)}")
# validation accuracy = 0.942

# worst perimeter         0.493        worst texture           0.074 ± 0.026
# worst concave points    0.159        worst perimeter         0.045 ± 0.031
# mean concave points     0.105        mean concave points     0.044 ± 0.025
# worst concave points    0.080        worst concave points    0.044 ± 0.033
# worst texture           0.029        worst radius            0.028 ± 0.029

Read the two columns against each other. Impurity importance says worst perimeter carries half the model (0.49) and worst texture is a footnote (0.03). Permutation importance says the reverse: shuffling worst texture hurts the most, and worst perimeter loses much of its crown. Why? worst perimeter, worst radius and worst area are almost the same measurement (correlation above 0.97). The trees split on perimeter first, so impurity credits perimeter; but when you shuffle perimeter alone, radius and area still carry the signal and the model barely suffers. Texture, by contrast, has no stand-in, so destroying it hurts. Also note the ± column: with 171 validation rows the ranks below the top two are genuinely uncertain. Report the uncertainty; do not present a ranked list as if it were carved in stone.

Common mistake

Computing permutation importance on the training set. An overfit model "needs" the noise it memorised, so training-set permutation importance rewards exactly the features that will fail in production. Always pass the validation (or test) set. The second mistake is the mirror image of the first: concluding "feature X has importance zero, so it is useless." With correlated features, zero permutation importance means redundant given the others, not uninformative. Drop-column importance (retrain without the feature) or grouping correlated features before shuffling both address this.

At work: the leakage detector

Run permutation importance on every model before you believe its validation score. A single feature with importance far above the rest — especially a date, an ID, a status field, or anything that could be written after the outcome — is a leakage suspect. In the lender's case days_since_last_statement had three times the importance of the next feature. That pattern should make you suspicious before it makes you proud.

5. Partial dependence & ICE

Importance says how much a feature matters; it says nothing about how. Does default risk go up with utilisation, down, or up-then-down? The partial dependence plot (PDP) answers this by brute force: take every row in the data, overwrite feature j with a chosen value v, predict, and average. Slide v across the feature's range and you get one curve — "the average prediction as a function of xj, with everything else as it is in the data."

The math (optional)
PDj(v) = (1/n) ∑i=1n f(v, xi,−j)

f is the fitted model, n the number of rows, xi,−j all the features of row i except j, which is forced to the value v. Each term f(v, xi,−j) as a function of v is one ICE curve (individual conditional expectation): the model's response for row i alone. The PDP is simply the mean of the ICE curves — and a mean can hide anything.

Three rules for reading these plots at work:

  • Always draw ICE curves under the PDP. If they are parallel, the PDP is trustworthy. If they fan out or cross, there is an interaction and the average is misleading; go find the interacting feature with a 2-D PDP or a SHAP dependence plot.
  • Mark where the data actually is. The PDP forces every row to take value v, including combinations that never occur (a 19-year-old with 40 years of credit history). In those regions the curve is pure extrapolation — the model's guess about a world it never saw. Overlay a rug of observed values, or restrict the grid to the 5th–95th percentiles as sklearn does by default. Accumulated local effects (ALE) plots avoid the problem by only asking about small local changes around real data points; use them when features are strongly correlated.
  • A PDP is not a causal curve. It shows what the model does when a feature changes, not what the world would do if you changed it. Section 8 returns to this.

6. SHAP: fair credit for every feature

Importance ranks features; PDPs draw average curves. Neither answers the local question the regulator asks: for this applicant, how much did each feature push the score up or down? SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) answers it with a number per feature per prediction, and those numbers have a property nothing else in this chapter has: they add up exactly to the prediction. To see where they come from we need a short detour through a taxi.

Explain it like I'm 9: three friends split a taxi

Anna, Ben and Cara share a taxi home along the same road. Anna lives closest: alone, her ride would cost €6. Ben's would cost €12. Cara lives far out: €42. Because they share, the total is just €42 (the taxi drives to Cara's anyway). How should they split it? "€14 each" is unfair to Anna. "Everyone pays their solo fare" over-collects. The fair answer, the Shapley value, is: imagine them getting in the taxi in every possible order, and each time charge each person only the extra cost they add to whoever is already in the taxi. Then average each person's extra cost over all six orders. Anna ends up paying €2, Ben €5 and Cara €35 — and 2 + 5 + 35 = 42 exactly. In SHAP, the "friends" are the features, the "fare" is the prediction, and "getting in the taxi" means "being known to the model."

The fully worked example

Formally we have a set of players N = {A, B, C} and a value function v(S) that gives the payout of any coalition S. Here v(S) is the fare of the taxi that serves the members of S. All 2³ = 8 coalitions:

Coalition S∅{A}{B}{C}{A,B}{A,C}{B,C}{A,B,C}
v(S) (fare, €)06124212424242

Now every one of the 3! = 6 arrival orders, and each player's marginal contribution — what v gains at the moment they join:

OrderA's marginal contributionB's marginal contributionC's marginal contribution
A → B → Cv(A) − v(∅) = 6v(AB) − v(A) = 6v(ABC) − v(AB) = 30
A → C → Bv(A) − v(∅) = 6v(ABC) − v(AC) = 0v(AC) − v(A) = 36
B → A → Cv(AB) − v(B) = 0v(B) − v(∅) = 12v(ABC) − v(AB) = 30
B → C → Av(ABC) − v(BC) = 0v(B) − v(∅) = 12v(BC) − v(B) = 30
C → A → Bv(AC) − v(C) = 0v(ABC) − v(AC) = 0v(C) − v(∅) = 42
C → B → Av(ABC) − v(BC) = 0v(BC) − v(C) = 0v(C) − v(∅) = 42
Average = Shapley value φ12 / 6 = 230 / 6 = 5210 / 6 = 35
The math (optional)
φi = ∑S ⊆ N∖{i} [ |S|! (n − |S| − 1)! / n! ] · [ v(S ∪ {i}) − v(S) ]

φi is player i's Shapley value; the sum runs over every coalition S that does not contain i; v(S ∪ {i}) − v(S) is i's marginal contribution to S; and the bracketed weight is the fraction of the n! orderings in which exactly the members of S arrive before i. Averaging over orderings and summing over coalitions with these weights are the same computation. For a model, the players are the n features, and v(S) is the model's expected prediction when only the features in S are known: v(S) = E[ f(x) | xS ]. SHAP estimates that expectation by filling the unknown features with values from a background dataset.

The Shapley value is the only way to split the payout that satisfies four fairness axioms at once, which is why it is worth the trouble:

Efficiency

The shares add up to the whole: ∑φi = v(N) − v(∅). For a model: base value + ∑ SHAP = prediction, exactly. This is what makes waterfall plots honest.

Symmetry

Two players who contribute the same to every coalition get the same share. Two perfectly redundant features split their credit equally.

Dummy (null player)

A player who adds nothing to any coalition gets zero. A feature the model never uses has SHAP = 0 for every row.

Additivity

For a sum of games, shares add. For an ensemble of trees, the SHAP values of the ensemble are the sum of the SHAP values of each tree — this is what makes TreeSHAP possible.

# Exact Shapley values for a 3-player game, by brute force over all coalitions
from itertools import permutations, combinations
from math import factorial

# v(S) = value the model "earns" with only the features in S present (here: a taxi fare shared by 3 friends)
players = ["A", "B", "C"]
v = {(): 0, ("A",): 6, ("B",): 12, ("C",): 42,
     ("A", "B"): 12, ("A", "C"): 42, ("B", "C"): 42, ("A", "B", "C"): 42}
def val(S): return v[tuple(sorted(S))]

# Method 1: average marginal contribution over all 3! = 6 orderings
phi = {p: 0.0 for p in players}
for order in permutations(players):
    seen = []
    for p in order:
        phi[p] += val(seen + [p]) - val(seen)
        seen.append(p)
for p in players: phi[p] /= factorial(len(players))
print("Shapley (via orderings):", {p: round(phi[p], 2) for p in players})

# Method 2: the weighted-coalition formula  phi_i = sum_S |S|!(n-|S|-1)!/n! * [v(S+i) - v(S)]
n = len(players)
def shapley(i):
    others = [p for p in players if p != i]
    total = 0.0
    for k in range(n):
        for S in combinations(others, k):
            w = factorial(k) * factorial(n - k - 1) / factorial(n)
            total += w * (val(list(S) + [i]) - val(list(S)))
    return total
print("Shapley (via formula):  ", {p: round(shapley(p), 2) for p in players})
print("efficiency: sum =", round(sum(shapley(p) for p in players), 2), "= v(all) =", val(players))
# Shapley (via orderings): {'A': 2.0, 'B': 5.0, 'C': 35.0}
# Shapley (via formula):   {'A': 2.0, 'B': 5.0, 'C': 35.0}
# efficiency: sum = 42.0 = v(all) = 42

Why exact SHAP is impossible, and how the approximations work

The formula sums over every subset of the other features: 2n−1 coalitions per feature. With 30 features that is half a billion model evaluations per row, each of which needs its own expectation over a background set. Nobody does that. Two shortcuts make SHAP practical:

  • KernelSHAP (model-agnostic). Sample a few hundred to a few thousand coalitions, evaluate the model with "absent" features filled in from the background data, and fit a weighted linear regression whose coefficients are the SHAP values. The weights are chosen so that the regression solution equals the Shapley value in the limit. It works on any model, and it is slow: explaining 1,000 rows of a 30-feature model can take minutes to hours.
  • TreeSHAP (model-specific). For tree ensembles, the expectation E[f(x) | xS] can be read off the tree by tracking how many training samples flow down each branch. Lundberg's algorithm computes exact Shapley values in O(T·L·D²) — trees × leaves × depth² — which is milliseconds per row. This is why SHAP on gradient boosting is the de facto standard for tabular explanation: exact, fast, and the additivity axiom means the ensemble's values are the sum of the trees' values.
  • DeepSHAP / GradientSHAP for neural networks, and LinearSHAP for linear models, use each model class's structure similarly.
Key idea

A SHAP value φij answers: "compared with the average prediction over the background data, how much did feature j push row i's prediction, fairly accounting for every way the features could have been revealed?" Positive pushes the prediction up, negative pushes it down, and base value + ∑j φij = f(xi) to the last decimal.

Reading the four standard SHAP plots

# SHAP values with TreeExplainer: global (mean |SHAP|) and local (one row) explanations
import numpy as np, pandas as pd, shap
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingClassifier

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True, as_frame=True)
X_tr, X_va, y_tr, y_va = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=0, stratify=y)
gbm = GradientBoostingClassifier(n_estimators=100, max_depth=3, learning_rate=0.1, random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)

explainer = shap.TreeExplainer(gbm)          # exact, polynomial-time Shapley values for trees
sv = explainer.shap_values(X_va)             # shape (n_rows, n_features), in LOG-ODDS for a GBM classifier
base = float(np.ravel(explainer.expected_value)[0])
print("shap matrix:", sv.shape, "| base value (mean log-odds on training data):", round(base, 3))

# Global: mean absolute SHAP per feature = the "bar plot"
global_imp = pd.Series(np.abs(sv).mean(axis=0), index=X.columns).sort_values(ascending=False)
print("\nmean |SHAP| (log-odds units), top 6:")
print(global_imp.head(6).round(3).to_string())

# Local: explain one borderline patient = the "waterfall plot"
proba = gbm.predict_proba(X_va)[:, 1]
i = int(np.argmin(np.abs(proba - 0.6)))           # a row near the decision boundary
row = pd.Series(sv[i], index=X.columns)
print(f"\nrow {i}: true label = {y_va.iloc[i]}, P(benign) = {proba[i]:.3f}")
print("largest contributions (log-odds):")
print(row.reindex(row.abs().sort_values(ascending=False).index).head(5).round(3).to_string())

# Efficiency check: base + sum(SHAP) must equal the model's raw (log-odds) output
raw = gbm.decision_function(X_va.iloc[[i]])[0]
print(f"\nbase + sum(shap) = {base + sv[i].sum():.4f}   model log-odds = {raw:.4f}   sigmoid -> {1/(1+np.exp(-raw)):.3f}")

# Interaction values: a (n_features x n_features) matrix per row; the diagonal is the main effect
inter = explainer.shap_interaction_values(X_va.iloc[[i]])[0]
k = list(X.columns).index("worst concave points"); j = list(X.columns).index("worst radius")
print(f"main effect(worst concave points) = {inter[k, k]:.3f}, interaction with worst radius = {inter[k, j]:.3f}")

Three things in that output deserve a second look. The base value is 2.25 log-odds — P ≈ 0.90 — because ~63% of the training rows are benign and the boosted model is confident on most of them; "average prediction" here means average model output, not the class prior. The row we picked is a malignant tumour the model calls benign with P = 0.60: the waterfall shows why it is borderline (three strong pushes down, two up), which is exactly the kind of case a clinician should review. And the interaction value between the two "worst" features is tiny (0.02) relative to the main effect (−0.99) — the model is treating them nearly additively for this row.

Dependence plots and interaction values

A SHAP dependence plot scatters one feature's value (x-axis) against its SHAP value (y-axis), one dot per row. It is the PDP's honest cousin: instead of averaging, it shows every row, so a vertical spread at a fixed x means an interaction — and colouring the dots by a second feature usually reveals which one. Interaction values (shap_interaction_values) go further and decompose each φij into a main effect plus pairwise terms φijk, so you can rank interactions the way you rank features. They are exact for trees and cost roughly n times more than plain SHAP.

Common mistake

Reading classifier SHAP values as probabilities. For tree-based classifiers, TreeExplainer returns SHAP values in the model's raw output space — log-odds (margin) for GBM/XGBoost/LightGBM. A SHAP value of +1.0 does not mean "+100% probability"; it means the odds were multiplied by e ≈ 2.7. Log-odds are the right space for additivity, but not for a stakeholder. Either explain in odds ("this feature tripled the odds of approval") or, if you must show probabilities, use model_output="probability" with a background dataset — and accept that those values are approximate and no longer exactly additive across features. For scikit-learn's RandomForestClassifier, TreeSHAP works in probability space directly, and returns one matrix per class.

At work: SHAP hygiene
  • Explain on validation or production data, never only training data. Global SHAP on training rows describes what the model memorised.
  • Fix the background. The base value depends on the background dataset. Use a representative sample (a few hundred rows of training data is typical), store it with the model, and never change it silently between releases — every waterfall's starting point would move.
  • Cache explanations. TreeSHAP is fast but not free; for a real-time credit decision, compute the waterfall once at scoring time and store the top-5 reasons alongside the score (Chapter 21 covers the logging).
  • Translate, don't paste. "worst concave points = 0.16 contributed −0.90 log-odds" is not a reason a customer can act on. Map features to plain-language reason codes in advance.

7. LIME, counterfactuals & surrogates

LIME: a local linear stand-in

Before SHAP, the standard local method was LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations), and it is still worth understanding because its idea is simple and its failure mode is instructive. To explain a prediction at x: (1) generate a few thousand perturbed copies of x by jittering its features; (2) get the black box's prediction for each copy; (3) weight the copies by how close they are to x (a kernel); (4) fit a weighted linear regression to those predictions; (5) report its coefficients. The linear model is nonsense globally, but in a small neighbourhood of x it is a decent approximation of the black box, and its coefficients are the local feature effects.

LIME's weakness is instability: the explanation depends on the random perturbations, the kernel width and the discretisation of features, none of which have a principled default. Run it twice on the same row and the top-3 features can reorder; run it on two nearly identical rows and the signs can differ. For a debugging session that is tolerable; for an adverse-action notice that must be reproducible six months later in front of a regulator, it is not. This is the main practical reason SHAP replaced LIME for tabular models: the same input, model and background give the same explanation every time. Where LIME still shines is unstructured data — explaining an image classifier by super-pixels or a text classifier by words — because its perturbation scheme adapts naturally to "turn this word off."

Counterfactual explanations

A counterfactual explanation answers "what is the smallest change to this input that would have produced the other decision?" — if your utilisation had been 48% instead of 71%, you would have been approved. It is the explanation format that humans find most natural, and the one that is most directly actionable for the person affected. Generating one is an optimisation: minimise the distance between x and x′ subject to f(x′) crossing the threshold, plus constraints that keep x′ plausible (a real customer could exist there — no age of 250), actionable (do not tell a customer to become younger; do not touch protected attributes) and sparse (change one or two features, not twelve). The dice-ml and alibi packages do this properly; the code below does the one-feature brute-force version, which is often enough for tabular models with a handful of actionable features.

Global surrogate models

The blunt instrument: fit a small, interpretable model — usually a depth-3 or depth-4 tree — not to the labels but to the black box's predictions. The surrogate is then a readable approximation of the black box, and the fraction of test rows on which it agrees with the black box (its fidelity) tells you how much to trust the approximation. A surrogate with 94% fidelity is a great way to explain "roughly how the model thinks" to a business audience; a surrogate with 70% fidelity is a story about a different model. Always report the number.

# Global surrogate tree + a brute-force counterfactual for one prediction
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier, export_text

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True, as_frame=True)
feats = ["mean radius", "mean texture", "mean smoothness", "mean concave points", "mean symmetry", "worst texture"]
X = X[feats]
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=0, stratify=y)
rf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)

# Surrogate: fit a shallow tree to the BLACK BOX'S predictions (not to the labels)
sur = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=2, random_state=0).fit(X_tr, rf.predict(X_tr))
fidelity = (sur.predict(X_te) == rf.predict(X_te)).mean()
print(f"surrogate agrees with the forest on {fidelity:.1%} of test rows")
print(export_text(sur, feature_names=feats, decimals=2))

# Counterfactual: smallest single-feature move (in std units) that flips a malignant call to benign
proba = rf.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]
i = int(np.argmin(np.abs(proba - 0.3)))                   # a clearly-malignant call, but not hopeless
x0 = X_te.iloc[[i]].copy(); print(f"\nrow {i}: P(benign) = {proba[i]:.3f} -> predicted malignant")
best = None
for f in feats:
    for step in np.linspace(-3, 3, 61):
        x = x0.copy(); x[f] = x0[f].values[0] + step * X_tr[f].std()
        if rf.predict_proba(x)[0, 1] >= 0.5 and (best is None or abs(step) < abs(best[1])):
            best = (f, step, x[f].values[0])
print(f"counterfactual: move '{best[0]}' from {x0[best[0]].values[0]:.3f} to {best[2]:.3f} ({best[1]:+.1f} std) -> benign")
# surrogate agrees with the forest on 93.6% of test rows
# |--- mean concave points <= 0.06
# |   |--- mean radius <= 15.05
# |   |   |--- class: 1
# |   |--- mean radius >  15.05
# |   |   |--- class: 0
# |--- mean concave points >  0.06
# |   |--- mean texture <= 14.12
# |   |   |--- class: 1
# |   |--- mean texture >  14.12
# |   |   |--- class: 0
#
# row 30: P(benign) = 0.290 -> predicted malignant
# counterfactual: move 'mean concave points' from 0.062 to 0.054 (-0.2 std) -> benign

A four-leaf tree reproduces 94% of a 300-tree forest's decisions — that is the sentence to put on the slide, with the tree beside it. And the counterfactual makes the surrogate's first split concrete: this tumour sits just above the 0.06 concave-points threshold, and a change of 0.2 standard deviations flips the call. For a medical model that is a warning ("this decision is fragile, get a second opinion"); for a lending model the equivalent sentence is the adverse-action reason and the path to approval in one.

8. Caveats: when explanations lie

Explanation tools are tools for understanding a model. They are not tools for understanding the world, and confusing the two is the most common expert-level mistake in this field.

A bad model explains itself confidently

SHAP values of a model with AUC 0.55 add up perfectly, look beautiful in a beeswarm, and mean nothing. Explanation quality is capped by model quality. Validate first (Chapter 13); explain second. If the validation score is poor, the explanation is a description of noise.

Correlated features spread credit

When worst radius, worst perimeter and worst area are near-duplicates, SHAP splits their credit (symmetry axiom), permutation importance under-counts each of them, and PDPs extrapolate into impossible combinations. Group correlated features and explain the group, or drop the duplicates before training.

No causal claims

"Utilisation has the biggest SHAP value" means the model leans on it, not that lowering utilisation causes repayment. The model may be reading a proxy for something unmeasured. Causal questions need causal methods — Chapter 22 — and usually an experiment.

Two more subtle traps. First, the background matters: SHAP's base value and every φij depend on what "absent" means, i.e. which background rows fill in the missing features. Explaining a rejected applicant against a background of all applicants gives different numbers than against a background of approved ones — both are legitimate, but they answer different questions ("why not average?" versus "why not approved?"). Say which one you used. Second, explanations can be gamed: research has shown that a model can be built to look innocuous under LIME and SHAP while relying on a protected attribute, by behaving differently on the off-manifold perturbed points those methods generate. If someone has an incentive to hide what a model does, an explanation plot is not an audit. Fairness metrics on real outcomes (next section) are.

Key idea

An explanation describes the model's function, faithfully. Whether that function is good, fair, or causally meaningful are three separate questions that need validation metrics, fairness metrics and causal methods respectively. SHAP tells you what the model does. It never tells you whether the model should.

9. Fairness & bias

Back to the lender. The model does not use gender, ethnicity or age as features — legal insisted. Then someone computes the approval rate by group and finds it is 58% for group A and 44% for group B. How, if the model never saw group membership? Because it saw proxies: postcode, first name, shopping categories, the device used to apply. Any feature correlated with a protected attribute lets the model reconstruct it. Removing the protected column is called fairness through unawareness, and it does not work. The only way to know whether a model treats groups differently is to keep the protected attribute out of the features, keep it in the evaluation data, and measure.

Explain it like I'm 9

Suppose a teacher promises not to look at whether you're a boy or a girl when marking, so she covers the name on each test. But she can still see the handwriting and the colour of the pen — and if girls in that class tend to use purple pens, she might, without meaning to, mark purple-pen tests differently. Hiding the name didn't hide the information. The honest check is simpler: after marking, compare the boys' and girls' scores and see if the difference makes sense.

Three definitions of "fair"

Take a binary decision ŷ (approve = 1), the true outcome y (would repay = 1) and a group attribute G ∈ {A, B}. The three metrics most used in practice each fix a different quantity to be equal across groups:

CriterionRequires, for every group gIn wordsReasonable when…
Demographic parity (statistical parity)P(ŷ = 1 | G = g) equalThe same fraction of each group is approved, regardless of outcomes.Historical outcomes themselves are distrusted (e.g. biased past hiring); you want equal access.
Equal opportunityP(ŷ = 1 | y = 1, G = g) equal (equal TPR)Among people who would repay, each group is approved at the same rate.The cost of a false negative (denying a good customer) is the harm you care about most.
Equalized oddsTPR and FPR equal across groupsEqual opportunity plus: among people who would default, each group is (wrongly) approved at the same rate.Both error types matter and the labels are trusted.
Calibration by groupP(y = 1 | score = s, G = g) equal for all sA score of 0.7 means 70% for everyone.The score itself is shown or used downstream by humans.
The math (optional)
Disparate impact ratio = P(ŷ = 1 | G = B) / P(ŷ = 1 | G = A)   ·   Equal-opportunity gap = TPRA − TPRB

ŷ is the decision, G the group, TPRg = P(ŷ = 1 | y = 1, G = g) the true-positive rate within group g. The US "four-fifths rule" (from employment law, widely borrowed) flags a disparate impact ratio below 0.80 as evidence of adverse impact. Gaps in TPR/FPR have no legal threshold; teams typically set an internal tolerance such as |gap| ≤ 0.05 and monitor it.

You cannot have all of them. A theorem from 2016 (Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan; Chouldechova) shows that if the base rates P(y = 1 | G) differ between groups — and they nearly always do — no classifier short of a perfect one can be calibrated by group and satisfy equalized odds. Demographic parity conflicts with both whenever base rates differ. This is not a technical limitation waiting for a better algorithm; it is arithmetic. So "make the model fair" is not a specification. The team, with legal and the business, must choose which criterion matters for this decision, write it down, and accept that the others will be violated. That choice is a policy decision, and it should be made before anyone looks at the numbers.

# Group fairness metrics on a synthetic loan dataset with a biased proxy feature
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 6000
group = rng.integers(0, 2, n)                        # protected attribute: 0 = group A, 1 = group B
income = rng.normal(50 - 8 * group, 12, n)           # group B has lower income on average (historical inequity)
zip_score = 0.7 * group + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)      # a "neighbourhood" feature that PROXIES the group
credit = rng.normal(0, 1, n)
# true repayment depends on income and credit only, NOT on group
p_repay = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(0.06 * (income - 46) + 1.2 * credit)))
y = (rng.random(n) < p_repay).astype(int)
X = pd.DataFrame({"income": income, "zip_score": zip_score, "credit": credit})   # group itself is NOT a feature

X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te, g_tr, g_te = train_test_split(X, y, group, test_size=0.4, random_state=0)
clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
scores = clf.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]

def group_metrics(y, s, g, thr):
    out = {}
    for name, m in [("A", g == 0), ("B", g == 1)]:
        t = thr[m] if np.ndim(thr) else thr                # thr may be one number or one per row
        yh = (s[m] >= t).astype(int); yt = y[m]
        out[name] = {"approval rate": yh.mean(),
                     "TPR (equal opportunity)": yh[yt == 1].mean(),
                     "FPR": yh[yt == 0].mean(),
                     "precision": yt[yh == 1].mean()}
    return pd.DataFrame(out).round(3)

m = group_metrics(y_te, scores, g_te, thr=0.5)
print(m)
print(f"\ndisparate impact ratio (B/A approval) = {m.loc['approval rate','B'] / m.loc['approval rate','A']:.2f}  (4/5 rule wants >= 0.80)")
print(f"equal-opportunity gap (TPR A - TPR B)  = {m.loc['TPR (equal opportunity)','A'] - m.loc['TPR (equal opportunity)','B']:.3f}")

# Mitigation by post-processing: a separate threshold per group that equalises TPR
thr_b = np.quantile(scores[(g_te == 1) & (y_te == 1)], 1 - m.loc['TPR (equal opportunity)', 'A'])
m2 = group_metrics(y_te, scores, g_te, thr=np.where(g_te == 1, thr_b, 0.5))
print(f"\nper-group threshold for B = {thr_b:.3f}")
print(m2.loc[["approval rate", "TPR (equal opportunity)", "FPR"]])
#                              A      B
# approval rate            0.579  0.441
# TPR (equal opportunity)  0.790  0.680
# FPR                      0.311  0.231
# precision                0.763  0.721
#
# disparate impact ratio (B/A approval) = 0.76  (4/5 rule wants >= 0.80)
# equal-opportunity gap (TPR A - TPR B)  = 0.110
#
# per-group threshold for B = 0.400
#                              A      B
# approval rate            0.579  0.549
# TPR (equal opportunity)  0.790  0.789
# FPR                      0.311  0.338

Notice what the synthetic data was built to show. Repayment truly depends only on income and credit — the "world" is not biased against group B given those. But group B has lower income on average (an inherited inequity) and the model has a proxy (zip_score) that lets it learn the group. Result: a 0.76 disparate impact ratio and an 11-point TPR gap, from a model that never saw the group column. The per-group threshold repairs equal opportunity (0.790 vs 0.789) but pushes group B's FPR up from 0.23 to 0.34 — the impossibility theorem in one table.

Mitigation: four places to intervene

  1. Data. Collect more and better-labelled data for the under-represented group; audit the labels themselves (a "default" label from a biased collections process is a biased label); remove or transform proxy features that have no business justification.
  2. Reweighing / in-processing. Weight training rows so that each (group, label) combination has equal total weight, or add a fairness penalty to the loss (the fairlearn package's ExponentiatedGradient does this for any sklearn classifier). This usually costs a little accuracy and moves the model toward the chosen criterion.
  3. Thresholds per group / post-processing. Keep the scores, choose decision thresholds per group to satisfy the criterion (as above; fairlearn.postprocessing.ThresholdOptimizer). Simple and effective, but it uses the protected attribute at decision time, which is illegal in some domains (US credit, for one) — check with legal first.
  4. Monitoring. Fairness is not a property you verify once. Compute the chosen metrics per group on every batch of production decisions, alert when a gap crosses the tolerance, and include them in the model card. Chapter 21 shows where these checks live in the pipeline.
Common mistake

Reporting fairness on the model's training distribution only. A model can be fair on last year's applicants and unfair on this year's, because the mix of groups, their proxies, or the base rates shifted. Fairness metrics are monitoring metrics, computed on production data with a lag for labels — the same way as accuracy. And never compute them on a test set so small that a 5-point gap is inside the noise: with 200 people in group B a TPR "gap" of 0.05 has a standard error of about 0.04. Report confidence intervals.

At work: the fairness conversation

Do not walk into the room with "the model is biased." Walk in with: the criterion you propose and why it fits this decision, the measured gap with its confidence interval, the three mitigation options with their accuracy cost, and a recommendation. Fairness decisions are made by product, legal and the business; your job is to make the trade-off visible and quantified. And if the mitigation you need is illegal in your jurisdiction, say so early — it changes the whole plan.

10. Model cards & the stakeholder recipe

The model card

A model card is a one-to-three-page document that travels with the model: what it is for, what it was trained on, how it performs — overall and per group — where it must not be used, and how it was explained. Proposed by Mitchell et al. in 2019 and now expected by most AI-governance frameworks (and by the EU AI Act for high-risk systems). The minimal card:

SectionWhat goes in it
Model detailsOwner, version, date, algorithm, features (with sources), training-data window, license/approval status.
Intended useThe decision it supports, the users, and explicit out-of-scope uses ("not for applicants under 21; not for business loans").
DataWhere the labels come from, known gaps, how the protected attributes were handled (held out of features, kept for evaluation).
PerformancePrimary metric on validation and test, with confidence intervals; performance sliced by group, region, time period; the accuracy cost of the interpretability/fairness choices made.
ExplanationsGlobal importance (permutation + mean |SHAP|), the PDP/ICE for the top features, the background dataset used, the reason-code mapping for local explanations.
FairnessThe criterion chosen and why, the measured gaps with intervals, mitigations applied, the monitoring plan.
Limitations & risksKnown failure modes (found by ICE, counterfactuals, error analysis), drift risks, what to do when the model is uncertain.

The "explain it to a stakeholder" recipe

You have thirty minutes with the head of credit. She does not want a beeswarm. Here is what has worked for me, repeatedly, across industries:

  1. One global bar chart (permutation importance, top 8). Ask: "does this match your intuition?" If the top feature surprises the domain expert, stop and investigate — you have either found leakage or found something valuable, and you need to know which before going further.
  2. Three PDP+ICE plots for the top three features. Show direction and shape. This is where "risk falls as utilisation rises above 90%" gets caught as an artefact of the 40 rows in that range. Mark the data density.
  3. Two waterfalls: a clear approval and a clear decline. Translate each bar into a reason code. Then show the counterfactual for the decline. This is the moment the room starts trusting the model, because it is reasoning the way they do.
  4. One fairness panel and one limitations list. Gaps with confidence intervals, the accuracy cost of constraints, the out-of-scope uses, and the known weak segments.
  5. Leave the beeswarm and the interaction values in the appendix. They are for you and the reviewers, not for the decision-maker. If she asks a question they answer, pull them out.
Explain it like I'm 9

When you show your parents a science project, you don't start with the messy notebook. You show them: what you were trying to find out, the one chart that answers it, an example that makes it real, and the part that didn't work. The notebook is there if they ask. Explaining a model is the same: lead with the answer to the question they have, keep the evidence one page behind it.

At work

Build the explanation pack as code, not as screenshots. A script that regenerates the four panels from the current model and a fixed background set takes an afternoon, and it means every retrain (Chapter 21) automatically produces an updated pack — and a diff against the previous one. "The top feature changed from utilisation to income between v3 and v4" is exactly the sentence a model-risk reviewer wants to see, and the one that catches a broken pipeline before the customers do.

Quiz

Check your understanding

1. Impurity-based feature importance from a random forest ranks a random ID column as the third most important feature. What is the most likely explanation?

A column with thousands of unique values can always reduce impurity somewhere by chance on the training data, so impurity importance over-credits it. Permutation importance on validation data would show it near zero. (Leakage is possible but would show up in permutation importance too.)

2. A PDP for "hours of exercise" is flat, but the ICE curves fan out: half rise steeply and half fall. What should you conclude?

A flat average of strongly non-flat individual curves is the signature of an interaction. Find the partner feature with a 2-D PDP or a coloured SHAP dependence plot.

3. In the taxi game, v(A)=6, v(B)=12, v(C)=42 and every coalition containing C costs 42. Which axiom guarantees that the Shapley shares 2 + 5 + 35 sum to exactly 42?

Efficiency: the shares always sum to v(N) − v(∅). For a model this becomes base value + ∑ SHAP = prediction — the property that makes waterfall plots exact.

4. TreeSHAP on a LightGBM classifier gives a feature a SHAP value of +1.1 for one customer. What does that mean?

Gradient-boosting classifiers are explained in their raw margin space, log-odds. e1.1 ≈ 3.0, so the odds tripled. It says nothing about global importance and nothing causal.

5. A lender's model never uses gender as a feature, yet the approval rate is 20 points lower for women. Which statement is correct?

"Fairness through unawareness" fails because of proxies. Keep the protected attribute out of the features but in the evaluation data, and measure. Equalizing approval rates (demographic parity) generally conflicts with equalized odds when base rates differ.

6. A declined applicant asks what they could do to be approved. Which explanation format is most appropriate?

A counterfactual ("reduce utilisation below 60%") is local, actionable and easy to understand. It must be restricted to features the person can actually change and must never suggest changing a protected attribute.

Exercises

Exercise 20.1 — Break impurity importance

Take the first code block. Add a column noise_id = np.arange(len(X)) (a unique integer per row) to X before splitting, retrain the GBM, and print both importance rankings again. Where does noise_id land in each ranking? Then replace it with a binary noise column (rng.integers(0, 2, len(X))) and repeat. Explain the difference.

Show answer

The unique-integer column typically appears in the impurity top-10 (many split points let it reduce impurity on training rows) while its permutation importance is ≈ 0 ± noise on validation data. The binary noise column ranks near the bottom of both: with only one possible split it cannot fit noise. This is the cardinality bias in action. Bonus: check the validation accuracy — it barely changes, because the GBM's learning rate and depth limit how much noise it can memorise.

Exercise 20.2 — A positive interaction in the taxi

Change the game so that Anna and Ben together are worth more than the sum of their solo values: keep v(A)=6, v(B)=12, but set v(AB)=30 (they carpool to a shared destination), and keep all coalitions with C at 42. Compute the Shapley values by hand using the ordering table, check with the widget, and then explain in one sentence why C's share fell even though nothing about C changed.

Show answer

Orderings — A: (6, 6, 18, 0, 0, 0)/6 = 5; B: (24, 0, 12, 12, 0, 0)/6 = 8; C: (12, 36, 12, 30, 42, 42)/6 = 29. Sum = 42 ✓. C's share fell because in the orderings where A and B arrive first, the coalition {A,B} already covers 30 of the 42, so C's marginal contribution there drops from 30 to 12. Shapley values are about marginal contributions, so raising what others achieve without you lowers your credit — the same reason a feature's SHAP value shrinks when a correlated feature is added.

Exercise 20.3 — Build the pack for a model at work

Take a model you or your team have in production (or the best model from your Chapter 13 tuning). Produce the four-panel pack of Figure 20.9: permutation importance (validation data, 20 repeats, with error bars), PDP+ICE for the top three features with a rug of data density, two SHAP waterfalls (one clear positive, one clear negative) translated into plain-language reasons, and the fairness table for one protected attribute or a plausible proxy (region, age band). Present it to a colleague who does not know the model and write down every question they ask that the pack could not answer. Those questions are the next version.

Show answer

There is no single answer, but check yourself against the failure modes in this chapter: Did you compute importance on validation data? Did you draw ICE under the PDP and mark where the data is thin? Are the waterfall units log-odds, and did you translate them? Did you state the background dataset? Did you report the fairness gap with a confidence interval and name the criterion? If the top feature surprised the domain expert, did you treat it as a leakage suspect before treating it as an insight?

Chapter summary

  • Explanations answer specific questions: global ("what does it rely on?"), local ("why this one?"), counterfactual ("what would flip it?"), fairness ("does it treat groups differently?"). Name the question first.
  • Impurity importance is free and biased (cardinality, training data, correlated features). Permutation importance on validation data with your real metric, 20+ repeats and error bars, is the honest default.
  • PDPs are averages of ICE curves; always draw both. Fanning ICE curves mean an interaction. Mark data density — the PDP extrapolates.
  • Shapley values split credit fairly by averaging marginal contributions over all orderings; efficiency guarantees base + ∑SHAP = prediction. Exact is exponential; TreeSHAP is exact and fast for trees, KernelSHAP is slow and model-agnostic.
  • Beeswarm = importance + spread + direction; bar = importance only; waterfall = one prediction; dependence plot = PDP's honest cousin. Classifier SHAP is in log-odds.
  • LIME is a local linear surrogate and is unstable; counterfactuals are the most actionable customer-facing explanation; surrogate trees need a fidelity number.
  • Explanations of a bad model are confidently wrong; correlated features spread credit; nothing here is causal.
  • Fairness through unawareness fails because of proxies. Choose demographic parity, equal opportunity or equalized odds deliberately — you cannot have them all when base rates differ — mitigate in data, training, thresholds or post-processing, and monitor.
  • Ship a model card and a four-panel stakeholder pack, regenerated by code on every retrain.
