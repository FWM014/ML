Linear Regression & Gradient Descent

The first real model, and the engine that trains almost every other one. Linear regression teaches you what a model, a loss and a fit actually are; gradient descent teaches you how a computer finds the best parameters when it cannot simply solve for them.

1. The model: a line through the data

Our running example: you work for a property portal and want to predict the asking price of a flat (in thousands of euros) from its size in square metres. You have a few hundred past listings. Linear regression makes the simplest possible claim: price is roughly a straight line in size.

ŷ = w · x + b
The math (optional)

x is the input feature (size in m²). ŷ ("y-hat") is the model's prediction of the target (price in k€); the hat marks it as a prediction, not the truth y. w is the weight or slope: how many k€ the prediction rises for each extra square metre. b is the bias or intercept: the predicted price at x = 0, which is rarely meaningful on its own but is needed to position the line. The two numbers w and b are the model's parameters: learning means choosing them.

Explain it like I'm 9

Imagine a pile of dots on a page and a ruler. You can slide the ruler up and down (that's b) and tilt it (that's w). "Fitting a line" is finding the ruler position where the dots are, on average, as close to the ruler as possible. Once the ruler is placed, predicting is just looking at where the ruler is above any new size.

Many features

Real listings have more than size: number of rooms, floor, distance to the metro, year built. With p features the model becomes a weighted sum, one weight per feature, plus the bias. Geometrically it is no longer a line but a flat surface (a plane in 3-D, a "hyperplane" beyond), which is why linear regression scales to hundreds of features without changing character.

ŷ = w1x1 + w2x2 + … + wpxp + b = w · x + b
The math (optional)

xj is the value of feature j for one example (x1 = size, x2 = rooms, …). wj is its weight: how much the prediction changes when xj increases by one unit while every other feature stays fixed. p is the number of features. The bold w and x are vectors of length p, and the dot w · x is their dot product (Chapter 3): multiply pairwise, add up. For a whole dataset we stack the n rows into a matrix X of shape (n, p) and write all predictions at once as ŷ = Xw + b, which is one line of NumPy: X @ w + b.

Key idea

"Linear" means linear in the parameters, not necessarily in the raw inputs. ŷ = w1·size + w2·size² is still linear regression, because you can compute size² as a new column and the model is still a weighted sum of columns. Section 7 uses exactly this trick to fit curves.

2. The loss: residuals and squared error

To choose between two candidate lines we need a number that says how wrong each one is. Start with one listing: it was priced at y = 450 k€ and the line predicts ŷ = 424 k€. The difference y − ŷ = 26 k€ is the residual: the vertical gap between the dot and the line. A good line has small residuals everywhere.

Why squared?

Why not just average the residuals? Because positive and negative gaps cancel: a line that is 100 k€ too high on half the flats and 100 k€ too low on the other half would score a perfect zero. We need every error to count as positive. Two obvious choices are the absolute value |y − ŷ| and the square (y − ŷ)². Squaring wins by default for three reasons: it is smooth (differentiable everywhere, which gradient descent needs and which gives the closed form in Section 3); it punishes large errors much harder than small ones (an error of 20 costs 400, an error of 2 costs 4), which is usually what a business wants; and under the assumption of Gaussian noise it is the statistically optimal choice. Its weakness is the flip side of the second point: a single wild outlier can drag the whole line. Section 8 gives you MAE for when that matters.

MSE(w, b) = (1/n) ∑i=1n (yi − ŷi)² = (1/n) ∑i=1n (yi − w·xi − b)²
The math (optional)

n is the number of training examples; i indexes them. yi is the true price of listing i and ŷi = w·xi + b the prediction. The ∑ adds the squared residuals over all listings and 1/n averages them. The result, the mean squared error, is in squared units (k€²), which is why we often report its square root (Section 8). Crucially, MSE is written as a function of w and b, not of the data: the data are fixed, and we search over the parameters. That is what makes it a loss function.

3. Fitting I: the closed form

For MSE and a linear model, the best parameters can be written down directly. Set the derivative of the loss to zero, solve, and you get the normal equation. No iteration, no learning rate, one line of linear algebra.

θ* = (XTX)−1 XT y
The math (optional)

X is the (n, p+1) design matrix: the feature matrix with an extra column of ones on the left so that the bias b becomes just another weight. θ ("theta") is the vector of all parameters [b, w1, …, wp], and the star marks the optimum. XT is the transpose (rows become columns), so XTX is a small (p+1)×(p+1) matrix summarising how features co-vary, and XTy a vector summarising how each feature co-varies with the target. The −1 is a matrix inverse. In practice you never compute the inverse; you solve the linear system XTX θ = XTy with np.linalg.solve or lstsq, which is faster and numerically safer.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 50
size = rng.uniform(40, 160, n)                      # flat size in m²
price = 50 + 2.5 * size + rng.normal(0, 25, n)      # true w = 2.5, b = 50, plus noise (k€)

# design matrix: a column of ones (for b) next to the feature
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), size])             # shape (50, 2)
theta = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ price)       # normal equation, solved stably
b, w = theta
print(f"b = {b:.2f}, w = {w:.3f}")
# b = 39.58, w = 2.609            <- near the true 50 and 2.5; noise explains the gap

pred = X @ theta
mse = np.mean((price - pred) ** 2)
print(f"MSE = {mse:.1f}  RMSE = {np.sqrt(mse):.1f} k€")
# MSE = 620.9  RMSE = 24.9 k€     <- ≈ the 25 k€ noise we injected: as good as it gets
Deep dive: where the normal equation comes from 5 min

Write the loss in matrix form: L(θ) = (1/n)‖y − Xθ‖², where ‖·‖² is the sum of squares of a vector. Expand: (1/n)(yTy − 2θTXTy + θTXTXθ). Take the gradient with respect to θ (the vector of partial derivatives): ∇L = (1/n)(−2XTy + 2XTXθ). At the minimum the gradient is zero, so XTXθ = XTy, and if XTX is invertible, θ* = (XTX)−1XTy. The same gradient, without setting it to zero, is exactly what gradient descent follows in Section 4: the closed form and gradient descent are two ways of using the same derivative. XTX fails to be invertible when a feature is a perfect linear combination of others (two columns in different units for the same thing, or a full set of one-hot columns plus an intercept); that is the "singular matrix" error, and Ridge regression (Chapter 7) is the standard cure.

So why do we need gradient descent at all?

Three reasons, and they explain most of the rest of the book. Cost: solving the normal equation costs roughly p³ operations plus n·p² to form XTX; with a million features (text, one-hot ids) that is impossible, while gradient descent costs n·p per pass. Memory and streaming: gradient descent can update from a few rows at a time, so the dataset never has to fit in memory. Generality: the closed form exists only for squared loss on a linear model. Logistic regression (Chapter 6), neural networks (Chapter 15) and boosting (Chapter 10) have no closed form; they are all trained by some flavour of gradient descent. Learn it here, on a problem where you can check the answer.

4. Fitting II: gradient descent

Explain it like I'm 9

You are standing on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the bottom of the valley. You cannot see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a step downhill, feel again, step again. The slope you feel is the gradient; the size of your step is the learning rate; and the valley floor is the set of parameters with the lowest loss.

The loss is a bowl

Plot MSE against w (holding b fixed) and you get a parabola: a U-shape with one lowest point. Plot it against w and b together and you get a bowl. This shape is not luck; the loss is a sum of squares of terms that are linear in the parameters, which is always a convex quadratic. Convexity is the guarantee that matters: there is exactly one valley, so walking downhill always ends at the global best. Neural networks lose this guarantee, but the walking algorithm stays the same.

The derivatives, step by step

To feel the slope we need the partial derivatives of MSE with respect to w and b: how much the loss changes if we nudge one parameter while holding the other fixed. This is the one derivation in Part I worth following line by line, because the same chain-rule pattern reappears in every backpropagation (Chapter 15).

The math (optional)

Start with L(w, b) = (1/n) ∑i (yi − w·xi − b)². Call the bracket ei = yi − w·xi − b (the residual).

Step 1, chain rule on the square. The derivative of ei² with respect to anything is 2ei times the derivative of ei.

Step 2, derivative of the residual. ∂ei/∂w = −xi (the only term containing w is −w·xi). ∂ei/∂b = −1.

Step 3, assemble and average.

∂L/∂w = −(2/n) ∑i (yi − ŷi) · xi   ∂L/∂b = −(2/n) ∑i (yi − ŷi)

Reading them: ∂L/∂b is just −2 × the average residual. If predictions are on average too low (positive residuals), the derivative is negative, so "stepping against the gradient" raises b. ∂L/∂w is the same idea weighted by x: a residual on a large flat says more about the slope than a residual on a small one. The minus signs and the 2 are bookkeeping; the pattern error × input is the heart of it and is exactly what neural networks compute layer by layer.

The update rule

w ← w − α · ∂L/∂w   b ← b − α · ∂L/∂b
The math (optional)

The arrow means "replace with". α (alpha, sometimes η or lr) is the learning rate: a small positive number that scales the step. The minus sign is the whole algorithm: the gradient points uphill (the direction of fastest increase), so we move the opposite way. Repeating the two lines is gradient descent. One pass through the whole training set is an epoch; with the full-batch version below, one epoch is one update, so people say "iterations" and "epochs" interchangeably here. They differ once you use mini-batches (Section 5).

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 50
size = rng.uniform(40, 160, n)
price = 50 + 2.5 * size + rng.normal(0, 25, n)

# scale the feature first (GD needs it, see Section 5): mean 0, std 1
x = (size - size.mean()) / size.std()
y = price

w, b = 0.0, 0.0            # start anywhere
lr = 0.1                   # learning rate α
for epoch in range(1, 41):
    y_hat = w * x + b                        # forward: predictions with the current parameters
    err = y_hat - y                          # ŷ − y for every row (note the sign: −residual)
    dw = 2 * np.mean(err * x)                # ∂L/∂w  (the minus signs cancel with err = ŷ − y)
    db = 2 * np.mean(err)                    # ∂L/∂b
    w -= lr * dw                             # step downhill
    b -= lr * db
    if epoch in (1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 40):
        print(f"epoch {epoch:2d}  loss = {np.mean(err**2):9.1f}  w = {w:6.2f}  b = {b:6.2f}")
# epoch  1  loss =  104354.6  w =  18.25  b =  61.78
# epoch  2  loss =   67010.4  w =  32.85  b = 111.20
# epoch  5  loss =   18024.5  w =  61.35  b = 207.67
# epoch 10  loss =    2489.6  w =  81.45  b = 275.71
# epoch 20  loss =     642.4  w =  90.20  b = 305.32
# epoch 40  loss =     620.9  w =  91.24  b = 308.84      <- same 620.9 as the closed form

# w is "k€ per standard deviation of size"; convert back to k€ per m²
print(f"slope in original units = {w / size.std():.3f} k€/m²")
# slope in original units = 2.609 k€/m²                   <- identical to the normal equation

Read the loss column: it falls fast at first (steep slope, big steps), then slowly (the valley floor is flat, so the gradient, and therefore the step, shrinks). This automatic slowing down is why gradient descent settles instead of overshooting, provided the learning rate is sane.

Deep dive: the MSE gradient, line by line, with numbers 7 min
Explain it like I'm 9

Three flats, one ruler. For each flat, measure how far the dot is above the ruler (the leftover). To learn how to tilt the ruler, ask each flat "how much would you like the slope to change?" — a big flat with a big leftover shouts loudest, because tilting moves the ruler a lot at the far end. Add up the shouts and you have the gradient for the slope. For sliding the ruler up and down, every flat shouts the same amount per unit of leftover.

Precisely. Take three listings with sizes x = (1, 2, 4) (in hundreds of m²) and prices y = (3, 5, 9) (in hundreds of k€), and the current guess w = 1, b = 0.5. Work every line of Section 4's derivation with these numbers.

The math (optional)

Predictions: ŷ = w·x + b = (1.5, 2.5, 4.5). Residuals: r = y − ŷ = (1.5, 2.5, 4.5). Loss: L = (1/3)(1.5² + 2.5² + 4.5²) = (2.25 + 6.25 + 20.25)/3 = 9.583.

∂L/∂w = −(2/n) ∑ ri xi = −(2/3)(1.5·1 + 2.5·2 + 4.5·4) = −(2/3)(24.5) = −16.333. ∂L/∂b = −(2/n) ∑ ri = −(2/3)(8.5) = −5.667. Both are negative: predictions are too low everywhere, so increasing either parameter lowers the loss, and the slope gets the bigger push because the big flat's residual is multiplied by its size.

Matrix form. With the design matrix X (a column of ones, then x) and θ = (b, w), the same two numbers are ∇L = (2/n) XT(Xθ − y). Read it right to left: Xθ − y is the vector of (ŷ − y), XT multiplies it by the ones column (summing the residuals → ∂L/∂b) and by the size column (summing residual × size → ∂L/∂w), and 2/n is the bookkeeping constant. This one line is the whole of Section 9's from-scratch code and, with more columns, the gradient for any number of features.

One step with α = 0.05: b ← 0.5 − 0.05·(−5.667) = 0.783, w ← 1 − 0.05·(−16.333) = 1.817. The loss drops from 9.58 to 0.47 in a single step, because the starting point was far from the valley and the slope was steep.

# The MSE gradient on a 3-row example: per-parameter formulas, the matrix form, and one step
import numpy as np
x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 4.0]); y = np.array([3.0, 5.0, 9.0])     # 3 flats: size (100 m²), price (100 k€)
w, b = 1.0, 0.5                                                   # current guess
y_hat = w * x + b                                                 # [1.5, 2.5, 4.5]
r = y - y_hat                                                     # residuals [1.5, 2.5, 4.5]
loss = np.mean(r ** 2)
dw = -2 / 3 * (r * x).sum()                                       # -(2/n) Σ (y-ŷ)·x
db = -2 / 3 * r.sum()                                             # -(2/n) Σ (y-ŷ)
print(f"loss = {loss:.4f}   dL/dw = {dw:.4f}   dL/db = {db:.4f}")

X = np.column_stack([np.ones(3), x]); theta = np.array([b, w])   # design matrix, theta = [b, w]
grad = 2 / 3 * X.T @ (X @ theta - y)                              # (2/n) Xᵀ(Xθ − y): the same two numbers
print("matrix form   [dL/db, dL/dw] =", grad.round(4))

alpha = 0.05
theta_new = theta - alpha * grad                                  # one gradient-descent step
print(f"after one step: b = {theta_new[0]:.4f}, w = {theta_new[1]:.4f}, "
      f"loss = {np.mean((X @ theta_new - y) ** 2):.4f}  (was {loss:.4f})")
# loss = 9.5833   dL/dw = -16.3333   dL/db = -5.6667
# matrix form   [dL/db, dL/dw] = [ -5.6667 -16.3333]
# after one step: b = 0.7833, w = 1.8167, loss = 0.4676  (was 9.5833)

The pattern to carry forward is error × input, averaged. Logistic regression (Chapter 6) has exactly this gradient with ŷ replaced by a probability; a neural network (Chapter 15) applies it layer by layer, where the "input" of a layer is the output of the one before. If you can reproduce the three numbers above by hand, you understand backpropagation's arithmetic already.

The learning rate: too small, too large, just right

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
size = rng.uniform(40, 160, 50); price = 50 + 2.5 * size + rng.normal(0, 25, 50)
x = (size - size.mean()) / size.std(); y = price

def gd(lr, epochs=30):
    w, b = 0.0, 0.0
    for _ in range(epochs):
        err = w * x + b - y
        w -= lr * 2 * np.mean(err * x)
        b -= lr * 2 * np.mean(err)
    return np.mean((w * x + b - y) ** 2)

for lr in (0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1.05):
    print(f"lr = {lr:<6} loss after 30 epochs = {gd(lr):>14.1f}")
# lr = 0.001  loss after 30 epochs =        92613.4     <- barely moved
# lr = 0.01   loss after 30 epochs =        31487.2     <- moving, needs 10× more epochs
# lr = 0.1    loss after 30 epochs =          621.0     <- converged
# lr = 0.5    loss after 30 epochs =          620.9     <- converged fastest
# lr = 1.05   loss after 30 epochs =     31585634.1     <- diverging → inf

step: 0 w: – loss: –
The loss is L(w) = (w − 3)² + 1, so the gradient is 2(w − 3). Step with α = 0.3 and watch the steps shrink as the slope flattens. Then reset, set α ≈ 0.5 (one step lands exactly at the bottom for this parabola), then α = 1.06 and watch it explode. Above α = 1 every step overshoots further than it started.

Convergence: when do you stop?

Three practical stopping rules, usually combined: a fixed budget of epochs (max_iter); a tolerance, stopping when the loss improves by less than tol between epochs; and, for anything that can overfit, early stopping on a validation set (Chapter 7). scikit-learn's SGDRegressor exposes all three. Always plot loss against epoch: a curve that is still falling means "train longer or raise α", a curve that jitters means "lower α or use bigger batches", and a curve that rises means "α is too large" or a bug in the gradient.

Common mistake

Comparing losses across differently scaled targets. A loss of 620 is "good" here only because prices are in k€ with 25 k€ noise; in euros the same fit has loss 620,000,000. Loss values mean nothing on their own; compare them to a baseline (predict the mean) and to the noise level, or use R² (Section 8).

5. Batch, stochastic, mini-batch — and scaling

The code above computes the gradient on all n rows before every step. That is batch gradient descent: exact gradient, smooth path, one update per epoch, and with ten million rows one update costs a full pass through the data. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) goes to the other extreme: it updates after every single row, using that row's gradient, which is a noisy estimate of the true one. Cheap and fast per update, but the path staggers. Mini-batch gradient descent uses a small random batch (32 to 1024 rows) per update, keeps most of the noise reduction, and maps beautifully onto GPUs, which is why it is the universal default in deep learning.

Feature scaling: why the bowl must be round

Look back at the widget in Section 2: nudging w changed the MSE far more than nudging b, because w multiplies sizes in the hundreds. In the loss landscape that means the bowl is stretched: steep in the w direction, shallow in the b direction. A learning rate small enough not to overshoot the steep direction is far too small for the shallow one, so the walker zigzags across the narrow valley and crawls along it. Standardising every feature (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation; Chapter 4's StandardScaler) makes the bowl roughly round, and then one learning rate works for every direction.

Key idea

Scale features for any model trained by gradient descent or that uses distances (linear/logistic regression via SGD, SVMs, kNN, neural networks). The closed-form solution and tree-based models do not care. When in doubt, put a StandardScaler in the Pipeline: it never hurts a linear model and often rescues it.

Deep dive: why scaling changes the gradient-descent path 8 min
Explain it like I'm 9

Imagine walking down into a long, narrow canyon in the fog. The walls are steep (one step sideways and you climb a lot) but the floor slopes gently toward the exit. If you take big steps, you bounce off the walls; if you take steps small enough not to bounce, walking along the gentle floor takes forever. A round bowl has no walls: the same step size works in every direction and you walk straight to the bottom. Scaling the features reshapes the canyon into a bowl.

Precisely. Because MSE is quadratic in the parameters, its curvature is constant: the matrix of second derivatives (the Hessian) is H = (2/n) XTX, and its two eigenvalues are the curvatures along the steepest and the shallowest direction of the bowl. Two facts about gradient descent on a quadratic follow directly.

The math (optional)
stable ⇔ α < 2 / λmax error along direction k shrinks by |1 − α λk| per step

λmax and λmin are the largest and smallest curvature (eigenvalues of H); α is the learning rate. Along any direction the distance to the minimum is multiplied by (1 − αλk) each step, so it shrinks only if that factor has magnitude below 1, which for the steepest direction requires α < 2/λmax. With α pinned near that limit, the shallow direction shrinks by 1 − αλmin ≈ 1 − 2λmin/λmax per step, so the number of steps needed grows in proportion to the condition number κ = λmax/λmin. Round bowl: κ = 1, one step can land at the bottom (that is the α = 0.5 case in the widget). Canyon: κ in the hundred-thousands, and the count of epochs is in the hundred-thousands too.

# The curvature of the bowl in each direction fixes the largest safe learning rate and the speed of descent
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
size = rng.uniform(40, 160, 50); price = 50 + 2.5 * size + rng.normal(0, 25, 50)

def analyse(name, x):
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])
    H = 2 * X.T @ X / len(x)                          # Hessian of MSE: constant, because the loss is quadratic
    lam_min, lam_max = np.linalg.eigvalsh(H)          # curvature along the shallow and the steep direction
    alpha_max = 2 / lam_max                           # any larger learning rate diverges along the steep direction
    alpha = 0.9 * alpha_max                           # a safe choice
    shrink = abs(1 - alpha * lam_min)                 # per-epoch factor on the error along the shallow direction
    epochs = int(np.ceil(np.log(1e-3) / np.log(shrink)))   # epochs to cut that error by 1000x
    print(f"{name:18s} curvatures {lam_min:9.4f} / {lam_max:10.1f}  ratio {lam_max/lam_min:8.0f}  "
          f"max safe α {alpha_max:.1e}  epochs to converge ≈ {epochs:,}")

analyse("raw size (m²)", size)
analyse("standardised size", (size - size.mean()) / size.std())
# raw size (m²)      curvatures    0.2060 /    23757.1  ratio   115352  max safe α 8.4e-05  epochs to converge ≈ 442,675
# standardised size  curvatures    2.0000 /        2.0  ratio        1  max safe α 1.0e+00  epochs to converge ≈ 31

With size in raw square metres the two curvatures differ by a factor of 115,000: the intercept direction is nearly flat (λ ≈ 0.2) while the slope direction is a cliff (λ ≈ 24,000), because a one-unit change in w moves predictions by up to 160 k€ while a one-unit change in b moves them by 1. The largest learning rate that does not diverge is about 8 × 10−5, and at that rate the intercept needs roughly 440,000 epochs to settle. Standardising the feature makes XTX/n the identity, both curvatures equal 2, α = 0.9 is safe, and 31 epochs suffice. Same data, same algorithm, a 14,000-fold difference in work, and it is the reason Section 4's code standardised size before the first epoch.

Key idea

Two features on different scales always produce an elongated bowl, and the elongation is set by the ratio of their scales squared. Standardising is not a stylistic choice for gradient-based models; it decides whether they converge at all. Adaptive optimisers such as Adam (Chapter 16) rescale each direction automatically, which is one reason they are popular for neural networks, but a StandardScaler in the pipeline remains the first thing to reach for.

6. Many features: reading the coefficients

The reason linear regression survives in a world of gradient-boosted trees is that its coefficients mean something. Used carefully, they answer the business question "what drives this?" Used carelessly, they mislead. Here are the rules.

Explain it like I'm 9

Suppose taller kids in your class also tend to be older, and you want to know whether height alone makes someone run faster. You would compare kids of the same age and see whether the taller one is faster. A coefficient in multiple regression does exactly that: it compares examples that match on all the other features and reports the effect of this one. That is what "holding the others constant" means, and why a coefficient can even flip sign once other features join the model.

  1. Units. A coefficient's unit is "target unit per feature unit". wsize = 2.5 means +2.5 k€ per extra m². If you re-express size in cm², the coefficient becomes 0.00025 and nothing about the model changed. Never compare raw coefficients across features with different units.
  2. "Holding the others constant". In a multiple regression, wrooms is the effect of one more room for a flat of the same size, floor and location. That is why wrooms can be negative even though bigger flats with more rooms cost more: at fixed size, more rooms means smaller rooms. Read every coefficient with the phrase "at fixed values of everything else".
  3. Standardised coefficients. To compare importance across features, fit on standardised inputs. Then each coefficient is "change in target per one standard deviation of the feature", and their absolute sizes are comparable. The intercept becomes the mean of y.
  4. Correlated features share credit. If two features move together (size and rooms), the model can hand the effect to either, and small data changes flip the split. Coefficients are then individually unstable even when predictions are fine. Ridge (Chapter 7) stabilises this; SHAP (Chapter 20) explains it.
  5. Association, not causation. "Flats near the metro cost 40 k€ more" does not mean building a metro adds 40 k€. Chapter 22 covers when a coefficient can be read causally.
wjstd = wj · σj
The math (optional)

wj is the coefficient on the raw feature j, σj the standard deviation of that feature in the training data, and wjstd the coefficient you would get by fitting the same model on the standardised feature. It says: "if feature j moves by one typical amount (one σ), the prediction moves by wjstd". The code below confirms the identity numerically: the s1 coefficient −792.2 on the raw scale becomes −37.7 on the standardised scale because σs1 ≈ 0.0476.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

data = load_diabetes()                  # 442 patients, 10 features, target = disease progression
X, y = data.data, data.target

raw = LinearRegression().fit(X, y)                  # raw: "per unit of feature j, others fixed"
Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)
std = LinearRegression().fit(Xs, y)                 # standardised: "per 1 σ of feature j"

table = (pd.DataFrame({"feature": data.feature_names, "raw_coef": raw.coef_, "std_coef": std.coef_})
           .assign(abs_std=lambda d: d["std_coef"].abs())
           .sort_values("abs_std", ascending=False)
           .drop(columns="abs_std"))
print(table.round(1).to_string(index=False))
# feature  raw_coef  std_coef
#      s1    -792.2     -37.7      <- s1 and s2 (blood lipids) are highly correlated:
#      s5     751.3      35.7         they take large opposite signs and share the credit
#     bmi     519.8      24.7
#      s2     476.7      22.7
#      bp     324.4      15.4
#     sex    -239.8     -11.4
#      s4     177.1       8.4
#      s3     101.0       4.8
#      s6      67.6       3.2
#     age     -10.0      -0.5      <- age adds ~nothing once bmi, bp and lipids are known
print(f"intercept (std model) = {std.intercept_:.1f}  = mean(y) = {y.mean():.1f}")
# intercept (std model) = 152.1  = mean(y) = 152.1
print(np.allclose(std.coef_, raw.coef_ * X.std(axis=0)))   # the identity w_std = w_raw · σ
# True

Categorical inputs: one-hot encoding (preview of Chapter 8)

A linear model needs numbers. A column like city ∈ {lyon, paris, nice} cannot be fed as 0/1/2, because that would claim Paris is "one more than Lyon" and Nice "one more than Paris". Instead we one-hot encode: one 0/1 column per category. With an intercept in the model, one category must be dropped as the reference level, otherwise the columns sum to the intercept's column of ones and XTX is singular. Each remaining coefficient then reads "premium over the reference city, at fixed size".

import pandas as pd
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

homes = pd.DataFrame({
    "size":  [60, 80, 100, 120, 70, 90, 110, 130],
    "city":  ["lyon", "paris", "lyon", "paris", "nice", "nice", "paris", "lyon"],
    "price": [180, 420, 290, 610, 240, 300, 560, 380],
})
X = pd.get_dummies(homes[["size", "city"]], columns=["city"], drop_first=True, dtype=float)
print(X.head(3))                     # city -> two 0/1 columns; "lyon" = dropped reference level
#    size  city_nice  city_paris
# 0    60        0.0         0.0
# 1    80        0.0         1.0
# 2   100        0.0         0.0

model = LinearRegression().fit(X, homes["price"])
for name, c in zip(X.columns, model.coef_):
    print(f"{name:12s} {c:8.1f}")
# size              3.3      k€ per m², at fixed city
# city_nice        42.0      k€ premium of Nice over Lyon, at fixed size
# city_paris      224.5      k€ premium of Paris over Lyon, at fixed size
print(f"{'intercept':12s} {model.intercept_:8.1f}")
# intercept       -37.7      a 0 m² flat in Lyon: meaningless alone, needed to place the plane
At work

In production code use OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore") inside a ColumnTransformer rather than get_dummies: it remembers the category list from training, so a city that appears only at prediction time does not silently change the column layout (error #2 in Chapter 4's table). get_dummies is fine for a quick look.

7. Curves, overfitting and assumptions

Polynomial features: fitting curves with a linear model

If price rises faster than linearly with size, add the column size² (and maybe size³) and fit as usual. PolynomialFeatures(degree=d) generates every power and cross-product up to degree d. The model is still linear in its weights; only the inputs got richer. This is powerful and dangerous in equal measure, because a degree-15 polynomial can pass through every training point exactly, memorising noise instead of learning the trend. That is overfitting, and Chapter 7 is entirely about it; here is your first look.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures, StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
def f(x): return np.sin(2.2 * x)                     # the true (non-linear) relationship
x_tr = rng.uniform(-2, 2, 20);  y_tr = f(x_tr) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, 20)    # 20 noisy train points
x_te = rng.uniform(-2, 2, 200); y_te = f(x_te) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, 200)  # fresh test points

for degree in (1, 3, 15):
    model = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(degree), StandardScaler(), LinearRegression())
    model.fit(x_tr[:, None], y_tr)                       # [:, None]: (20,) -> (20, 1) matrix
    tr = mean_squared_error(y_tr, model.predict(x_tr[:, None]))
    te = mean_squared_error(y_te, model.predict(x_te[:, None]))
    print(f"degree {degree:2d}:  train MSE = {tr:.3f}   test MSE = {te:8.3f}")
# degree  1:  train MSE = 0.676   test MSE =    0.556
# degree  3:  train MSE = 0.167   test MSE =    0.179     <- noise variance 0.09: near the floor
# degree 15:  train MSE = 0.041   test MSE = 1392.103     <- best on train, catastrophic on test

Assumptions, and how to check them with residual plots

Linear regression is honest about what it assumes; most models are not. The classical assumptions are: linearity (the true relationship is a weighted sum, possibly of transformed features); independence of the errors (one flat's surprise tells you nothing about another's, violated by time series and grouped data); homoscedasticity (the noise has the same spread everywhere, violated when expensive flats have bigger errors); and normal residuals (needed for confidence intervals and p-values, not for the fit itself). The fit and the predictions survive mild violations; the interpretations and the uncertainty estimates do not. You check all of them with one habit: plot the residuals.

import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")                                   # omit in a notebook
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

X, y = load_diabetes(return_X_y=True)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=0)
model = LinearRegression().fit(X_tr, y_tr)
pred = model.predict(X_te)
resid = y_te - pred                                      # always on held-out data

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(13, 4))
ax[0].scatter(pred, resid, s=14); ax[0].axhline(0, color="k", lw=1)
ax[0].set(title="residuals vs fitted (want: flat cloud)", xlabel="predicted", ylabel="residual")
ax[1].hist(resid, bins=25); ax[1].set(title="residual histogram (want: bell-ish)", xlabel="residual")
ax[2].scatter(y_te, pred, s=14); lim = [y.min(), y.max()]; ax[2].plot(lim, lim, "k--", lw=1)
ax[2].set(title="predicted vs actual (want: on the diagonal)", xlabel="actual", ylabel="predicted")
fig.tight_layout(); fig.savefig("residual_checks.png", dpi=110)

print(f"mean residual = {resid.mean():.2f}   std = {resid.std():.1f}")
# mean residual = -3.72   std = 56.3                     (exactly 0 only on training data)
print(f"largest |residual| = {np.abs(resid).max():.0f}  vs RMSE = {np.sqrt(np.mean(resid**2)):.1f}")
# largest |residual| = 162  vs RMSE = 56.4
Explain it like I'm 9

After you draw your ruler line, look at the leftovers: how far each dot is above or below the ruler. If the leftovers look like confetti thrown at random, the ruler was the right tool. If they make a smile shape, the dots were really on a curve. If they fan out like a trumpet, the dots get wilder on one side, and the ruler is more trustworthy on the narrow end than the wide one.

8. Metrics: MSE, RMSE, MAE, R²

You trained on MSE; you do not have to report MSE. Four numbers cover almost every regression conversation at work.

RMSE = √MSE   MAE = (1/n) ∑i |yi − ŷi|   R² = 1 − ∑i(yi − ŷi)² / ∑i(yi − ȳ)²
The math (optional)

RMSE (root mean squared error) is the square root of MSE, which brings it back to the target's units (k€ instead of k€²); it is roughly "a typical error, weighted toward the big ones", and for Gaussian noise it estimates the noise standard deviation. MAE (mean absolute error) averages |residual| instead: "a typical error, every error counted equally". Because it does not square, one outlier contributes its size, not its size squared, so MAE ≤ RMSE always, and a large gap between them tells you the errors have a heavy tail. R² compares your squared error to that of the dumbest model, predicting the mean ȳ for everyone: R² = 1 means perfect, 0 means no better than the mean, and negative means worse than the mean, which happens on test data more often than people expect. It is unit-free, so it lets you compare a price model to a demand model, but a "good" R² depends entirely on the domain: 0.35 is respectable for medical outcomes and terrible for a physics sensor.

MetricUnitsSensitive to outliers?Prefer it when
MSEtarget²verytraining (smooth gradient); rarely for reporting
RMSEtargetverylarge errors are disproportionately costly (stock-outs, safety margins); comparing to noise level

Common mistake

Reporting R² on the training set. A degree-15 polynomial has training R² ≈ 0.94 and test R² deeply negative (Figure 5.7). Every metric in this chapter is only meaningful on data the model did not see. From here on, "the model's RMSE" means test (or cross-validated) RMSE, always.

9. In practice: from scratch, then scikit-learn

You have now seen the whole algorithm in NumPy. In real work you write it once, for understanding, and then use scikit-learn, which does the same thing with better numerics, feature scaling in a Pipeline, and every metric one import away. Here are both versions on load_diabetes, with the baseline every model must beat.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, SGDRegressor
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error, mean_absolute_error, r2_score

X, y = load_diabetes(return_X_y=True)             # 442 patients, 10 features
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=0)

# --- from scratch: multi-feature batch gradient descent on standardised inputs ---
mu, sd = X_tr.mean(axis=0), X_tr.std(axis=0)      # statistics from TRAINING rows only
Z_tr, Z_te = (X_tr - mu) / sd, (X_te - mu) / sd
w, b = np.zeros(X.shape[1]), 0.0
for epoch in range(10_000):                       # cheap: 331 rows × 10 features per epoch
    err = Z_tr @ w + b - y_tr                        # ŷ − y for every row, as before
    w -= 0.05 * 2 * Z_tr.T @ err / len(y_tr)          # ∂L/∂w for all 10 weights at once
    b -= 0.05 * 2 * err.mean()
pred_gd = Z_te @ w + b

def report(name, p):
    print(f"{name:14s} RMSE = {np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(y_te, p)):6.2f}  "
          f"MAE = {mean_absolute_error(y_te, p):6.2f}  R² = {r2_score(y_te, p):.3f}")

report("from scratch", pred_gd)
ols = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LinearRegression())
sgd = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SGDRegressor(max_iter=1000, tol=1e-4, random_state=0))
report("closed-form", ols.fit(X_tr, y_tr).predict(X_te))
report("SGDRegressor", sgd.fit(X_tr, y_tr).predict(X_te))
report("mean baseline", np.full_like(y_te, y_tr.mean()))      # the model to beat
# from scratch   RMSE =  56.39  MAE =  45.12  R² = 0.359
# closed-form    RMSE =  56.39  MAE =  45.12  R² = 0.359   <- identical: same optimum
# SGDRegressor   RMSE =  56.77  MAE =  45.41  R² = 0.351   <- stochastic, ≈ the same
# mean baseline  RMSE =  70.46  MAE =  58.31  R² = -0.000  <- R² ≈ 0 by definition

Three lessons from those four lines. The from-scratch code and LinearRegression agree to every printed digit, which is the best possible check that you understood the algorithm. SGDRegressor lands close but not identical: it is the stochastic variant with its own tolerance and learning-rate schedule, and it is the one you reach for when n is millions. And the baseline sets the bar: R² = 0.36 sounds weak until you see that predicting the mean gives RMSE 70; the model removes a fifth of the error, which in a medical setting is real information.

Regularised variants: Ridge and Lasso (preview of Chapter 7)

Two siblings of LinearRegression deserve a first mention now. Ridge adds α·∑wj² to the loss, which shrinks coefficients toward zero, tames correlated features (the s1/s2 tug-of-war above) and never fails on a singular XTX. Lasso adds α·∑|wj|, which shrinks some coefficients to exactly zero and thereby selects features. Both are one-word swaps in the Pipeline (Ridge(alpha=1.0), Lasso(alpha=0.1)) and both need scaled inputs. Chapter 7 explains why shrinking coefficients reduces overfitting and how to choose α.

Which one do I use at work?

At work

Linear regression is the right final model more often than the leaderboard culture admits: when stakeholders must understand and defend the numbers (pricing, credit, compensation), when a regulator will ask "why", when you have a few hundred rows and a tree ensemble would just memorise them, and when the gain from a complex model is a few percent of RMSE that nobody will act on. A well-scaled, well-diagnosed linear model with honest test metrics beats an unexplained black box in most business decisions.

At work: the baseline habit

Every regression project you run should produce, on day one, a table with three rows: predict the mean, linear regression, and the fancy model, all with test RMSE and MAE. If row three does not beat row two by a margin that changes a decision, ship row two. This table is also the first slide of every model review you will ever give.

Key idea

Everything after this chapter is a variation on one loop. Define a model with parameters, define a loss that says how wrong it is, compute the gradient, step against it, repeat, and judge the result on data you did not train on. Logistic regression changes the loss; neural networks change the model; boosting changes what a step means. The loop never changes.

Quiz

Check your understanding

1. A model predicts flat prices with ŷ = 2.5·size + 50 (k€, m²). What does the 2.5 mean?

w is the slope: change in prediction per one-unit change in the feature. 50 k€ is the intercept, the prediction at size 0.

2. Why does MSE square the residuals rather than just averaging them?

Without squaring (or an absolute value), errors of +100 and −100 average to zero. Squaring also gives a smooth, convex loss with a closed form, at the cost of amplifying outliers.

3. Your training loss printed 4.1, 3.0, 2.2, 1.9, 1.8, 1.8, 1.8. What is happening?

Falling fast, then slowly, then flat is textbook convergence. Too large an α would show rising or oscillating loss; overfitting is invisible in training loss alone.

4. Gradient descent zigzags and converges very slowly on your data, but the closed form solves it instantly. The most likely cause is…

Elongated contours force a small learning rate along the steep axis, which crawls along the shallow one (Figure 5.6). Standardise the features. The closed form is unaffected by scaling.

5. In a multiple regression of salary on years-of-experience and age, the coefficient on age is negative. The correct reading is…

Every coefficient is "holding the other features constant". Age and experience are strongly correlated, so the age coefficient captures only what age adds beyond experience, and it is unstable (Ridge helps).

6. A demand model has RMSE 120 units and MAE 40 units on the test set. What does the gap tell you?

MAE ≤ RMSE always, and for well-behaved errors they are close (RMSE ≈ 1.25 × MAE for Gaussian errors). A ratio of 3 means a handful of huge misses; look at them before choosing which metric to optimise.

Exercise 5.1 — Derive and verify the gradient

Using the from-scratch code of Section 4, check the derivative numerically: for the current (w, b), compute the loss at w + 0.001 and at w − 0.001, and compare (L+ − L−) / 0.002 to your analytic dw. Do the same for b. They should agree to about six decimal places. This "gradient check" is how deep-learning frameworks are tested, and it is how you will debug your own gradient code in Chapter 15.

Show answer 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
size = rng.uniform(40, 160, 50); y = 50 + 2.5 * size + rng.normal(0, 25, 50)
x = (size - size.mean()) / size.std()
L = lambda w, b: np.mean((w * x + b - y) ** 2)
w, b, h = 1.0, 2.0, 1e-3
dw_analytic = 2 * np.mean((w * x + b - y) * x)
dw_numeric  = (L(w + h, b) - L(w - h, b)) / (2 * h)
print(round(dw_analytic, 6), round(dw_numeric, 6), np.isclose(dw_analytic, dw_numeric))
# -180.499139 -180.499139 True

The two numbers agree because MSE is a quadratic, so the central difference is exact up to floating-point rounding. If they disagreed, the analytic formula (not the numeric one) would be wrong.

Exercise 5.2 — Break the assumptions on purpose

Generate y = 50 + 2.5·size + noise where the noise standard deviation is 0.2·size (bigger flats, bigger errors). Fit LinearRegression, make the residuals-versus-fitted plot, and describe the shape. Then fit the same model to log(y) instead of y and re-plot. What changed, and what does the coefficient on size now mean?

Show answer

The first residual plot is a funnel: spread grows with the prediction (heteroscedasticity). Modelling log(y) compresses large values, and the funnel largely flattens into a band. The coefficient on size now reads as a multiplicative effect: each extra m² multiplies the predicted price by ew, i.e. roughly +100·w percent. This "log the target" move is the single most common fix for money, counts and durations, and it also makes RMSE on the log scale behave like a relative error.

Chapter summary

  • The model is ŷ = w·x + b: one weight per feature plus a bias; "linear" means linear in the weights, so transformed features are allowed.
  • Residual = y − ŷ. MSE averages squared residuals: it cannot cancel, it is smooth, it punishes big errors, and it is outlier-sensitive.
  • The normal equation θ* = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy solves MSE exactly; gradient descent replaces it when p is huge, data streams, or the loss has no closed form.
  • Gradient descent: compute ∂L/∂w = −(2/n)∑(y−ŷ)x and ∂L/∂b = −(2/n)∑(y−ŷ), then w ← w − α·∂L/∂w. The loss is a convex bowl, so it always reaches the global minimum if α is sane.
  • α too small crawls, too large diverges (loss → NaN); plot loss per epoch to tell them apart. Batch, stochastic and mini-batch differ only in how many rows estimate the gradient.
  • Standardise features before any gradient-based or distance-based model; it turns the elongated bowl round.
  • Coefficients mean "target units per feature unit, holding the others fixed"; standardise to compare them; one-hot categories read as premiums over a reference level; never read them causally.
  • Polynomial features fit curves and overfit fast; residual plots reveal non-linearity (curve) and unequal variance (funnel).
  • Report RMSE (outlier-sensitive), MAE (typical error) and R² (fraction of variance explained), always on held-out data, always next to the mean baseline.
  • Linear regression is the day-one baseline and, for interpretable, small-data or regulated problems, often the final model. Ridge and Lasso are its regularised siblings (Chapter 7).
